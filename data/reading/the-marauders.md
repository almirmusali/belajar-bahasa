# THE MARAUDERS

### The story of four friends — easy English

---

## Читателю

Вы знаете, чем кончилась эта история. Хэллоуин 1981 года, разрушенный дом в Годриковой Впадине, мальчик со шрамом. Об этом написаны семь книг.

Но вы не знаете, как она началась. Роулинг так и не написала историю Мародёров — четырёх мальчишек, которые встретились в поезде в 1971 году и поклялись, что их дружбу не разрушит ничто. Джеймс. Сириус. Римус. Питер. Один из них — предатель, и вы с первой страницы знаете его имя. В этом и есть жестокость этой книги: вы будете смотреть, как он смеётся с друзьями, ест их сладости, плачет на их свадьбах, — и искать момент, где всё сломалось. Это детектив наоборот: не «кто убил», а «как хороший мальчик дошёл до этого». Рассказывает Римус Люпин — единственный, кто остался и узнал правду.

**Как читать эту книгу.** Она построена по принципу метода Ильи Франка: художественный текст, в котором перевод всегда на расстоянии одного касания, — только вместо вставок в скобках здесь живые подсказки читалки.

**Шаг 1.** Читай абзац целиком, не останавливаясь. Незнакомые слова пропускай — смысл соберётся из контекста. Это главное правило метода: мозг учит язык на потоке текста, а не на зубрёжке.

**Шаг 2.** Только если без слова никак — наведи на него (на телефоне тапни). Появится перевод и словарная форма: *ran → бежали, от run*. Переведено каждое слово книги, все 2700 форм.

**Шаг 3.** Дочитал абзац — нажми кнопку перевода рядом с ним и сверься. Понял правильно? Перечитай абзац ещё раз по-английски, уже зная смысл. Это второе правило Франка: одно и то же место дважды — сначала с усилием, потом свободно.

**Шаг 4.** Не выписывай слова столбиком. Ключевые слова книги — *secret, trust, betray, moon, brave* — повторяются десятки раз намеренно: к середине книги они станут твоими без всякой зубрёжки. Врезка «New Words» в конце главы покажет, что глава тебе подарила. Самое ценное отмечай звёздочкой — из «Избранного» слова учатся карточками в разделе «Словарь».

**Шаг 5.** Включи озвучку абзаца и слушай, следя глазами. Читай каждый день по одной-две главы — закладка запомнит место, а полоска прогресса покажет, сколько позади.

И последнее. Книга растёт вместе с тобой. Часть первая написана самым простым английским — короткие фразы, самые частые слова. К третьей части предложения становятся длиннее и глубже: это сделано нарочно, ты поднимаешься по ступеням незаметно для себя. Если долистаешь до «Самой длинной ночи» — ты уже читаешь настоящую английскую прозу. Без словаря. Проверь сам.

Открывай первую главу. Поезд уже отходит.

## People in this Story

**Remus Lupin** — the one who tells this story. Quiet, kind, always tired. He has a secret.

**James Potter** — rich, funny, brave. The best flyer in school. Harry's future father.

**Sirius Black** — beautiful, loud, angry. His family is dark, but he is not.

**Peter Pettigrew** — small, soft, always near. He laughs at every joke. Remember him.

**Lily Evans** — clever and warm. Green eyes. James loves her from year one. She does not love James.

**Severus Snape** — a thin boy in old clothes. Lily's friend. He hates James.

**Albus Dumbledore** — the head of Hogwarts school. The only wizard Voldemort is afraid of.

**Voldemort** — the dark wizard. His war is coming.

## Part One: The Castle

I was eleven years old, and I thought my life was over.

That is a strange thing to say about a child. But it is true. I had a secret so heavy that no school wanted me, no friend could know me, no future was open to me.

Then a letter came. And after the letter, a train. And on that train — three boys.

This part of the story is about them. It is the happy part. Read it slowly, because happiness in this book does not stay long.

---

## 1. The Train

The first of September, 1971. King's Cross station, London. Rain.

I stood on the platform with my father. Around us, hundreds of children said goodbye to their parents. Owls cried in cages. Cats ran between legs. A big red train made white smoke.

My father held my shoulder too hard.

"Remus," he said. "Remember the rules. Tell nobody. Not one person. If they learn what you are —"

"They will send me home. I know, Dad."

"Dumbledore is doing a dangerous thing, taking you. Do not make him sorry."

I nodded. I was very good at nodding. When you have a secret, you learn to nod and say nothing.

I found an empty room in the train and sat by the window. I hoped nobody would come in. Eleven years old, and my biggest hope was to be alone.

The door opened.

Two boys fell into the room — really fell, laughing, one on top of the other. The first boy had black hair that stood up in all directions and round glasses. The second boy had long dark hair and a face from an old picture — the face of a rich, old family.

"Sorry," said the boy with glasses, not sorry at all. "Everywhere else is full. I'm James. James Potter."

"Sirius Black," said the beautiful one.

"Black?" James looked at him with big eyes. "The Blacks? The dark family? The ones with the —"

"Yes, that family," said Sirius. His voice became flat, like a door closing. "And I'm nothing like them, so shut up about it."

For a second the room was cold. Then James smiled.

"Fine. I hate them too. Want a chocolate frog?"

And that was that. That is how the best friendship I ever saw was born — in one second, over a chocolate frog, between a boy who had everything and a boy who wanted to burn his own house down.

They turned to me.

"Who are you?" said James.

"Remus," I said. "Remus Lupin."

"You look sick, Remus," said Sirius. It was true. The full moon had been four days ago. I still had grey skin and a cut on my neck. My father's words were loud in my head: *tell nobody, not one person.*

"I was ill," I said. "I'm better now."

James opened his mouth to ask more. And then the door opened again, and a small round boy stood there, red in the face, out of breath.

"Can — can I sit here?" he said. "Some big boys took my sweets and my room and —"

"They took your sweets?" James stood up. "Which boys? Come on, Sirius. This is not right."

They came back ten minutes later with the sweets, with red faces, laughing so hard they could not talk. The small boy looked at them like they were kings.

"That was amazing," he said. "You are amazing. I'm Peter. Peter Pettigrew."

Remember this picture. I always will. Four boys in a small train room: James giving Peter his sweets back like a king giving gold. Sirius with his feet on the seat, laughing. Peter looking at them with hungry, thankful eyes. And me in the corner — quiet, sick, sure that I could never be their friend, because friends do not have secrets like mine.

Outside, England ran past the window. Green fields, small houses, rain.

Somewhere far away, in places we could not see, a dark wizard was getting stronger. His name was Voldemort. In ten years he would take everything from every boy in that room.

But we did not know. We were eleven. We ate chocolate frogs and laughed all the way to Scotland.

## 2. The Hat

You come to Hogwarts by water. Nobody tells you that before.

The train stopped in the dark. A giant man with a lamp — Hagrid, the nicest giant in the world — called all the small children to the boats. And then, across black water, we saw it.

The castle.

A hundred towers, a thousand yellow windows, standing on a mountain over a black lake. Peter made a small sound, like a mouse. James said a bad word. Sirius said nothing at all, and I understood later why: for him it was not just a castle. It was the door out of his terrible house.

For me it was something else. It was the school that was not supposed to take me. Dumbledore had said yes when every rule said no. I looked at the yellow windows and thought: *please. Please let me stay. I will be so careful.*

Inside, in a hall with a sky instead of a ceiling, they put an old hat on your head, and the hat looks inside you and calls the name of your house. Four houses: Gryffindor for the brave, Ravenclaw for the clever, Hufflepuff for the true, and Slytherin — for the ones who want power.

"Black, Sirius!"

The hall became quiet. Everybody knew the Blacks. Every Black, for three hundred years, went to Slytherin. His whole family — his cold mother, his dark cousins — they were all Slytherin. At the Slytherin table, people were already smiling, moving to make a place for him.

The hat sat on his head for a long, long time.

I watched his face. He was afraid. The loud, beautiful boy from the train was afraid. His hands held the chair so hard they were white.

Then the hat opened its mouth and shouted: "GRYFFINDOR!"

Silence. Total silence in the whole hall. One boy at the Slytherin table stood up, angry. Sirius took off the hat, and his face — I will never forget his face. It was the face of a man walking out of prison.

He walked to the Gryffindor table alone, and nobody clapped, so he clapped for himself.

"Evans, Lily!"

A girl with dark red hair almost ran to the chair. The hat barely touched her head: "GRYFFINDOR!" She smiled and sat down across from Sirius. I saw her look back at a thin boy in the line — a boy with black oily hair and old clothes. He looked like somebody had taken his sweets, his room, and his whole world.

"Lupin, Remus!"

Walking to that chair was the longest walk of my life. The hat fell over my eyes.

*"Ah,"* said a small voice in my ear. *"Difficult. Very difficult. So much fear. You think you are a monster, boy?"*

*Please,* I thought. *Please just let me stay.*

*"A monster would not ask so politely,"* said the hat. And then, out loud: "GRYFFINDOR!"

I sat down next to Lily Evans on legs made of water.

"Pettigrew, Peter!"

The hat thought about Peter almost as long as it thought about Sirius. Years later, I ask myself all the time: what did it see? What did it see in that small, soft head, and why did it still say "GRYFFINDOR"?

Maybe the hat sees not what you are. Maybe it sees what you want. And Peter — Peter always wanted to be near the brave. That is not the same thing as being brave. We learned that too late.

"Potter, James!"

"GRYFFINDOR!" — the hat did not even sit down properly. Of course. James ran to our table like it was his birthday, shook everybody's hands, and sat down between me and Sirius like the place was kept for him from the beginning of the world.

"Snape, Severus!"

The thin boy with oily hair went to Slytherin. Lily's face fell. He looked at her from the Slytherin table, and she looked at him, and there was a bridge of sadness between the two tables.

That night I lay in a warm bed, in a round tower, and could not sleep. Three boys were sleeping around me.

*They can never know,* I told the ceiling. *I will be careful. I will be quiet. I will be nobody's friend, and then I will be safe.*

That was my whole plan. It lasted about six weeks.

## 3. Four Beds

Let me tell you about our room, because for seven years it was the best place in the world.

It was round, at the top of Gryffindor tower. Four beds with red curtains. A window that looked at the mountains. It was always a mess — James's clothes, Sirius's music papers, Peter's sweet papers, my books. In the winter, ice flowers grew on the window. You could hear the wind sing.

The first weeks, I kept my plan. I was quiet. I studied. I said "no, thank you" so many times that James started calling me "No-Thank-You Lupin."

But it is hard to say no to James Potter. Not because he pushed. Because he *noticed*.

He noticed that I never had money for sweets, and suddenly there were always "extra" sweets that "somebody had to eat, come on, Remus, help me." He noticed that I read at night, and one evening a small lamp appeared by my bed. Nobody said a word about it. That was James: loud about jokes and quiet about kindness. His son is the same, they tell me.

And Sirius — Sirius was a fire. You could not be cold next to him. He talked in class, sang bad songs after ten o'clock, and made war on the school rules like the rules had killed his dog. Our head of house, Professor McGonagall, said his name so often — "Black! *Black!*" — that first-year students thought it was a magic word.

Peter followed them everywhere, one step behind, like a small boat behind two big ships. He was not funny himself, but he was the best laugher in school, and every clown needs somebody who laughs.

And slowly, without my permission, they became my friends.

It happened through small doors. Somebody has to help James with his star maps — he cannot tell one star from another. Somebody has to keep Peter's notes, because Peter loses everything. Somebody has to sit with Sirius by the fire when a black-and-green letter comes from his mother and his face turns to stone. He never opened those letters. He burned them, and we sat and watched them burn together, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say.

By December, I had broken my whole plan. I was somebody's friend. I was four people's friend.

And so, of course, the problem came. The problem came every month, round and white, over the mountains.

The full moon.

Every month I disappeared for two or three days. The story was: my mother is ill, I go home to see her. Madam Pomfrey, the school nurse, took me down to the school gates in the evening. But I did not go home.

There was a new tree on the school grounds — a huge angry tree that hit anyone who came close. They planted it the year I came. Now you know why. Under that tree there was a secret tunnel, and at the end of the tunnel, an empty house in the village.

There, in that cold house, they locked me in.

I cannot tell you much about the nights themselves, because I do not remember them. That is the truth about my illness that books do not say: the wolf remembers, the boy does not. I woke up in the mornings on the wooden floor, with blood in my mouth — my own blood — and new cuts on my arms, and Madam Pomfrey's steps in the tunnel, and pain like the whole world was made of broken glass.

A werewolf alone attacks himself. There is nobody else to bite.

Then two days in the hospital wing. Then back to class, grey and slow, with a new story ready: *my mother, yes, she is better now, thank you.*

I thought it was working. I thought they believed me.

I forgot that I was living with the three most curious boys in Britain.

## 4. The Empty Bed

It was Sirius who started counting.

Second year, October. I came back from "visiting my mother," grey as old snow, and found the three of them sitting on James's bed with a piece of paper. They stopped talking when I came in. That silence — you know that silence, when you enter a room and the air is still warm from words about you.

"How is your mother?" asked Sirius. His voice was too light.

"Better, thanks."

"That's good," said Sirius. "That's the fourth time she was ill this year. Poor woman. And always so exactly ill. September, October — every month, Remus. Every single month."

My hands went cold.

"That's not — sometimes it's my grandmother —"

"And the funny thing," Sirius went on, in that same light voice, and his eyes were not light at all, "the funny thing is the moon. James, tell him about the moon."

James looked unhappy. He held the paper like it burned him. I could see my own name on it, and dates, and small drawings of circles. Circles. Moons.

"It's just — Remus, we were worried, you always come back hurt, we thought maybe somebody at home was — hurting you," said James. "So we wrote down the days. And Peter said — Peter noticed —"

Peter. Little Peter with his round, soft face. He looked at me and then quickly looked at his hands. Even then, even in kindness, he could not look anyone in the eye.

"I noticed the moon was always full," Peter said to his hands. "My mum has a calendar with moons on it."

The room was very, very quiet. Down in the common room, somebody laughed at something. Far away, in another world, where nobody's life was ending.

You must understand what stood in front of me. Not three curious boys. My whole life. Werewolf children did not go to school. Werewolf adults did not get work. There were laws — actual laws written by the Ministry — about "the control of dangerous half-humans." Dangerous half-humans. That was me at twelve: a dangerous half-human with a lamp by his bed for reading at night.

If they told anyone, I was finished. Not "in trouble." Finished.

I did what I had planned in my head a hundred times for this exact moment. I lied.

I laughed — I remember how bad it sounded, like a cough — and said the moon was nothing, the dates were nothing, and I felt the lie die in the air in the middle of the room. Sirius looked at me. He was twelve, but that look was a hundred years old. It was a look from a family where everybody lies beautifully at dinner every night.

"Remus," he said quietly. "Stop. We know. We have known for a week. You are a werewolf."

And there it was. The word. Out. In the air. In my room, in front of my three — I could not even think the word "friends" anymore, because now it was over, and —

"We think," said James, "that it is completely brilliant."

I stood there with my mouth open.

"Brilliant?" I said. "It is — what?"

"Well, it's terrible," said James quickly. "The pain and everything, that part is terrible, obviously. But Remus — you turn into a *wolf* every month and then you come to class and get better marks than all of us? That is the hardest thing I have ever heard of anyone doing. Also," he added, and this is a real thing that James Potter said, "also it explains why you always know when the sausages at dinner are old. Wolf nose."

Peter laughed. Sirius laughed. And I stood in the middle of our round room and started to cry, and could not stop, and nobody made fun of me. Sirius sat me down on the bed. Peter pushed sweets into my hand — his own sweets, his last ones, and if you knew Peter, you know what that meant.

"You can't tell anyone," I said, when I could talk. "Please. Not anyone, ever. If the school parents learn —"

"Tell anyone?" Sirius looked truly insulted. "Remus. We are your friends. Your secret is our secret now."

*Your secret is our secret now.*

I have turned those words over in my hands for forty years. Sirius kept them all his life, through things you cannot imagine. James kept them to his last night. And Peter — Peter kept them too, kept them and kept them, until the one day when a secret was the only money he had left to buy his life with.

But that came later. That night, I just cried, and ate Peter's sweets, and was, for the first time since I was four years old, not alone.

## 5. The Promise

Now I must explain something about my three friends, because what they did next is not a normal thing.

A normal friend, learning your illness, is kind to you. He asks how you feel. He is a little careful with you, like you are made of thin glass, and slowly — not because he is bad, but because it is heavy — he stands a little further away.

My friends were not normal.

For them, my illness was not my problem. It was *our* problem. And James Potter did not believe that "our problems" existed. Only "our projects."

It began a few weeks later, in the library. James dropped a huge old book on the table in front of me, and dust flew up like smoke.

"Animagus," he said.

"Keep your voice down," I said. "And — what?"

"An-i-ma-gus." He said it slowly, like I was old and could not hear. "A wizard who can turn into an animal. When he wants. And back. McGonagall is one — she turns into a cat, you have seen it."

"I know what an Animagus is, James. Why is it on my homework?"

James leaned over the table. His glasses shone. Sirius sat down on my left. Peter sat down on my right. I was surrounded.

"Because werewolves," James said quietly, "are only dangerous to people. A wolf does not hurt animals. We read it. If we could be animals — real animals, big ones — we could be *with you*. On the nights. In that house. You would not be alone anymore."

I want you to feel the whole weight of this idea, so let me put it in plain words.

Becoming an Animagus is one of the hardest things in magic. Grown wizards fail at it and are hurt forever — there are stories about a witch who lived thirty years with a cat's tail. It takes years. And doing it in secret, without a teacher, is against the law — you must write your name in a Ministry book, and if you do not, and they catch you, that is prison.

Three thirteen-year-old boys decided to do it in secret, without a teacher, against the law. For me. So that I would not be alone at the full moon.

"No," I said. "No, no, and no. It is too dangerous. You will hurt yourselves. And if you are caught, it is prison, James, actual prison, and it will be because of me —"

"Nobody will catch us," said Sirius, and yawned.

"You cannot know that!"

"Remus." James took off his glasses, which he did about once a year, when he was serious. "One question. On those nights — does it hurt?"

Silence. Peter was watching me with big eyes. Somewhere behind us, the librarian was telling somebody off.

"Yes," I said. My voice came out very small. "It hurts."

"Then we are doing it," said James, and put his glasses back on, and the talk was over.

They started that week.

I said no for about a month more. Then I stopped, because — and this is a hard thing to say honestly — because some deep, hungry part of me did not want them to stop. All my life I had been a closed door, and here were three people trying to break the door down just to sit with me in the dark. You try saying no to that. You try saying no to that when you are thirteen.

So instead I helped. I was the best reader of us four, so I found the books. And what I read frightened me. The process took years. You had to change your whole body with your own will. One mistake — one moment of fear at the wrong second — and the books had pictures of what happened. I did not show those pictures to Peter. Peter was frightened enough.

That is a small detail, but write it down somewhere: from the very beginning, Peter was frightened. He wanted to stop in year three. He wanted to stop in year four. And every time, James put a hand on his shoulder and said, "You can do it, Pete. We will not go without you." And Peter stayed — not because he believed in himself, but because James believed in him.

Peter's whole life was built on borrowed courage. When the lender died, the debt came due.

But I run ahead again. Forgive me. Old men do that, and old men telling this story do it worst of all.

## 6. Three Animals

It took them three years.

Three years of stolen library nights. Three years of strange leaves under the tongue — do not ask, the process is disgusting — and secret words said at the right moon, and small failures that we laughed about, and big failures that we did not.

I remember Sirius walking around for a week with grey dog hair on the backs of his hands, wearing gloves in June, telling teachers he was cold. I remember James's eyes turning brown-gold for two days — he told everyone he had eaten a bad sweet. I remember Peter crying in the bathroom, third year, because his hands would not change at all, not even a little, while the other two were already halfway.

"Maybe I'm not strong enough," he said. "Maybe you should just go on without me."

"Don't be stupid," said Sirius — kindly, the way he said "stupid" only to Peter. "It's not strength. It's wanting. You have to want it with your whole body. What do you want most, Pete, in the whole world?"

I remember that Peter was quiet for a strange, long moment.

"To be with you three," he said finally. "Always."

Well. He got that wish, more or less. Be careful with wishes.

And then, one night in October — fifth year, we were fifteen — it happened.

They woke me after midnight. The common room was empty and dark red, just coals in the fire. They stood in a line, all three in their night clothes, and James said, with a huge and stupid smile:

"Watch."

And my three friends folded up like paper birds — down, and smaller, or bigger, and *changed* — and in the common room of Gryffindor tower there stood, where three boys had been:

A stag. A big grey dog. And a rat.

I sat down on the stairs. My legs simply stopped working.

The stag — James — was tall and impossible, with young antlers, and it walked to me on careful thin legs and looked at me through the same round glasses-shaped patches of light in its eyes, I swear. The dog — Sirius, of course Sirius, big and grey and loud even in silence — knocked me flat and licked my whole face, and it was completely Sirius: even as a dog, no respect for anybody's personal space.

And the rat sat small and still by the fire, nose moving fast, watching.

A rat. Of the three animals, Peter's was a rat.

Listen to me now, because this is important. In our world, they tell children that your animal shows your true self. So it is easy — so terribly easy — to say now: of course, a rat, we should have known, rats run, rats hide, rats leave a sinking ship.

But that night, the rat was the bravest thing in the room. That is the truth, and I will defend it. Little Peter Pettigrew, who was afraid of loud doors and deep water, had done one of the hardest things in magic — three years, no teacher, breaking the law every day — and he had done it last of the three, alone at the end, with nobody left to fail with. The rat sat by the fire, and its whiskers shook, and I picked Peter up in my two hands and told him he was a marvel.

He was, then.

Both things are true, you see. That is what nobody tells you about traitors. They are not born on the day they betray you. Before that day they are your friend — really, truly your friend — brave with borrowed courage, warm at your fire. If Peter had died at fifteen, he would have died a hero, and I would have cried at his grave for the bravest small boy I ever knew.

He did not die at fifteen.

That night we sat by the coals until morning, and they showed me again and again — boy, animal, boy — laughing into their hands so the tower would not wake. And near dawn, James gave everyone their new names, because James named everything; it was his way of loving the world.

The stag with the antlers: Prongs.

The black-grey dog with the soft feet: Padfoot.

The rat with the worm-like tail: Wormtail.

"And Remus is Moony," said James, "because — well, obviously."

Moony. Wormtail. Padfoot. Prongs. Four stupid names invented at four in the morning by four boys who loved each other. If you have ever held the Marauder's Map in your hands, you have read those names. Now you know: that map is signed by two dead men, a traitor, and me.

But that October night nobody was dead and nobody was a traitor, and the next full moon, for the first time in my life, I did not go into the dark alone.

## 7. Full Moon

How do I explain the nights to you?

Here is what a full moon was, before. Evening: goodbye, I am going to see my mother. The walk to the angry tree, Madam Pomfrey's hand on my back. The tunnel. The cold house with boards on the windows. Then waiting — the worst part, the waiting — watching the sky turn silver through the cracks, feeling the wolf come up through my bones like water coming up through sand.

Then nothing. Blackness.

Then morning: floor, blood, pain, the smell of dust, my own hands coming back to me finger by finger. Two days in bed. Repeat forever.

Now here is a full moon, after.

The wolf rises — and the world does not go black. The world goes *simple*. Because standing in the room there is a stag, and a huge dog, and the smell of them is not the smell of people — of food, the dark voice says, of prey — it is the smell of *pack*. The wolf knows them. I cannot tell you how the wolf knows, but it knows: these are mine.

And the door opens.

They planned it, my three friends, like a small war. Padfoot could hold the wolf back if the night went wrong; a big dog speaks a language a wolf understands. Prongs could push open the heavy tree-tunnel door with his antlers and carry half the forest on his back. And Wormtail — Wormtail was the key, in the most exact way. The angry tree that hit everyone: there was a small root at its base, and if you pressed it, the tree went still. A boy could never get close enough to press it. A rat could.

Remember that too. For years, Peter was the one who opened the way. Every single month, everything began with Peter slipping quietly through the dark and pressing a secret spot nobody else could reach. We built him into our lives as exactly that.

And so, at fifteen, I ran.

We ran the school grounds under the moon. We ran the forest, where the trees are a thousand years old and the paths glow. We ran to the village and up the hills behind it, the dog and the wolf shoulder to shoulder, the stag ahead like a king, the rat riding between the antlers like a small mad captain. I woke in the mornings tired and whole. No new cuts. No blood. Just grass on my clothes and, once, a completely wonderful memory of the four of us standing on a hilltop at three in the morning, looking down at the sleeping lights of the village, the wolf's head raised, and the long cry I sent into the sky — not pain, for once. Just a voice, saying: here I am. Alive.

Madam Pomfrey could not understand why I was suddenly so well. "The visits home are good for you," she said, and I nodded. I was still very good at nodding.

Was it dangerous? 

Yes. Say it plainly, Remus, you owe the truth to this page: yes. A werewolf ran the grounds of a school full of sleeping children, and three boys of fifteen believed they could control it, and they were lucky, month after month we were lucky, and luck is not a plan. Once — one time — the wolf caught a smell from the village and turned, and Padfoot stood in the way, and there are marks on Sirius that stayed his whole life. He called them his favourite marks. "Got them dancing," he told people, and winked at me across the room.

We were stupid. We were fifteen and immortal and in love with each other in the way of boys who would all die before they could say it — except stupid is not the whole truth either. The whole truth is that those nights held me together. Everything I am that is not broken, I built on this: once, four boys were told one of them was a monster, and instead of running, three of them learned to change their skin, so the fourth would never again wake up alone on a cold floor.

Nobody has done more for me in my whole life. And nobody has done worse to me. And they were the same three people. Hold that in your hands, reader, and do not let anyone tell you people are simple.

## 8. The Map

If you steal one thing from this story, let it be this: we knew that castle better than anyone has ever known it, before or after.

Seven years of night walks will do that. We knew the corridor that was only there on Thursdays. We knew the mirror with a passage behind it, and the one-eyed statue with the tunnel to the sweet shop in the village, and the room that becomes whatever you need — Peter found that one, hungry, looking for cake, and found a room full of cake, and came back to the tower crying with happiness.

We knew the kitchens. We knew the roofs. Sirius knew the roofs *especially*; he liked high places, our dog. He said in his parents' house all the windows looked down at other people, and he was done looking down at anybody.

By sixth year, the knowledge was too big for four heads. Facts fell out of us. Peter forgot which stairs disappeared on Fridays and spent an hour stuck in a wall. So James, who could not see a problem without building something too clever for it, said the words:

"We need a map."

It took a year, and it was, I say this calmly, the finest piece of magic our year produced — better than anything we did in class, better than most things the teachers could do. Parchment that shows the whole castle, every room, every passage, every person — real time, little moving feet with names on them. Nobody hides from it. Not teachers. Not ghosts. Not headmasters.

Each of us gave it something. James gave the charms that made it see — his magic was the engine of everything we built. I gave the order of it, the way the castle folded onto the page, and the insult it shouts at strangers who try to open it — I was the politest of us, which is why my insults were the best. Sirius gave it its personality, its cheek, the voice that talks back to anyone who grabs it. And Peter gave the small patient hours, the drawing and re-drawing of eight hundred doors, the checking of every stair — the work with no glory in it, which he did without complaining, evening after evening, because we were doing it together and together was the only place he was happy.

And on the front, in green ink, the words I can still see with my eyes closed:

*Messrs Moony, Wormtail, Padfoot and Prongs, Purveyors of Aids to Magical Mischief-Makers, are proud to present THE MARAUDER'S MAP.*

The Marauders. That is where the name comes from — we called ourselves that as a joke, four gentleman thieves of the night castle. Nobody outside the room ever called us it. It was a private country with four citizens.

I must tell you honestly what we used the map for: nothing important. Sneaking food. Avoiding teachers. Finding out which girl was walking where — that was mostly James, tracking one name, *Lily Evans*, moving through the halls like a small green star. We made the most powerful watching-tool in the school's history so that boys could avoid the caretaker and carry cakes.

But a tool does not stay with its makers. Years later, our map — Filch took it from us in seventh year, and it found its way on — years later it lay in the hands of James's son. And the little moving names it showed him, my God, the things that map watched happen. It watched a dead man's name walk the corridors at night, a name that could not, could not possibly be there, and nobody understood what it was trying to say.

The map never lies. We built it that way, four honest boys, sixteen years old.

It never once occurred to us that one of our own names would some day be the lie.

## 9. The Worst Night

I have been putting this chapter off. You can probably feel it. But this book is about how trust breaks, and the first crack came long before the war. It came from Sirius, in our sixth year, and it nearly came through me.

To tell it, I need Severus Snape.

You met him on the platform — the thin boy in old clothes, Lily's friend. Understand: the hate between him and James was not school silliness. It was real, it was ugly, and — being honest at last, which is the point of this book — it was not equal. James and Sirius were golden boys, loved by everyone; Snape was poor, strange, alone, and they were cruel to him. Yes, he gave back everything he could. Yes, he ran with a crowd of future Death Eaters who called people like Lily "mudblood" — there it is, the filthiest word we have; it means *dirty blood*, blood from non-magic parents. But two against one is two against one, and half the school laughing is half the school laughing. I was a prefect. I sat nearby with a book and said nothing. My particular way of being a coward has always been very quiet.

Snape hated them. But me — me he *studied*. Every month I vanished; every month my friends lied for me a little too smoothly. He started following. Watching the tree. Counting, the way Sirius had once counted — but Sirius had counted out of love, and Snape counted the way you sharpen a knife.

And one evening near the full moon, he pushed Sirius too far. Sneering, in the corridor: where does Lupin go, Black? What are you all hiding? And Sirius — 

I need you to understand what Sirius was that year, and honesty will cost me something here too. Sixteen, beautiful, furious. Things were bad at home in the way that ends with a boy leaving forever — that story is the next chapter. He carried a black anger looking for a door. And Snape, sneering in a corridor, was a door.

"You want to know where he goes?" said Sirius. "Press the root at the base of the tree with a long stick. Go and see."

That is all it takes. One sentence. One tired, angry, show-off sentence, and a boy walks into a tunnel at the end of which there is no Remus Lupin, there is a grown werewolf, and the boy is dead — or worse, bitten, turned, condemned to my life — and the werewolf who did it is put down like an animal, because that is the law, and the school closes, and every door Dumbledore ever opened for people like me slams shut for a hundred years.

Snape went to the tree that night. He pressed the root. He went in.

And James — James, who heard what Sirius had done and did not stop even to shout at him — went in after, down the black tunnel, sixteen years old, no plan, no help coming, and pulled Snape back from the end of it. From the sight of me. From the reach of me. They both saw the wolf. The wolf nearly had them both.

I woke up on the floor as usual. Pain as usual, blood as usual. And Dumbledore sitting on the broken stairs, waiting, and his face told me before any words did.

What I felt — no. I will not dress it up in fine sentences. I had been, for one night, without my knowledge or consent, a loaded gun in a schoolboy's quarrel. Everything I feared about myself had nearly come true, not through my carelessness, but through my best friend's mouth. You can be careful your whole life, and someone who loves you can still spend you like a coin.

I did not speak to Sirius for weeks. He apologised — Sirius, who apologised to nobody, apologised over and over, white-faced, and I believe to the end of my days that he meant it. He had not wanted Snape dead, or me a murderer. He simply had not *thought*. He was a boy from a cruel house playing a cruel trick, and it did not occur to him — this is the terrible, believable truth — it did not occur to him that I was in the trick at all. That the wolf was me. That his joke had my body in it.

Here is what stayed, though, like a splinter too deep to pull.

Dumbledore made it secret. Snape was ordered silent; James was called a hero — how he hated that, the one truly right thing he ever did and he could not stand hearing about it, because the mess was ours. And we four went on. Friends. Truly friends; do not think the love was false. But I had learned something I could never unlearn, and it waited in me, patient, for eleven years:

*One of us is capable of it.* Capable, in one careless furious moment, of putting another of us into the fire.

So years later, in the war, when the whispers began — *there is a traitor among your friends, someone close is passing secrets* — and every one of us lay awake at night privately weighing the other three, I did my weighing, God forgive me, and the scales in my head barely moved. There was already a name on them.

Not Peter. Nobody ever weighed Peter.

Sirius had put his own name there, at sixteen, with one sentence in a corridor. And in the end that old splinter helped kill us all.

## 10. Lily's Eyes

Enough darkness for a moment. Let me tell you about Lily Evans, because without her there is no story — no marriage, no Harry, no reason for that last house on that last street. And because remembering her is simply good for me. Some people are lamps. Thirty years out, the light still works.

Lily was Muggle-born — her parents had no magic at all. She was the best in our year at Charms, maybe the best at everything; she laughed loudly and argued louder; she collected strays. Shy first-years, homesick students, odd boys with no friends — everyone got pulled in and fed and defended. I was one of her strays before I was anyone's. In second year, when I was the quiet sick boy in the corner, Lily Evans sat down next to me and shared her sweets and talked to me like I was ordinary. If you have never been eleven and monstrous, you cannot know what a girl asking your opinion about homework can do.

And James loved her. From — I want to say from the train, honestly. From before he knew anything about her except the colour of her hair.

Here is the part the pretty version of this story leaves out: for years, she could not stand him.

And she was right. Say it plainly, Remus. At fifteen James Potter was the kind of boy who hexed people in corridors because he was bored and called it funny, and everybody clapped because he was handsome and brilliant at Quidditch — our flying game, played on brooms; James on a broom was the best thing our school ever put in the air. He showed off for Lily the way he showed off for everyone, only louder. And Lily looked straight through the shine at the cruelty under it, mostly the cruelty to Snape, her oldest friend, and told James — in front of everyone, more than once — exactly what she saw.

"You're a bully, Potter. An arrogant, strutting bully. I would not go out with you if it was a choice between you and the giant squid."

The whole courtyard heard that one. Sirius laughed for a week. James laughed too, in public. In the dormitory that night he lay on his bed with his glasses off, throwing a small ball at the ceiling, catching it, throwing it, for about two hours. Peter fell asleep. Sirius fell asleep. Finally I said, into the dark:

"She's not wrong, you know."

The ball stopped. A long silence. Then James's voice, quiet, no audience in it at all:

"I know."

That is the sentence people miss about James Potter. He knew. And — this is rarer than any magic on the map — he set about becoming somebody else. Not overnight; there is no such spell. But fifth year into sixth, the public hexings stopped. The strutting quieted. He put his gift for noticing people — always his best magic, better than anything with a wand — to work on people who were not himself. He led, instead of performing. The younger students started coming to him with problems the way they came to Lily.

And the terrible thing, the thing that ended a friendship older than ours: Snape moved exactly the other way. The same years that pulled James toward the light pulled Snape down, deeper into his Death Eater crowd, deeper into the Dark Arts, until the day — a bad public day, James in the middle of it, I have no wish to retell it — when Snape, hanging upside down with the whole courtyard laughing, needing somebody to bleed for his shame, called Lily — his Lily, his first friend, the girl from his own street — *mudblood*. In front of everyone.

You do not get that word back. She forgave him everything for years, the crowd he ran with, the curses he collected, and this one word ended it — because it was not an insult from an enemy; it was where his road was going, said out loud, and she heard the whole road in it.

Lily and James happened slowly, seventh year, once the boy who shouted had finished becoming the man who listened. I watched her watch him that autumn — puzzled at first, then interested against her will, then simply happy in his direction. He had stopped asking her out, which is of course exactly when it became possible. In the end — she asked him. He said, and this is a direct quotation, preserved seventeen years in amber: "…what?"

The best flyer in school. The map-maker, the stag, the future first of the fallen. *What.*

They were together from that day until the last day. I mean that with a plainness that frightens me still: from that day, until the last day, there was no other day for either of them.

## 11. The House of Black

The summer after sixth year, at two in the morning, somebody knocked on James Potter's front door.

James's parents opened it — his parents were old and rich and endlessly kind, the sort of old money that had gone soft and warm like a favourite chair — and on the step, in the rain, stood Sirius Black, sixteen, with one bag, a bleeding lip, and a face like a closed door.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't know where else —"

They had him at the table with soup inside ten minutes. He never lived anywhere else again until the war. The Potters put his school things on the family account, gave him the room next to James, and — the detail that undoes me every time I remember it — his own set of house keys, Muggle-style metal keys, because Mr Potter thought a boy should hear a door open to his own hand.

What happened in the house he left, that final night, I never fully learned. He did not tell it, and we did not dig. But I can tell you the shape of the years before it, the shape we watched from the outside, every September, every Christmas.

The Noble and Most Ancient House of Black. Say it with the capital letters; they did. A family that had been magic for so long they believed magic *was* them — that blood came in grades, and theirs was the finest, and Muggle-borns like Lily were dirt wearing a wand. Their family motto — I will not write it in French — meant *Always Pure.* Their house in London was a dark, locked thing full of serpent door-handles and the heads of dead servants on the walls. I am not inventing that. Ask anyone who ever went in.

To that family, Voldemort was not a horror rising. He was — at last — somebody with the courage of their convictions.

And their eldest son was Sirius. Loud, laughing, Gryffindor Sirius, who papered his bedroom walls with pictures of Muggle girls and Muggle motorbikes specifically because the pictures could not be removed by magic and his mother hated them. Every summer he went back to that house, and every September he came back to us a little harder around the eyes, with new silences in odd places. His mother's voice — we heard it once, on the platform, third year — was the sound a whip makes when it has learned words. His little brother Regulus, softer, sadder, wanting to be loved in that house rather than out of it, drifted year by year the other way — toward the family, toward the motto, and eventually, at eighteen, under the sleeve of it. The Death Eaters got the spare.

The night Sirius ran, the House of Black burned his name off the family tree. There was an actual tree, painted on an actual wall, and his mother put her wand to it and burned a hole where her first son had been. He laughed about it. He laughed about all of it, our dog; laughter was his whole armour. "Best thing my mother ever did for me," he said. "Very freeing. I recommend it."

But I want to put one more picture beside that laugh, because this book is a set of scales too, and Sirius gets weighed honestly like everyone else.

Christmas, seventh year, at the Potters'. All four of us — Peter was there too, remember Peter is always there — and Mrs Potter did what mothers do: presents for everyone, and for Sirius the same number as for James, wrapped the same, signed *with love*. I watched Sirius Black open a soft parcel — a hand-knitted jumper, dark red — and go absolutely still, the way animals go still when they do not want a moment to end. Sixteen years in the noble and most ancient house of his blood, and I think that jumper was the first thing anyone had ever made him with their hands.

He wore it until it fell apart. Years. I want that on the page, before everything else that comes: before the prison, before the horrors and the vengeance, the truest thing I know about Sirius Black. However loud he laughed, he was, underneath and always, a boy at a table with soup, listening to rain on somebody else's roof, learning at sixteen what a house is actually for.

He guarded the people who taught him that like a dog guards a gate. Hold that fact. In two chapters, men will tell you he sold them. Men told *me* that, and to my shame I believed it for twelve years.

## 12. Seventh Year

Our last year at Hogwarts, somebody pinned a newspaper page to the notice board in the common room. The headline said: TWO MORE FAMILIES MISSING. MINISTRY URGES CALM.

Under it, some seventh-year had written in ink: *They urge calm because they have nothing else.*

That was seventh year. The castle stayed warm; the world outside went dark at the edges like burning paper. Voldemort was no longer a whisper. There were attacks now, disappearances now, dark marks burned green into the sky over broken houses — his sign, so that everyone would know his work and be afraid. And the war had a sound inside the school too, low but constant: which families had lost someone. Which students' fathers were "being questioned." Which Slytherins had started wearing long sleeves in summer heat — you cover a forearm for one reason; the Dark Mark went on the forearm, and we all knew it, and nobody could prove anything.

Snape had stopped even pretending, by then. He moved through the halls with boys who would be convicted murderers within five years, and the message of the group of them was plain: *soon.*

And in the middle of the darkening, two absurd things happened. Dumbledore made James Head Boy — James, whose file of school crimes physically could not close — and the school did not fall down. It turned out all those years of captaining a Quidditch team and captaining us had built exactly the man for a frightened castle: he knew every first-year's name by October, walked the anxious Muggle-born students to their common rooms after dark, and did it all so lightly that being protected felt like being included in a joke.

The second absurd thing I have already told you. Lily.

They were together that whole last year, and I will keep the memory of it plain and short, because it needs no dressing: the two best people I knew, finding each other at the exact moment the lights were going out. Watching them was like watching somebody light a fire in a window while a storm comes up the valley. You are glad of the fire. You watch the storm.

We talked about the war constantly, that year — around the fire at night, the four of us plus Lily now, which none of us minded; she had somehow always been the fifth Marauder without a name.

James was going to fight. That was never a question in his body; you might as well ask whether water was going to be wet. His parents' money meant he never needed work, so he would give the war his whole self. Sirius would go where James went — also not a question, also never had been, since a chocolate frog on a train in 1971. I said I would fight too, and meant it, and privately wondered what an army does with a soldier who is a monster two nights a month.

And Peter — 

I have gone back and forth about how to write this, and I will simply write what happened. One night by the fire, the war talk got real: names of the missing, guesses about traitors, what the Death Eaters did to the families of people who fought. And Peter, staring at the coals, said, quietly:

"We don't have to decide anything, though. Do we? I mean — maybe it'll be over before it needs us."

Sirius, not unkindly: "And if it isn't?"

Peter didn't answer for a while. The fire cracked. Then he said, "You three are good at things. I'm only brave next to you," and laughed his small laugh to make it a joke, and reached for another sweet.

We treated it as a joke. It was the truest sentence anyone said in seven years, and we filed it under jokes. *I'm only brave next to you.* He was telling us. People do tell you, I have learned; almost always, at some point, quietly, in plain words, people tell you exactly what they are, and you nod and pass the sweets because you love them and it is warm by the fire.

School ended the way it does, suddenly, all at once, with trunks in the corridors and signatures on each other's yearbooks and Hagrid crying into a tablecloth-sized handkerchief. On the last night, the four of us broke rules one final time — up onto the roof, Padfoot's roof, above the astronomy tower, and watched the sun come up over the lake with the giant squid making slow circles in the pink water.

"Whatever happens out there," said James — eighteen, glasses flashing in the sunrise, absolutely certain, the way only James could be certain — "we stay us. The four of us. Agreed? Nothing gets in."

"Agreed," said Sirius.

"Agreed," I said.

"Agreed," said Peter.

Four for four. Then we went down the stairs and out of the castle and into the war, and the war took us one, two, three, four — every single one, each in a different way. But I am the only one who knows now that on the roof that morning, when we swore it, all four of us meant it.

Even him. That is the hardest part to carry. Even him.

## Part Two: The War

Here the story changes, and I must warn you the way nobody warned us.

School stories have rules. The dangers are the size of children; the year ends in summer; the castle always stands. You have just read a school story, and even its worst nights had walls around them.

The war had no walls.

We were eighteen when we walked out of the castle, and by twenty-one, half the faces from our common room were in the ground or in hiding or behind a mask. I will try to tell it in order, but grief does not keep order. Somewhere in these chapters, a rat begins to drown; and I did not see it, because I was looking at the moon, at the funerals, at everything except the smallest of us, quietly going under.

Read on. It does not hurt less if you stop.

## 13. Dark Days

Nobody declares a war like this. There was no morning when the radio said *it has begun.* There was only a slow rise of water.

First it was news items with strange spaces in them. A family found dead, no marks on them — the Muggle police "unable to explain." A bridge falls in perfect weather. A whole village loses one night of memory. The Ministry of Magic said: isolated incidents. The Ministry said: no cause for alarm. The Ministry, as the boy wrote on our notice board, had nothing else.

The truth walked around the official words. Voldemort was building an army — giants and dark creatures and, above all, wizards; old pure-blood families who wanted the world he promised, and frightened people who wanted to be on the winning side, and cruel people who wanted permission. That is all a dark army has ever been, in any world, ours or yours: the convinced, the afraid, and the cruel, marching in one column.

His soldiers called themselves Death Eaters, wore masks, and marked the sky over their kills. And the quiet mathematics of it, the thing that made your stomach cold: nobody caught them. Witnesses forgot. Judges changed rulings. Prisoners escaped between the arrest and the cell. The rot was inside the walls — inside the Ministry, inside old families, inside shops on our own street. By 1979 you could feel it everywhere we went: the war was not in some far country. It was at the next table. It listened while you ordered coffee.

What do you do, at eighteen, nineteen, against a rising sea?

James and Lily married — I will give the wedding its own chapter; joy earns its own chapters now. James poured his family gold into the fight, quietly, the way his father would have: safe houses paid for, escapes paid for, families of the dead kept fed, and never a word about any of it — we learned the size of it only after, from the people he had saved.

Sirius bought the motorbike. Of course he did — a huge Muggle machine, and he charmed it to fly, which was illegal, dangerous, and completely unnecessary, three of his favourite qualities in one object. But understand what it actually was. His whole family had gone to the other side; cousins he had grown up with were behind those masks now, and his brother — little Regulus, eighteen — wore the Mark itself. Sirius against Voldemort was not politics. It was a boy burning his surname off his own arm the way his mother had burned him off the wall. Every ride on that roaring Muggle machine was a letter home.

I took work where a werewolf could — small jobs, short jobs; my condition has a way of ending employment — and said yes the moment the real fight found us. Peter took a Ministry desk job, junior, harmless, the kind of post nobody looks at twice. We were glad. Somebody sensible, we said, somebody with a steady wage. And — hear the sound of this now, though we could not hear it then — a desk in the Ministry, where the papers go past, where the corridors murmur, where a small man nobody looks at twice hears a very great deal.

The water kept rising. And then one evening in a back room, a tired-looking wizard we all knew — one of our old teachers — put cups of tea in front of us and told us that Dumbledore was quietly gathering people who intended to actually fight, and that we four had been asked for.

All the reckless courage in that room, and nobody's hand was steady on the teacup. That is what I remember. We had been playing at war in our heads since seventh year. Here was the door, actually open, actually asking.

James looked around at the three of us, that old look, the roof-at-sunrise look.

"Well," he said. "We did say agreed."

## 14. The Order of the Phoenix

The Order of the Phoenix met wherever it could — back rooms, kitchens, once a cave — and if you are imagining an army, stop. I could count us without taking off my shoes. Twenty-some people, against a movement. A schoolteacher's resistance, funded by a dead man's gold and led by the only wizard Voldemort had ever feared.

But what people they were. Let me light a few candles, because almost every name in this paragraph is a grave now. The Prewett brothers, Gideon and Fabian, who fought back to back and laughed like Sirius, and who took five Death Eaters to kill the two of them. Benjy Fenwick, who was so gentle before the war that he apologised to owls. Dorcas Meadowes, so effective that in the end Voldemort did not send anyone; he came for her himself. Marlene McKinnon — Lily's best friend, keep her name, her chapter is coming. Frank and Alice Longbottom, newly married like James and Lily, brave in that quiet married way that has no performance in it at all. Old Mad-Eye Moody, scarred like a chopping board, who taught us duelling and shouted CONSTANT VIGILANCE until we heard it in our sleep. Young Hagrid, half-giant and whole heart. And Dumbledore at the head of the table, making even a cave feel like a headmaster's office, holding the map of the whole war behind half-moon glasses.

They gave us real work, and I will not pretend it was not, at first, terribly exciting. Guarding families on the target lists. Pulling Muggle-borns out of houses one night ahead of the masks. Watching, following, listening. James and Sirius fought like they had been built for it, and the stories got around; three times each, in those years, they stood in front of Voldemort himself and lived — a sentence I can write in nine words that cost me more than any other in this book.

I went where the Order could not politely go: to my own kind. There are werewolf packs at the edges of our world — bitter people, thrown out of jobs and families by the same laws that had hunted me, and Voldemort courted them with promises: *under me, no more registries, no more locked doors; under me, you hunt.* Dumbledore hoped a werewolf of his own could argue against the promises. So several times a year I vanished into the cold camps under false names and argued, in the careful sentences of a spy, for a world that had never once been kind to me.

Mark what that meant at home, though, because it is a wheel in the machine that kills everyone at the end of this book: I vanished, for weeks, and could not say where, and could not say why, even to James, even to Sirius. Dumbledore's orders. Secrecy was the Order's blood — we worked in small cells, each knowing only pieces, so that no captured friend could spill the whole. Wise. Standard. And it meant that all of us, all the time, were disappearing from each other behind walls of permitted silence.

Now set the last stone in place. Peter.

Peter was in the Order too. Do not let the later story tell you he wandered in by accident; he was asked, same as us, and he came, same as us, and in the beginning he was genuinely useful. Small, unnoticed, mild — the perfect watcher. He carried messages. He kept lists. He sat in corners of pubs hearing things, a soft round man with a half-pint, invisible in plain sight. We had spent seven years building our lives around exactly this Peter — the one who slips through quietly and presses the hidden root that the rest of us cannot reach.

It never once occurred to any of us — this sentence again; it is the sentence of my life — that the same gift points in any direction it is aimed. That the man nobody notices leaving your meeting is also the man nobody notices arriving at another one.

We fought for two years, and we were young and together and sometimes even winning, and the water kept rising anyway.

## 15. White Dress in a Dark Time

They married in the autumn of 1978, in the garden of the old Potter house, and I am now going to spend a whole chapter on one afternoon, because I promised you joy earns its chapters, and because when everything after is ash, an afternoon is exactly the size of thing a survivor keeps.

It was small. War-small: you did not gather crowds in 1978, and you did not print invitations with an address on them. Perhaps thirty people, told the place by owl the day before. Fairy lights in the apple trees. Tables from the kitchen carried out onto the grass. Mad-Eye stumping the edge of the garden at intervals, checking the protective spells, growling that the cake was a security risk; the cake, to be clear, not anything near the cake; he had reasons; nobody asked.

Lily wore her mother's dress and no shoes by the second hour. Her sister did not come — the Muggle sister, Petunia; there is an ordinary human sadness that magic never fixed — but her parents did, two kind bewildered dentists standing in a garden where the teapot poured itself, watching their daughter marry into a fairy tale that they could feel, though no one said it, had teeth.

James could not stand still. He was never still, but that day he vibrated; he greeted every guest twice; he hugged Hagrid, who cried, which set off Peter, who always cried at the same moments Hagrid did, I think honestly as a service, so that no one large would cry alone.

And Sirius was best man. Of course. Who else on this earth. He stood beside James in dress robes he had made a point of wearing incorrectly, holding the rings, and when the little wizard performing the ceremony asked for them, Sirius pretended, for three full seconds, to have lost them — patting his pockets, horror on his face — and the garden laughed, and Lily laughed hardest, and he produced them from behind James's ear like a Muggle magician. That was his speech, too, later: no fine sentences, just stories, each one worse for James than the last, until James was the colour of the wine and Lily was weeping into her veil from laughing.

But I saw the other thing, because I was standing where I always stood, a little to the side, where you see things.

When James and Lily said the words — the old binding words, *until the end* — Sirius's face came all the way open. Just for a moment. The laughing armour, the whole performance of Sirius Black, swung open like a gate, and behind it was something I had only seen once before, over a red hand-knitted jumper: a boy watching, from very close, a thing he wanted and did not believe existed. A vow that would actually be kept. A family on purpose.

He caught me seeing him. And — this is the measure of that day — he did not put the armour back on. He raised his glass an inch, to me, across the garden. I raised mine. Neither of us said anything, then or ever. There are toasts that have no words; we drank to the same thing; I believe we both knew it.

The war took one month off, in my memory, for that afternoon. False, of course — that same week, elsewhere, the lists grew — but memory is not a court, and in mine the sun sets very slowly on that garden, fairy lights coming on in the apple trees, Peter asleep in a chair with cake on his shirt, Lily barefoot in the grass teaching her bewildered father to wave a wand that would not work for him, James and Sirius shoulder to shoulder by the fence, quiet for once, two brothers by choice looking out at the dark fields.

Three years, almost to the week, from that sunset to Halloween of 1981.

If some cruel power had walked into the garden that evening and told us — *one of the people at these tables is already, or soon, carrying your deaths* — I know exactly what we would have done. We would have laughed. Not from courage. From certainty. You could have put us under oath, every guest, every friend: name the person here who could sell James and Lily.

No one would have said the man asleep in the chair, with cake on his shirt, who cried when Hagrid cried.

## 16. Small Rat, Big Fear

I must do a difficult thing now. I must go inside Peter.

Understand what this chapter is: not knowledge, but the work of many years. I have laid every fact I own side by side — things he said, things he did, things he confessed at the very end with my wand at his chest — and I have walked around them ten thousand nights, asking the only question that survivors of a betrayal ever really ask: *how.* Not why. How. How does the boy from the train become the man at the door.

Here is my answer. I warn you: the horror of it is that there is no horror in it. It is all so small.

Begin with this. For seven years at school, Peter Pettigrew stood in the light of three unusually bright people, and everyone — teachers, students, us — looked at him and privately thought a version of the same sentence: *what is he doing with them?* Do you know what it costs, hearing that question in every glance for seven years? We thought our friendship answered it. I think our friendship *asked* it, every single day, and he had to answer it, every single day, and his answer was work: laugh first, agree fast, fetch, flatter, stay useful, stay near. We read it as devotion. It was devotion. It was also, though we never saw it, exhausting fear — fear of the only thing Peter truly could not survive, which was being put out of the light.

School ended. And the light changed.

At school, being near James and Sirius cost nothing but homework. In the Order, the bill arrived. Being near them now meant duelling masked men on rooftops. It meant Voldemort himself, twice, three times — and do you remember what the stories said? *James and Sirius defied him and lived.* The stories never said what Peter did in those rooms, and I never asked. I ask now. I think he stood behind his friends' shoulders and looked into that white face, those red eyes, the way a mouse looks into a cat's — and understood something the brave are protected from understanding.

The brave calculate wrongly, you see. James, Sirius, the Prewetts, all our beautiful dead — each privately believed they would probably live. It is the lie courage runs on. Peter had no such protection. He was a small man with accurate eyes, and he did the mathematics honestly, and the mathematics said: *these people are going to lose, and I am going to die with them — not for anything, not saving anyone, just die, in a corridor, because I followed my school friends into their war.*

And the other side had noticed him.

That is the part I only assembled later, from trial records and testimony after everything ended. The Death Eaters were clever about people; terror is a kind of attention. They watched our little Order and they did not waste recruiters on Sirius Black or on me. They looked for the seam. They found the soft man with the Ministry desk and the tired eyes, and they did not threaten him first. First — and this is the thing I cannot stop seeing — first, someone *flattered* him. In some pub, some evening, some smooth-voiced man told frightened, weightless Peter Pettigrew that people had noticed him. That his gifts were wasted where he was. That the winning side rewards men who are realistic.

They offered the exact thing we had given him at eleven. A place near power, safe from it. The rat rides between the antlers, and the forest is afraid of the stag, and nothing can touch the rider.

You want a monster in this chapter. I understand; I wanted one for years; hatred is so much lighter to carry when it has claws. But I promised you the truth, and the truth is a small wet-eyed man at a corner table, listening, saying nothing treasonous at all — that first evening. Just listening. Just warming himself, one evening, at a second fire.

That is how. Not a fall — a lean. Nobody sells four lives in an afternoon. He leaned, an inch a month, from fear toward safety, from safety toward secrecy, from secrecy toward the first small payment — and every inch of the way, I am certain, he told himself the story cowards always tell: *I have done nothing yet. I can still stop. It is only talk. I am still their friend.*

He was at the wedding after the leaning had already begun. Cake on his shirt. Crying when Hagrid cried. Still our friend — in his own head, honestly still our friend, planning nothing final.

The door to hell, reader, is not a door. It is a slope with no single step on it worth dying over. That is the whole engineering of it. That is why the brave miss it: they guard against the dramatic moment, the temptation with trumpets, and there is none. There is a tired man, a warm room, a listening ear, and a thousand tiny mercies extended to oneself, one at a time, until one October night there is a cottage with its door blown off its hinges, and a baby crying over two bodies, and the man who caused it is *still telling himself the story.*

At the end, in the Shrieking Shack, twelve years too late, with his life in his own mouth, Peter looked at me — at all that remained of us — and said, and I believe him, God help me, I believe this was the true confession under all the squealing:

"You don't understand. He would have killed me."

Yes, Peter. He might have. That was the price of the roof at sunrise, of *agreed, agreed, agreed, agreed*. Three of us had always known the price and signed anyway. You signed for the seat near the fire, and when the bill came, you paid it with our lives instead of yours.

That is the how. It fits in a sentence, after ten thousand nights:

He was never wicked. He was never brave. And it turns out that in a war, the second thing, missing, does all the work of the first.

## 17. Friends Die

In the summer of 1981, the war stopped being a sea and became a hand, closing.

I will give you the arithmetic first, coldly, because we lived it coldly; there came a point when the heart simply refuses ceremony. The Order had numbered around twenty-five. By that summer we had buried, or worse, a third of them. Benjy Fenwick — they found only pieces of him; I told you he apologised to owls; hold both facts, that is the war in two facts. Gideon and Fabian Prewett, back to back, five against two, and the five needed all five. Dorcas Meadowes, personally, by *him*. Edgar Bones and his family — the whole family; the Death Eaters had started including families, as a message, and the message was received.

And in July — Marlene.

Marlene McKinnon had been at the wedding; she had helped Lily dress. She was loud and generous and utterly without fear, and she had been Lily's closest friend since they were girls, the friend from before James, the one who knew Lily's Muggle house and her awful sister and her whole unmagical heart. The McKinnons were attacked at night, at home. All of them. Marlene, her parents, her brothers. There was nothing left to defend by the time anyone knew.

I was at James and Lily's the morning the news came. I watched Lily read the letter standing up, and I watched her fold it, very carefully, into quarters, and put it on the table, and smooth it flat with her hand, and then keep smoothing it, over and over, long after it was flat, because the hand had to do something and there was nothing on this earth for it to do.

Harry was crying upstairs — he was a year old that July. She went up to him. Through the ceiling we heard her walking him, singing something Muggle, her voice absolutely level. James sat with his glasses in his hands, not moving. Sirius stood at the window like a man guarding a house that had already been robbed.

That was also the summer the deaths stopped being random.

Moody saw it first — of course; vigilance was his religion. Our operations were failing in a pattern. Safe houses found too fast. Guards ambushed at handovers only four people knew about. The McKinnons had been moved, quietly, two weeks before the attack; the attack came to the new address. Dorcas's location had been a secret shared with a handful. The handful kept shrinking, and the deaths kept finding the middle of it.

There was a word for what the pattern meant, and for a while nobody would say it, because saying it ended the world we had been fighting for. Moody said it, at last, at a meeting in somebody's kitchen, with the curtains sealed:

"We have a spy. Inside the Order. Close to the Potters."

Close to the Potters. Because that was the other thing, the thing I have not told you yet: by then, the war had a direction, and the direction was James and Lily's front door. Voldemort was hunting them — them specifically, their son specifically. Why, I did not know then; that terrible why has its own chapter. What we knew that summer was simpler and sufficient: the most hunted family in Britain was being tracked by someone who sat at our meetings, drank our tea, knew our faces. Someone we loved. There was no other candidate pool. That was what *inside* meant.

I want you to sit where we sat, in that sealed kitchen, and feel the poison start to work. Look around the table. Everyone there had bled for the cause; everyone there was above suspicion; and arithmetic said one of them was writing our funerals. So the mind — the loyal, grieving, exhausted mind — begins, against every order you give it, to sort its dearest people into *possible* and *impossible*.

And here is what my mind did, and I will confess it as plainly as I have confessed Peter's sins, because this book does not get to have two standards.

My mind said: *Sirius is possible.*

Not probable. Never probable. But — possible. He was a Black. His blood, his brother, his cousins, the whole serpent-handled house of them, were on the other side. And I had seen — long ago, a corridor, one sentence about a root and a tree — I had seen that under sufficient pressure, Sirius could spend a friend's life without thinking. The old splinter. It had waited fifteen years for exactly this infection.

And at the same table, Sirius's mind was working too. I know now what it concluded. *Remus is possible.* The werewolf. Disappears for weeks. Cannot say where — orders, he claims. Goes among the dark packs — his own kind, whom Voldemort courts with better promises than we could ever make. When had my missions gone wrong? Cross-check the dates. His mind, like mine, betrayed nobody in public. It just — sorted.

We were still brothers. We still guarded each other's lives. But between us now, behind the eyes, each kept one small locked drawer with the other's name in it.

And Peter passed both of our inspections without a second's pause. I have searched my memory of those months — searched it under bright lights, the way police search a room — for one flicker of doubt in his direction, from anyone, ever. There is nothing. He cried at the funerals. He was frightened in exactly the amounts a harmless man should be frightened. He asked, in meetings, the slightly slow questions of a man trying his best to keep up.

The spy hunt looked everywhere except at the floor. Meanwhile, month by month, the hand closed around the house in Godric's Hollow.

## 18. The Prophecy

Now the terrible why. Why, out of every fighting family in Britain, the war narrowed to one door.

I learned the full truth years later, in pieces, from Dumbledore himself; that summer we knew only the shape of it. But you, reader, may as well have it whole.

Some months before Harry was born, a prophecy was spoken. A true one — they are rare, and stranger than the fairground kind. It said, in the sideways language of such things, that as the seventh month died, a boy would be born to parents who had three times defied the Dark Lord — a boy with the power to end him.

Two families fit the words like a key fits a lock. The Longbottoms — Frank and Alice, quiet, married, thrice-defiant — expecting a boy at the end of July. And the Potters.

Voldemort should never have known a syllable of it. But the prophecy was overheard — the first half of it only — by an eager servant who ran to his master with it. And so a being of limitless power and limitless fear sat down with a riddle and did what fear always does: chose the answer that looked most like himself. The Longbottoms were old wizarding stock. Lily was Muggle-born; her son was a half-blood — like Voldemort himself, whose father was a Muggle, a fact he had murdered men for knowing. He looked into the riddle and saw his own reflection, and pointed his whole war at a baby in a knitted hat.

Harry James Potter, born the thirty-first of July, 1980. I held him at three days old. He had James's impossible hair, already, from the first hour — hair with its own opinions — and then he opened his eyes at me, and they were Lily's, exactly, green as the first thing that grows after a fire. Sirius was named godfather in that room, and stood holding the baby with a face I had seen twice before — the jumper, the wedding — the gate-swinging-open face. James put his arm around him and said, "If anything ever happens to us, Padfoot —" and Sirius said, "Nothing is going to happen to you," in a voice that ended the sentence like a door.

When Dumbledore came to them — late in 1980, on a night I was far away in the camps — he brought the worst sentence a parent can hear: *he has chosen your son.* He told them to disappear. Completely. No more missions, no more meetings, no more Order work for James and Lily Potter: a locked house, drawn curtains, a hidden life.

For Lily, the choice was no choice; she was a mother. For James — I want this understood, because his stillness cost him more than our movement cost us — for James it was a kind of death before death. Twenty years old, the best fighter of us all, forbidden the fight; James Potter's whole soul was built for standing between danger and other people, and the war had reversed the geometry: now everyone else stood between danger and him. He wrote me that winter — I keep the letter — one line of it says: *the worst part, Moony, is being the thing everyone I love is in danger* for.

They went under the Fidelius Charm in the last autumn. And here I must stop the story and teach, briefly, because everything that remains turns on this one piece of magic; learn it now and the ending will need no footnotes.

The Fidelius Charm hides a truth by placing it inside a single living soul. Not a code, not a wall — a person, called the Secret-Keeper. Once the charm is cast, the hidden thing — say, the location of a certain cottage in Godric's Hollow — cannot be found by anyone, ever, unless the Keeper himself chooses to tell it. Voldemort could press his face to their front window and see an empty ruin. Armies could search the village for a century and walk through their garden daily, blind. The house has left the world of findable things and exists only inside one man's faithfulness.

Perfect protection. With exactly one moving part.

You see it, of course. The charm does not fail; charms are honest. It has no weakness at all — except that it is built, entirely, out of the thing our war had been losing all year. It is a machine that runs on trust. You must pour every drop of your family's safety into a single human vessel, and so the entire question of survival becomes, nakedly and forever, the oldest question in this book, the question of the train compartment and the dormitory and the sealed kitchen:

*Which of your friends do you hand your life to?*

They had to choose in October, 1981. Spy-season. The month of locked drawers.

## 19. The Switch

The obvious Secret-Keeper was Sirius. Everyone knew it would be Sirius. That was, in the end, the whole problem.

Understand how the last week of October looked from inside each head, because this chapter is four decisions, made by four people, each one reasonable, locking together into a machine none of them could see.

Dumbledore offered first. He came to the cottage and said: let it be me. Voldemort himself could not pull a secret out of Albus Dumbledore. James said no — with love, with thanks, but no. Some of that was the old certainty: we look after our own; the Marauders had never once, in ten years, needed rescuing from outside the circle of four. And some of it — I learned this much later — was darker: by then, whispers of the spy had reached even Dumbledore's ear, and Dumbledore trusted no one completely, and James could hear that in his offer, and it stung. If the choice lay between handing his family to the great man who suspected everyone, or to the brother who had held the rings at his wedding — for James Potter that was no choice at all.

So: Sirius. It was settled for about three days.

And then Sirius sat in the cottage kitchen — Lily making tea, Harry asleep, the last ordinary evening of that family's life ticking quietly on the shelf — and unmade it. I have the scene from him, told to me twelve years after, in a voice I did not recognise, and I give it to you almost in his words.

"Think about it like they will," he said. "The whole world knows James doesn't move without me. I'm the obvious lock on this door — so obvious that the door might as well be marked. He'll come for me first, James. First hour of the first day. And —" here the pause, here the whole tragedy in one breath of honesty — "I can hold out against a lot. I don't know if I can hold out against *him*. Nobody knows that about themselves. I won't be the single thing between you and — no. Not me."

And then the idea. The clever, loving, fatal idea.

"Use Peter. You see it, don't you? It's perfect. It's the map all over again — nobody watches the small ones. Voldemort will hunt *me*. I'll run, loudly. Decoy. Let him spend everything breaking the obvious lock, and the whole time, the real key sits quiet in the pocket of the one man on earth he'd never think to search. Wormtail hides; I draw fire. It's the best trick we ever pulled."

Read it again. It is a good plan. That is what I need you to sit with — it is genuinely, technically, a good plan, built from true observations: people do overlook Peter; Sirius was the obvious target; a decoy does add a layer. Sirius offered to take the dangerous half himself — his half of the plan was *being hunted by Voldemort*, and he volunteered for it with both hands. Men have been made saints for less. Do not let anyone simplify this chapter into carelessness. He was not careless. He aimed every drop of his cleverness and courage at saving them, and because one load-bearing fact in his machine was false — one fact no one alive doubted — the cleverness and the courage carried the death warrant straight to the door and handed it over with the rings.

James laughed and agreed. Lily — Lily asked twice whether Peter himself was willing; that was Lily, thinking even then of the weight on the smallest back. And Peter?

Peter said yes.

Here is where my imagination stops obeying me, reader. I have stood in every corner of that kitchen ten thousand nights. The fire. The tea. Harry's stuffed toy on the floor. And Peter Pettigrew — already months in Voldemort's service, already carrying the deaths of better men on his soft hands — listening as his three dearest friends, faces bright with relief and trust, invent for themselves the plan that removes every obstacle between his master and this room, and beg him, *beg him please*, to accept the very position his master would have starved a continent to place him in.

He did not have to hunt the secret. The secret walked to him, embraced him, and thanked him for the favour.

What was in him in that moment? Even now I do not know. The confession in the Shack skipped it; his eyes went somewhere else. I have wondered — on the merciful nights I have wondered — whether some piece of him said yes hoping to be caught; whether the boy from the train screamed anywhere at all behind the wet frightened eyes. On the other nights I remember that he had a week of chances to whisper one sentence to anyone — *I am compromised, use Dumbledore* — one sentence, deniable, safe, and every one of those chances he let fall, quietly, the way he did everything.

They cast the Fidelius in the last days of October. Dumbledore was not told of the change. Nor was I.

And now the final decision of the four, the small hinge everything swings on, the one that was aimed at me.

The switch had to stay secret to work — of course; a decoy only draws fire while the world believes him real. But the circle of who knew was drawn at exactly three: James, Lily, Peter. Sirius knew the plan was made but even the timing was kept loose; and I — I, Moony, one of the four names on the map — was left outside entirely, and the reason was left politely unspoken, and I felt the shape of the unspoken thing like a wall of glass.

The locked drawers, reader. The sealed-kitchen arithmetic. *The spy is close to the Potters.* Sirius had done his silent sorting long ago, and his drawer had my name in it: the werewolf, who vanishes among the dark packs, who cannot say where he goes. Tell Remus nothing — not from hatred; never from hatred; from love doing triage under fire, which is the most forgivable form of the unforgivable. And I, who had a drawer of my own with his name in it, felt myself shut out and drew the mirror conclusion. The last week of October, 1981, the two of us — the two *innocent* men — circled each other in the ruins of a ten-year trust, each privately grieving the other as maybe-fallen.

While the rat sat in the pocket of the secret, and the house in Godric's Hollow filled up, evening by evening, with its last seven days.

## 20. The Last Week

What does the end of the world look like, from one week out? I can tell you exactly. It looks like nothing.

I have kept my diary from October 1981. It is the most terrible object I own, precisely because there is no terror in it. Tuesday: rain, letter from Mother, the Prewetts' memorial moved to the 9th. Wednesday: camp business — I was between missions, sleeping on a friend's floor in Yorkshire, eating badly. Thursday: *tried to call on J & L via the usual arrangement; put off again. Third time. Something is wrong, or I am.* Friday: nothing. Saturday: nothing.

*Put off again. Third time.* One line of dry ink is all my life's great regret gets, because I did not know it was the last week; nobody stamps the last week for you. Since the Fidelius, visits to the cottage went through channels, for safety — requests passed along, times arranged. And my requests, that autumn, kept sliding. Not refused — nothing is ever refused among friends; just deferred, rescheduled, *soon, Moony, things are mad right now, you understand.*

I understood. That was the poison working exactly as designed: I understood *perfectly*. The circle around James and Lily had pulled tight, and I was outside it, and there was only one reason I could construct for that, and the reason had my own guilt in it — because meanwhile I could not tell them where I went either. Two innocences, each reading the other as evidence. Voldemort's cruelest weapon that year was not the Killing Curse. It was inference.

So the last week of October, while the machine finished closing, here is where the four of us stood. Attend to the geography; it is the whole ending.

James and Lily were inside the charm, inside the cottage, inside a safety total and absolute — conditional on one man. James chafed; paced; wrote letters. His last one to me is dated the 28th. It is four sentences, and none of them matter — Quidditch, a joke about Harry's first broom, *come after the moon, we'll fix a date properly* — four sentences of ordinary love from a man with three days to live, and I have read that scrap of paper more times than every book I own together.

Sirius was running his half of the plan: being visible, being loud, being bait. Riding the motorbike high over motorways, sleeping in a different place each night, letting himself be seen far from Godric's Hollow. Alone — the plan required even his friends kept at arm's length — carrying what he believed was the war's most dangerous role, believing his own courage was, at that hour, standing between Voldemort and everyone he loved. Every mile of it aimed away from the true door. He might as well have been guarding the moon.

Peter had gone quiet "into hiding" — the story fit the plan; the plan was his alibi; the alibi was our own invention. Somewhere in those days, in some dark place I will not invent because even my hatred refuses the scene, he stood before his master and opened his mouth and said an address.

That is the moment, if you are looking for the moment. Not Halloween. Halloween was execution; this was the murder. A sentence of perhaps eight words, spoken by the boy we fed on the train, in exchange for — and here the ledger of history is exact — his own continued heartbeat, and nothing else. No throne. No gold. History should record that the price of the Potters was one small man's permission to keep being afraid.

And I was on a floor in Yorkshire, an hour away by any broom, waiting for a visit that was never going to be scheduled, checking the lunar calendar — the moon was coming on the 12th — and nursing, God forgive me, my grievance. My last coherent feeling toward the four of us, before the world ended, was a sulk. That the others had drifted. That Sirius was cold on the mirror-calls. That war was making us strangers. I fell asleep on the 30th of October composing, half-angrily, the letter I would send James after the moon: *old friend, whatever is going on, the four of us are older than this war —*

I never sent it. There was no one left to send it to.

On the morning of the 31st of October, 1981, the cottage at Godric's Hollow held a man of twenty-one, a woman of twenty-one, and a boy of one year and three months. It was a Saturday. Lily carved a pumpkin. There is testimony to this — the last neighbour to see them alive, through the one window the charm let her see as a ruin's window, none of it real to her until after. A pumpkin, on a step, in the rain.

The world does not stamp the last week. It lets you spend it on rain, and rescheduling, and a sulk.

## Part Three: The Longest Night

Everything in this book so far took ten years. Everything that remains took about eight hours.

One night — Halloween into the first grey of November, 1981 — unmade the four of us so completely that when the sun rose, there was no version of the world in which we had ever been happy. Two of us were dead. One was on his way to a cage beyond description. One was a hero, and a rat. And one — I was always the lucky one, people said so all my life — one was left standing, in a borrowed room in Yorkshire, to spend twelve years believing the wrong story with all his heart.

I have told you everything in order, as I promised. Here is the order's end.

## 21. Halloween

He came on foot, they say. Through the falling leaves, down the middle of the lane, past the lit windows of Muggle children in paper masks — the one night of the year when a walking horror draws no eyes at all. Halloween gave him that. He always did have a taste for theatre.

The charm fell before him like a curtain drawn aside — for the Keeper had told, and what the Keeper tells, the world may enter. Where every eye for a year had seen a ruin, he saw it: lit windows, a garden gate, a pumpkin on a step. The hidden house of the Potters, standing in the world of findable things, with its door.

I was not there. No friend was there; that was the design of it. What follows is assembled from the testimony of the aftermath, from Dumbledore, and from the one living witness — who was one year old, and who met the memory again, years later, in the presence of certain dark creatures that force the worst hour of a life back up into the light. Harry paid for these facts. Remember that when you read them.

James died first, and he died exactly as everyone who ever knew him would have written it, and I mean that as the highest sentence I can build: he heard the door and shouted to Lily — *take Harry and run, I'll hold him off* — and went into the hallway to buy time.

His wand was on the sofa.

Do not rush past that. For eleven years I have kept that detail in the place other men keep prayers. It was Halloween in a safe house; he had been making coloured smoke for Harry with it, earlier, Lily's letter to Sirius said so, coloured smoke and a laughing baby; and when the end of the world came through his front door, James Potter, twenty-one, the finest duellist of our year, stepped toward it with empty hands. Buying seconds. Seconds are all the stairs need, if you love the people on them. There was no version of James — none, in any world — that stays behind the wife and child while the door comes down. Voldemort killed him in the hallway of his own house, and I hope — it is my oldest hope — that it was too fast to hurt.

Upstairs, Lily had no way out. The design of the trap was total; the windows were sealed by the same falling charm that had let him in. She put Harry in his cot, and she put herself between the cot and the door, and this you must hear precisely, because the exact words became the axis of the entire world after: he offered her life. *Stand aside, girl.* Three times — the testimony is specific — three times he told her she did not need to die, that his business was only with the boy. This was no mercy of Voldemort's; I will not let the scene be misread. Mercy was not in him. Someone had asked him for her life — and years passed before I learned whose asking that was, and the learning redrew yet another map of those years; but that is a story past the edge of this book. What matters is the offer, and what she did with it.

She refused. Three times. *Take me, take me instead. Not Harry, please — not Harry.*

She was twenty-one. She was holding no wand either. She could not have known what her refusal would do — no book anywhere promised it would do anything. It was not a spell, not a plan, not anything but the plain vertical fact of a mother declining to move. And when he killed her — annoyed, they say his voice was, *annoyed* — and turned the wand at last on the child in the cot and spoke the killing words that had never once failed in all his years of murder —

The curse broke.

It struck the boy, and it rebounded, and it tore Voldemort out of his own body like a hand pulled from a glove. The house came down around the two of them. And in the smoking ruin of the nursery, under half a fallen roof, with a cut on his forehead in the shape of lightning, Harry Potter sat in his cot — alive. Crying for his mother. Alive.

The books your world writes about ours begin here, with the miracle: the Boy Who Lived, the night the war ended, bells and owls and toasts raised by strangers. All true. The darkest wizard of the age undone by a baby; there has never been such a night.

But this book is not those books, and from inside our story, reader, here is Halloween entire: the safest house in Britain opened like a tin; the two brightest people I will ever know dead on their own floors, twenty-one years old, wandless, buying seconds; a miracle of love standing in the wreck of it — and the whole night purchased, unlocked, and delivered by the small friend of their childhood, in exchange for the continued beating of his own heart.

Somewhere in England at that hour, Peter Pettigrew heard that his master had gone into a house he himself had opened, and had not come out.

And began, at once — you must grant the survivor's instinct its dark genius — began to think about doors.

## 22. The Ruined House

Sirius knew before anyone told him.

He had arranged to check on Peter that night — part of the plan, the guard checking the vault. He flew the motorbike to the hiding place, and found it empty. No Peter. No body. No sign of a fight: cups on the table, bed made, a lamp left burning. Just — gone, softly, the way rats leave, and in that instant, he told me twelve years later, the entire year turned over in his head like a picture righting itself: the leaks, the ambushes, the deaths that found the middle of every shrinking circle. Not Remus. *Not Remus.* Oh God. Peter.

He flew to Godric's Hollow with his heart already knowing.

Try, if you can bear it, to ride behind him. Low over the October country, the cold rain needling, the bike screaming — and then the village, and then the lane, and then the thing that could not be there: the house *visible*. From the air. To his eyes. He knew exactly what that meant; he had helped design what that meant. The charm holds until the Keeper tells. If you can see the house, the secret has been spoken, and there was only one living mouth the secret was in.

The roof was torn open like paper. Smoke still standing in the rain.

He found James in the hallway. I will not stay in this paragraph long, and he never stayed in it at all — in twelve years of cages and two years of freedom, Sirius Black described that hallway to me exactly once, in one sentence, and the sentence was: *"His glasses were still on."* Whatever else that night burned out of him, there — I believe — is where the burning began: kneeling in the doorway of the house he had been given his own keys to, at the body of the boy from the train, the brother by choice, still wearing his glasses, still between the door and the stairs.

Lily was in the nursery. And in the wreck of the cot, wet and screaming and bleeding from the forehead — Harry.

For one moment — hold this moment; it is the last unbroken piece of Sirius Black — for one moment, standing in the ruin with the baby in his arms, there was still a road. He was the godfather. It was legal, it was named, it was what James's will asked. A cottage somewhere; a war-orphan and a war-orphaned man; the bike sold; a pram bought. Harry raised by the man his father loved most, who would have — I state it as fact, I knew him — who would have died for that child daily and called it wages.

Hagrid was there, in the garden. Dumbledore's man, sent the moment the wards fell, with Dumbledore's orders: the boy goes to his mother's Muggle sister. Protections in the blood, plans already forming — Dumbledore saw far, and, as always, told no one the whole of anything. And Sirius — Sirius stood in the rain holding everything he had left, and argued, and Hagrid, gentlest of giants, wept and held firm, orders are orders, Sirius, the boy's to go to his aunt's —

And Sirius gave in.

Gave Harry into Hagrid's arms. And then — the detail that history mistook for a clue and I know for a goodbye — gave him the motorbike. *Take it. I won't need it.* The bike he loved, the letter home on wheels, his one possession that meant anything: handed up into a giant's hands along with the future. People later read it as a man shedding weight before running. It was a man shedding *everything*. He had done his sums in the rain and arrived at a total, and the total was: James is in the hallway because of me. My plan. My clever plan. My mouth that said *use Peter.* The godfather's road — the pram, the cottage, the daily dying-for — belonged to a man who could still carry a future, and he had concluded, in the wet dark, with the smell of the burnt house in him, that he was not such a man. One thing remained that he was fit for, one service left he could do the hallway and the nursery.

Find the rat.

Hagrid rose into the rain on a flying motorbike with the Boy Who Lived asleep against his chest, toward the lit rooms where the story you already know was beginning. And down in the dark garden of the ruined house, alone now with the two quietest people he had ever heard, the last Marauder standing turned his face toward the city, and went hunting.

## 23. The Street of Muggles

It took Sirius one day to find him. Give the hunt its due: one day, in a country the size of ours, with the whole Ministry also looking — for Sirius, as it happens, though he did not yet know it. The rat-catcher was faster than all of them. He knew Peter's habits, Peter's boltholes, Peter's mother's street; he had watched over Peter for ten years, and the same knowledge serves love and vengeance equally well. It is the identical map. Only the direction of travel changes.

He cornered him at midday, the first of November, on an ordinary Muggle street in London. Shops, buses, mothers with prams; the Muggle world was drinking its Tuesday tea, and — this detail belongs to the picture — our world was erupting around it everywhere, owls by daylight, men in cloaks toasting in the streets, shooting stars over Kent, because for everyone else in wizarding Britain this was the first morning of the peace. The bells were still ringing when the last two Marauders faced each other on the pavement.

Sirius had his wand out. And here his account and the world's account agree completely; the difference is only in what everything meant. He meant to take Peter in — or to finish it there; twelve years later he was honest enough to say he no longer knew which, that the hallway and the glasses were the whole of his mind by then. It did not matter. He never got to choose.

Because Peter — and now, reader, watch the small man of this book perform, one time, at the very end, in front of witnesses — Peter chose everything.

He backed away, and he began to shout. Not at Sirius: *for the street.* For the two dozen Muggle heads already turning. "Lily and James, Sirius!" — his voice cracking, carrying, pitched to be remembered, every word placed like a witness statement — *"How could you? Lily and James!"*

Then, behind his back, hidden from the crowd: his own wand.

He blew the street open. One curse into the pavement — into the gas main beneath it, the Muggle investigators concluded, insofar as they were permitted to conclude anything. Twelve Muggles died where they stood. Mothers, shop-men, a boy with a delivery bicycle. Twelve names history barely kept, killed for punctuation — to end a sentence that was framing another man.

And in the smoke and the screaming, Peter Pettigrew — who had spent three years of his youth learning, in secret, for love of a friend, to become at will a small unremarkable animal — cut off his own finger, dropped it in the wreckage, folded up into a rat, and went down a drain. Into the London sewers, with the rest of the story in his teeth.

Consider, one last time, the craft of it. It has our whole book inside it. The instant, animal reading of the situation — the mathematics of survival, done in a heartbeat, correct to the last decimal. The public voice borrowed from better men: he shouted at Sirius exactly what Sirius, at that moment, was feeling — *how could you* — the accusation pre-stolen, the way he had always borrowed courage, warmth, a place by the fire. The finger: because a fled man is hunted, but a dead man is mourned, and Peter understood to the bone that safety is not being defended — safety is being *unlooked-for*. He had been unlooked-for all his life. He made it a fortress. They awarded that finger, later, the Order of Merlin, First Class, and sent it to his mother in a box.

And Sirius?

When the Ministry men arrived — twenty of them, wands drawn, at the crater's edge — they found the last Marauder standing in the wreckage of everything, and he was laughing.

I need you to stand very still while I try to carry this laugh across to you, because it convicted him — the Muggle witnesses, the wizard witnesses, twenty Ministry men, all testified to it, and each retelling made it more monstrous: the Black heir, the traitor, the mass-murderer, laughing among the bodies. It closed the case before there was a case; no trial was ever held. Why would you try a man the whole street heard accused, at whose feet lay twelve dead and a friend's finger, and who *laughed*?

But you have read this book. You were on the train; you saw the boy clap for himself when no one else would; you know that laughter was the only armour Sirius Black ever owned, riveted on in a house where crying was fed to the wall-mounted heads. And you know what he had, in that moment, just watched happen. The friend for whom he had offered to be hunted in decoy — the friend whose innocence was the last plank of a burnt life — had, in five seconds, framed him for the two murders that were already eating him alive, killed twelve people as a *flourish*, and escaped down a drain wearing the shape that the four of them had built together, as children, out of loyalty, for love of me. The map never lies; the animal was the lie. Every step of the trap he was standing in had been cut from the timber of their friendship.

What is left, at the centre of a man, when that finishes arriving? I have been near the place; the night I learned the truth, I felt the edge of it. There is nothing there to speak with. Speech belongs to the world with sense in it. What came out of Sirius Black in the crater was the sound of the world's sense ending — grief with no floor under it, wearing the only face his childhood ever taught it. The Prophet printed it as the cackle of a fiend. Print this instead: it was the sound a dog makes, alone, over the ruin of its house. They took him without a fight. I do not think he noticed being taken.

They shipped him to Azkaban — the sea-fortress, the cage of the soul-eating guards — that same week. No trial, reader. Mark it in the margin in your own hand: *no trial.* Barty Crouch's signature, wartime powers, case closed. The cell door shut on the twenty-two-year-old boy who had knelt in the hallway at the glasses, and the world outside toasted the end of the war, and the account was considered settled: Black the traitor, Pettigrew the martyr, Potter the miracle.

Three lies out of three. The bells rang for days.

## 24. The Empty World

Now I must write the chapter I have avoided for forty years: mine.

Where was I on the night of the thirty-first? You know where. A borrowed floor in Yorkshire, an hour away, nursing a sulk. I slept through the end of the world. That is not a figure of speech; I ask you to feel the plain size of it: the house fell, the street learned it, owls went up all over Britain — and Moony slept, and woke on the first of November to an ordinary ceiling, and lay there for a minute, and his last thought of the old world was that it looked like rain.

The news came the way it always comes for the ones a step outside the circle: late, secondhand, and all at once. Not a friend at the door. A newspaper. I stood in a shop doorway with the Prophet shaking in my hands — the photograph of the ruined cottage on the front, the headlines stacked like graves — and read, in the space of ninety seconds, standing up, in public, the following, in order: that James was dead. That Lily was dead. That Harry lived. That Voldemort was gone. And that all of it had been done — sold, arranged, betrayed — by Sirius Black.

Ninety seconds. Four names. My whole population.

Here is the thing I have never written down before. It made *sense.* That is my shame; hold the light steady on it. When the words *Black betrayed them* went in, something in me — not all of me, but a real part, a part with a vote — something in me said: *ah.* The locked drawer swung open by itself, and everything I had filed in it came out in order: the family, the blood, the brother behind the masks; the corridor, sixteen years old, one sentence, my body spent like a coin; the cold mirror-calls of the last autumn; the visits deferred, the circle closed against me. I had built the case against my brother for years without admitting I was building it, and the newspaper called for the verdict, and the part of me with the vote said *guilty,* and the case snapped shut with my grief inside it.

Peter's genius — I will call it that; precision demands it — Peter's genius was never the lie itself. It was choosing the lie we were all already halfway to believing. He did not have to build the trap. He had watched us build it — watched the suspicion pass back and forth between Padfoot and Moony for a year, each of us politely grieving the other as maybe-fallen — and at the end he simply stepped out, and let the door we had hung ourselves swing shut, and went down his drain. The frame fit because we had carved it. All he supplied was the push.

So I believed it. Fully. For twelve years, reader, I believed Sirius Black killed my family — and do the arithmetic of what that made my grief: I could not even mourn cleanly. James and Lily, dead. Peter — brave, harmless Peter — dead too, I believed, heroically, torn to a finger for confronting the traitor. And Sirius, the brother of my whole youth, not dead but *worse*: transformed retroactively, every memory poisoned at the root. The train, the Hat, the jumper, the wedding — twelve years, every time a good memory of him surfaced, I had to kill it on arrival: *that was the performance of a spy.* I mourned three friends and executed the memory of the fourth, daily, for twelve years, and he was innocent in a sea-cage the entire time, keeping himself sane — he told me afterward — on the single thought: *I know I am innocent. They cannot eat that.*

Two men in two prisons, both furnished by Peter Pettigrew. His masterpiece had a matching pair.

What was left of my life picked itself up slowly, and I will give it to you in a paragraph, because this book is about the four of us and there were no longer four of us. Dumbledore vouched for me where he could; the werewolf laws did the rest. Work found and lost, found and lost — a classroom here, a warehouse there, gone each time the calendar betrayed me. Rooms rented near the tops of stairs. The moons, alone again, in locked sheds and cellars — no stag now, no dog, no small mad captain between the antlers; the wolf woke each month and found its pack gone and took the news out on my own body, as in the beginning. I got older. My clothes got older faster. I was twenty-two, then thirty, then thirty-three, and if you had asked me what I was, I would have said: the one who was left. The keeper of a story with no living listeners, wrong in its central fact.

And Harry — Harry grew up an hour and a world away, behind blood-wards, in a cupboard, as it turns out, under his aunt's stairs; Dumbledore's protections held the death out but no one inspected what they held *in*; that is a bill of Dumbledore's own, still being argued. I did not go to him. Mark this too, in the margin, in the honest column: the last friend did not go to the boy. There were reasons — good ones, Dumbledore's design, the wards, the werewolf laws, what claim had I, a monster on a calendar, on a child kept safe precisely by his distance from our burnt world? Twelve years of unimpeachable reasons. It is remarkable, I have found, how the finest reasons always post themselves between us and the door we are afraid to knock on. Peter taught me that about cowardice, in the end: mine wore better clothes than his, and cost less, and I have never entirely stopped hearing his voice on the subject.

Twelve years. And then, one summer morning in 1993, the Prophet ran a photograph of a family on holiday in Egypt — red-headed, cheerful, a boy with a rat on his shoulder — and in a cell in the North Sea, a man who had kept one thought alive for a decade looked at the front page, and at the small unremarkable animal in the picture, and saw that it was missing a toe.

## 25. The Rat in the Picture

You may know some of what follows; it touches the books your world has read. I will keep to my thread of it, the Marauders' thread, and be brief where other tellings are long.

Sirius Black escaped Azkaban in the summer of 1993. No one had ever done it; the guards of that place do not sleep, and what they eat cannot be starved out of reach — they feed on happiness, on hope, on every warm thing in a prisoner until nothing is left but the worst hour of his life, on a loop, forever. What saved Sirius, in the end, was the exact shape of his ruin. His sustaining thought — *I am innocent; Peter lives; the rat is with the boy* — was not a happy thought. The guards had no appetite for it. It was cold and pointed and it lay in his cell like a nail, and when the photograph from Egypt reached him — the rat on the boy's shoulder, one toe short, riding to Hogwarts with the red-headed family's youngest son, into the same school as Harry — the nail became a compass. He slipped his skin, walked out through the bars as a starved dog, and swam the North Sea.

That same autumn, Dumbledore offered me the Defence Against the Dark Arts post at Hogwarts. I have wondered since how much of that offer was kindness, how much was a chess move — the old man placing the one person who knew Sirius Black's mind, his tricks, his shapes, between the escaped "murderer" and the boy. Both, knowing Dumbledore. Everything was always both.

So I went back. Twelve years a shabby stranger, and then one September morning I walked again under the enchanted ceiling, and taught, in the same rooms where the four of us had passed notes, the children of the people we had fought beside and buried. And I met Harry.

He was thirteen. He had James's face, James's impossible hair, James's exact way of standing when he decided a thing was worth being punished for — and Lily's eyes, which meant that every conversation with him was held under the gaze of both my dead at once. He did not know me. That was correct — the unimpeachable reasons held; I was Professor Lupin, a stranger who taught him to fight fear, and found him good at it, better than his father had been, because the boy had more practice being afraid. I taught him the guardian-charm against the soul-eaters; his took the shape of a stag. I stood behind him the first time Prongs came silver out of his wand, and I am not able, even now, to put that moment into a sentence, so I will leave it standing in the doorway of one, and go on.

All year, Sirius circled the school as a dog, hunting the rat. All year I said nothing about what the dog might be — for how could I explain what I knew without confessing what we four had been, and what I still was, and how betrayed the map of my own history had become? The old silence, the prefect with his book, quiet cowardice in better clothes. It very nearly killed the boy. Let every account of that year state it plainly: the truth arrived in spite of me, not because of me.

It arrived in June, in the Shrieking Shack — the old cold house of my transformations, where every wall already knew us — and I will give you the scene in one breath, for it has been told elsewhere and I lived it at a temperature no telling reaches. The map — our map, never a liar — showed me a name walking the grounds beside Harry and his friends: PETER PETTIGREW. I ran without a cloak. And in the room at the top of the broken stairs, for one hour, the Marauders held their last meeting. Moony, thirteen years older. Padfoot, a skeleton wearing prison. And Wormtail — dragged out of the boy's terrified pet rat back into a soft, wet-eyed, balding man, twelve years fat on a family's kindness, pleading in a voice I knew from a train compartment: *Remus, Sirius, my old friends —*

He confessed. Cornered, at wandpoint, before Harry's eyes — the whole architecture of it came out: the leaning, the listening, the address spoken, the street, the finger, the drain. And there, over his kneeling body, the last two friends of James Potter prepared, together, at last in perfect agreement, to commit the murder the world had been punishing the wrong man for.

Harry stopped us.

Thirteen years old. He stepped between the wands and the rat who had sold his parents, and said no — not out of mercy for Peter; he said it precisely: *I don't reckon my dad would have wanted his best friends to become killers for you.* James's son, in the house of the wolf, teaching the wolf what James would have wanted. We lowered our wands, both of us, because the voice that commanded it was, in every way that has ever mattered to me, the voice of the man in the hallway.

And within the hour, the moon rose — that night, of all the nights of my life — and the wolf came, and in the chaos Peter folded up small one more time, and was gone into the dark grass. Alive. Free. On his way, though none of us knew it, to the master he would help rebuild, and to the further harms that lie beyond this book's last page.

So the truth won nothing, that night, in the world's ledger. Sirius remained hunted; I resigned my post when the school learned what I was; the rat ran to his reward. Set the scene beside Halloween of '81 and the pattern is exact: Peter escapes, the innocent pay, the world believes wrongly, twelve years later as twelve years before.

And yet I have never once counted that night a defeat, and I will tell you what was in it that outweighs the ledger. In one hour in a broken house I got back, from the drawer where I had executed it daily for twelve years, my brother — alive, innocent, his hand in mine over Peter's confession, the case against my own memory dismissed with costs. Harry got the truth of his father, and a godfather who loved him — two years they had, letters and stolen visits and one shining hour of *come and live with me*; too short, and not nothing. And the four of us got, at last, what this whole book has been: an accounting. Who stood where. Who opened which door. What the laughter meant. What the silence cost.

The truth arrived thirteen years late, on the last possible night, through our own old map in a child's hands.

It has never once been early, in my experience. But I have stopped believing it fails to arrive.

## 26. What I Know About Friendship

It is late now, in every sense, and the tea has gone cold, and I owe you an ending.

You have had the story: 1971 to 1994, train to Shack. If you came for the war, the war is told. But I promised at the beginning that this was a book about how friendship breaks, and a promise to a reader is a small Fidelius — it holds only if the keeper chooses to tell. So here, in place of an ending, is everything the four of us taught me, laid out plainly, one old man's accounting. Take what is useful. Leave the rest on the table with the cold tea.

First. The love was real. All of it, the whole time, including his. I have checked this conclusion for forty years the way you check a knot that once failed — and it holds. Peter loved us. The boy who cried when Hagrid cried, who gave me his last sweets, who drew eight hundred doors without complaining — that boy was not a mask over a monster. He was exactly what he seemed: a loving, frightened, ordinary person. The horror is not that the love was false. The horror is that love, in a person who has never once chosen the hard thing, weighs precisely nothing at the moment of pressure. It is weather. It is warmth without structure. When they came for him, his love for us had no vote, because a vote must be *cast*, and casting costs, and he had never in his life practised paying. Watch, therefore — this is the only advice in this book, so I will say it slowly — watch not what your people feel. Feeling is free. Watch what they have ever paid.

Second. The brave broke too, and differently, and I must be fair to the ways. James's certainty — *nothing gets in* — could not imagine the door opening from inside; his trust had no locks on it, and trust without locks is how you get a hallway. Sirius's cleverness built a perfect machine on one wrong fact; cleverness does that; it is always the load-bearing fact that goes. My patience — the politest cowardice in Gryffindor — sat quiet through every year the truth needed shouting: nearby, with a book, saying nothing. Four friends, four failures, one of them criminal. That proportion is the honest arithmetic of most disasters: one betrayal, resting on three ordinary human insufficiencies that opened the road for it. Judge Peter. I do. But if you leave this book blaming only Peter, I have wasted our winter evenings: the reason his one push toppled us is that we had, each in our own loving way, already built the fall.

Third — and this one took me longest, and cost the most, so I have put it last. Suspicion is not vigilance. For one whole year, every one of us was vigilant, Moody-vigilant, constant-vigilant — and the vigilance itself was the wound Voldemort used. Sirius and I, two innocent men, each concluded the other was the traitor, on evidence, reader — real evidence, dates and silences and bloodlines, better evidence than anyone ever had against Peter. The locked drawers did not protect us; they were the mechanism of the disaster. Here is what I know now, too late for everyone I have loved: distrust does not find traitors. Distrust *manufactures* them — it dissolves exactly the connective tissue that would have caught the real one. Had any two of us, in that sealed year, simply said aloud the unsayable — *I have a drawer with your name in it; show me I'm wrong; here is mine* — the whole machine jams. Peter survives on our silence. He fed on it twice: once as our friend, once as our suspect pool of everyone-but-him. The rat's whole species of safety is the unasked question.

That is the accounting. What remains?

This remains. It is a spring morning as I finish these pages, and on the desk in front of me lies a piece of old parchment, confiscated once by a caretaker, carried now by a boy with his mother's eyes — returned to me, for a while, by the strange circulation of loved objects. If I touch my wand to it and say the old words — *I solemnly swear that I am up to no good* — four names bloom in green ink across the top, in the handwriting of four sixteen-year-olds who sat up past midnight inventing themselves: Moony, Wormtail, Padfoot, Prongs.

I have been asked — Harry asked me, in fact, the year I taught him — why we never took Wormtail's name off the map.

The answer is that the map never lies, and the map says what we were: four. Not three and an error. Four boys, one round tower, the best years of a bad century; the stag, the dog, the wolf, and the rat, shoulder to shoulder on a hilltop over the sleeping village, alive, alive, all of it real, before any of the paying came due. The truth does not subtract. It only adds. What Peter became is on the map of the war. What Peter was is on our map, and I am the keeper of both now, and a keeper — you have read the book; you know the cost of the office — a keeper holds what he holds entire, or he is no keeper at all.

So: four names. I am the last of them above ground and out of hiding, the moon still finds me every month, and on the bad nights, the very bad ones, I light the lamp James bought a sick boy in 1971, and spread the map, and watch the little named footprints of children move along corridors we measured, and I say the four names in order, all four, and I do not explain, to anyone, why the fourth. This book is the explanation. Friendship was the country I was born in; it is burnt, and I will not renounce my citizenship.

Mischief managed. Put the lamp out gently, whoever is last.

## Как читать эту книгу

Книга написана простым английским, но это настоящая проза, а не учебник. Несколько советов:

- **Не переводи каждое слово.** Сначала прочитай абзац целиком. Обычно смысл понятен из истории. Наведи на слово (или тапни), только если без него никак.
- **Повторы — не случайность.** Ключевые слова (*betray, trust, secret, moon, brave*) возвращаются из главы в главу. К концу книги ты будешь знать их навсегда.
- **Врезка «New Words»** в конце главы — это самые важные слова главы. Просмотри их до или после чтения.
- **Кнопка перевода у абзаца** покажет русский перевод целиком. Полезно проверить себя после сложного абзаца.

## Мир Гарри Поттера: краткая справка

Эта история — фанфик по миру Джоан Роулинг. События происходят в 1971–1994 годах, за поколение до книг о Гарри.

- **Hogwarts (Хогвартс)** — школа магии в Шотландии. Четыре факультета: **Gryffindor** (храбрость), **Slytherin** (амбиции), **Ravenclaw** (ум), **Hufflepuff** (верность).
- **Muggle (магл)** — человек без магии. **Mudblood (грязнокровка)** — грубое оскорбление волшебников из семей маглов.
- **Voldemort (Волдеморт)** — тёмный волшебник, развязавший войну за «чистоту крови». Его сторонники — **Death Eaters (Пожиратели смерти)**.
- **Order of the Phoenix (Орден Феникса)** — тайное общество Дамблдора, воевавшее против Волдеморта.
- **Animagus (анимаг)** — волшебник, умеющий превращаться в животное. **Werewolf (оборотень)** — человек, который в полнолуние против воли становится волком.
- **Fidelius Charm (заклятие Доверия)** — чары, прячущие тайну внутри одного человека — **Secret-Keeper (Хранителя тайны)**.
- **Azkaban (Азкабан)** — тюрьма волшебников на острове в Северном море.
- **The Marauders (Мародёры)** — школьное прозвище четырёх друзей: **Moony** (Лунатик — Римус), **Wormtail** (Хвост — Питер), **Padfoot** (Бродяга — Сириус), **Prongs** (Сохатый — Джеймс).
