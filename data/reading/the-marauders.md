# THE MARAUDERS

### The story of four friends — easy English

---

## Читателю

Вы знаете, чем кончилась эта история. Хэллоуин 1981 года, разрушенный дом в Годриковой Впадине, мальчик со шрамом. Об этом написаны семь книг.

Но вы не знаете, как она началась. Роулинг так и не написала историю Мародёров — четырёх мальчишек, которые встретились в поезде в 1971 году и поклялись, что их дружбу не разрушит ничто. Джеймс. Сириус. Римус. Питер. Один из них — предатель, и вы с первой страницы знаете его имя. В этом и есть жестокость этой книги: вы будете смотреть, как он смеётся с друзьями, ест их сладости, плачет на их свадьбах, — и искать момент, где всё сломалось. Это детектив наоборот: не «кто убил», а «как хороший мальчик дошёл до этого». Рассказывает Римус Люпин — единственный, кто остался и узнал правду.

**Про язык.** Книга написана нарочно простым английским — примерно полторы тысячи самых частых слов, короткие предложения, обычные времена. Уровень не растёт к финалу и не проваливается в середине: последняя глава такая же простая, как первая. Всё редкое, что в книге всё-таки есть, — это слова её мира: *wand, werewolf, spell, traitor*. Они возвращаются десятки раз и запоминаются сами.

**Как читать.** Никаких правил здесь нет — это роман, а не учебник. Просто читай историю. Всё остальное — советы, и каждый можно нарушать.

Читай абзацами, а не словами. Наткнулся на незнакомое слово — иди дальше: чаще всего смысл догоняет через строчку. Если слово всё-таки не отпускает — коснись его, и появится перевод. Переведено каждое слово книги, так что застрять невозможно.

Кнопка перевода рядом с абзацем — запасной выход, а не часть ритуала. Жми её, когда совсем потерял нить, и спокойно двигайся дальше. Перечитывать абзац по второму разу не нужно: важные слова этой книги — *secret, trust, betray, moon* — сами вернутся к тебе десятки раз, и где-то к середине ты заметишь, что читаешь их не переводя. Так и задумано.

Иногда — не каждый раз, а под настроение — прослушай абзац кнопкой озвучки. Услышать фразу голосом — совсем другое дело, чем увидеть глазами: слова начинают звучать в голове, когда читаешь дальше сам.

Понравилось слово — поставь звёздочку. Оно уйдёт в «Избранное», и потом его можно погонять карточками в разделе «Словарь». Но это на потом; сейчас важнее история.

И не читай запоем. Главы здесь короткие — две-три за вечер лучший темп: закладка запомнит место, полоска прогресса покажет, сколько позади. В книге пятьдесят глав, и это настоящий роман, а не рассказ: к последней странице за спиной будет больше слов, чем в иной книге с полки.

Открывай первую главу. Поезд уже отходит.

## People in this Story

**Remus Lupin** — the one who tells this story. Quiet, kind, always tired. He has a secret.

**James Potter** — rich, funny, brave. The best flyer in school. Harry's future father.

**Sirius Black** — beautiful, loud, angry. His family is dark, but he is not.

**Peter Pettigrew** — small, soft, always near. He laughs at every joke. Remember him.

**Lily Evans** — clever and warm. Green eyes. James loves her from year one. She does not love James.

**Severus Snape** — a thin boy in old clothes. Lily's friend. He hates James.

**Albus Dumbledore** — the head of Hogwarts school. The only wizard Voldemort is afraid of.

**Voldemort** — the dark wizard. His war is coming.

## Part One: The Castle

I was eleven years old, and I thought my life was over.

That is a strange thing to say about a child. But it was true. I had a secret. It was so heavy that no school wanted me. No friend could know me. No future was open to me.

Then a letter came. After the letter, a train. And on that train — three boys.

This part of the story is about them. It is the happy part. Read it slowly. Happiness in this book does not stay long.

---
## 1. The Train

The first of September, 1971. King's Cross station in London. Rain.

I stood on the platform with my father. Around us, hundreds of children were saying goodbye to their parents. Owls cried in their cages. Cats ran between people's legs. A big red train stood there and made white smoke.

My father held my shoulder too hard.

"Remus," he said. "Remember the rules. Tell nobody. Not one person. If they learn what you are —"

"They will send me home. I know, Dad."

"Dumbledore is doing a dangerous thing. He is taking you when he does not have to. Do not make him sorry."

I nodded. I was very good at nodding. When you have a secret, you learn to nod and say nothing.

Then he was gone, and I was on the train.

I found an empty room and sat by the window. I hoped that nobody would come in. I was eleven years old, and my biggest hope in the world was to be alone.

The door opened.

Two boys fell into the room. I mean it: they really fell, laughing, one on top of the other. The first boy had black hair that stood up in every direction and round glasses. The second boy had long dark hair and the face of an old, rich family.

"Sorry," said the boy with the glasses. He was not sorry at all. "Every other room is full. I'm James. James Potter."

"Sirius Black," said the beautiful one.

"Black?" James looked at him with big eyes. "*The* Blacks? The dark family? The ones with the —"

"Yes. That family." Sirius's voice went flat, like a door closing. "And I am nothing like them, so be quiet about it."

For a second the room was cold. Then James smiled.

"Fine. I hate them too. Do you want a chocolate frog?"

And that was all. That is how the best friendship I have ever seen began: in one second, over a chocolate frog, between a boy who had everything and a boy who wanted to burn his own house down.

Then they turned to me.

"Who are you?" said James.

"Remus," I said. "Remus Lupin."

"You look ill, Remus," said Sirius.

It was true. The full moon had been four days before. My skin was grey and there was a cut on my neck. My father's words were loud in my head. *Tell nobody. Not one person.*

"I was ill," I said. "I'm better now."

James opened his mouth to ask more. Then the door opened again.

A small round boy stood there. His face was red and he could not breathe well.

"Can — can I sit here?" he said. "Some big boys took my sweets, and then they took my room, and —"

"They took your sweets?" James stood up. "Which boys? Come on, Sirius. This is not right."

They came back ten minutes later with the sweets. Their faces were red and they were laughing so hard that they could not speak.

"That was amazing," said the small boy. "*You* are amazing. I'm Peter. Peter Pettigrew."

Keep this picture in your head. I will keep it in mine for the rest of my life.

Four boys in a small train room. James giving Peter his sweets back like a king giving gold. Sirius with his feet on the seat, laughing. Peter looking at them with hungry, thankful eyes. And me in the corner: quiet, ill, sure that I could never be their friend. Friends do not have secrets like mine.

Outside the window, England ran past us. Green fields, small houses, rain.

Somewhere far away, in a place we could not see, a dark wizard was getting stronger. His name was Voldemort. In ten years he would take everything from every boy in that room.

But we did not know that. We were eleven. We ate chocolate frogs and laughed all the way to Scotland.

## 2. The Castle on the Water

You come to Hogwarts by boat. Nobody tells you that before.

The train stopped in the dark. Outside there was a small station, a wet wooden roof, and one lamp. Then a voice came out of the night, and it was the biggest voice I had ever heard.

"First years! First years, over here!"

He was a giant. His name was Hagrid, and he was, I can tell you now after forty years, the kindest man I have ever met. He had a black beard like a small forest and a lamp in his hand. He walked us down a narrow path to the water.

There were little boats waiting on the black lake. Four children to a boat.

"Get in!" said Hagrid. "Careful now. No standing up. Peter, is it? Sit down, lad."

The boats moved by themselves. Nobody rowed. We went out onto the water, and the rain stopped, and then we came round the side of a hill and saw it.

The castle.

A hundred towers. A thousand yellow windows. It stood on a mountain above the black lake, and every window was warm, and the whole thing sat in the water upside down as well, in the reflection.

Peter made a small sound, like a mouse.

James said a bad word.

Sirius said nothing at all. I understood why, much later. For him it was not just a castle. It was the door out of his terrible house.

For me it was something else again. That castle was the school that was not supposed to take me. There were rules about children like me, and the rules said no. Dumbledore had said yes.

I looked at the yellow windows and thought: *please. Please let me stay. I will be so careful. I will be so quiet that nobody will ever notice me.*

"Look at it," said James beside me. He was hanging over the side of the boat like he wanted to fall in. "Look at it, Remus. We live here now."

*We live here now.* He said it to me, a grey boy with a cut on his neck, as if it were true for both of us.

We came in under the mountain, through a curtain of green plants, into a dark cave under the castle. Then steps, and a huge door, and a tall witch in dark green robes waiting at the top.

"The first years, Professor McGonagall," said Hagrid.

"Thank you, Hagrid."

She looked at us. She had a straight back and a straight mouth and eyes that missed nothing. In seven years I never once got past those eyes with a lie. Nobody did. Sirius tried twice a week for seven years.

"In a few minutes," she said, "you will go through those doors and be sorted into your houses. There are four houses: Gryffindor, Hufflepuff, Ravenclaw and Slytherin. Your house will be like your family here. Your good work will win your house points. Any rule you break will lose them."

She looked at James's hair for one second longer than she needed to.

"Wait here quietly."

She went away. The moment the door closed, forty children began to talk at once.

"How do they sort us?" said Peter. His face had gone white. "My cousin says there's a test. He says you have to do magic in front of everybody —"

"That's rubbish," said Sirius. He was leaning on the wall with his arms crossed, and he sounded bored. But I saw his hands. They were shaking.

I said nothing. I looked at the floor.

I was not afraid of a test. I had read all the school books twice, and I was better at reading than at anything else in my life. I was afraid of something else. I was afraid that the magic hat, whatever it was, would look inside my head, and see the thing that lived there, and say it out loud in front of four hundred people.

Then the doors opened, and we walked into the light.

## 3. The Hat

The room was as big as a church and there was no ceiling.

That is what it looked like, anyway. Above our heads there was only the night sky, black and full of stars, with a thousand candles burning under it in the air. Four long tables. Hundreds of faces turning to look at us.

At the front there was a chair, and on the chair there was a very old, very dirty hat.

Then the hat opened a hole near the bottom, like a mouth, and sang a song about the four houses. When it finished, everybody clapped, and the hat sat still again.

"When I call your name," said Professor McGonagall, "you will sit down and put on the hat."

She had a long list in her hand.

"Black, Sirius!"

The hall went quiet. Everybody knew the Blacks. For three hundred years, every Black had gone to Slytherin. His mother, his father, his cousins — all of them. At the Slytherin table people were already smiling and moving along the bench to make a place for him.

The hat sat on his head for a long, long time.

I watched his face. He was afraid. The loud, beautiful boy from the train was afraid, and his hands held the sides of the chair so hard that they went white.

Then the hat opened its mouth and shouted: "GRYFFINDOR!"

Silence. Complete silence in the whole hall. At the Slytherin table, an older boy stood up, angry, and somebody pulled him back down.

Sirius took off the hat. I will never forget his face. It was the face of a man walking out of prison.

He went to the Gryffindor table alone. Nobody clapped for him. So he clapped for himself.

"Evans, Lily!"

A girl with dark red hair almost ran to the chair. The hat hardly touched her head.

"GRYFFINDOR!"

She smiled and sat down in front of Sirius. Then she looked back at the line of waiting children, at a thin boy with black oily hair and old clothes. He looked like somebody had taken away his sweets, his room and his whole world.

"Lupin, Remus!"

That was the longest walk of my life. The hat came down over my eyes and the hall disappeared.

*"Ah,"* said a small voice in my ear. *"Difficult. Very difficult. So much fear in here. You think you are a monster, boy?"*

*Please,* I thought. *Please just let me stay.*

*"A monster would not ask so politely,"* said the hat.

And then, out loud: "GRYFFINDOR!"

I sat down next to Lily Evans on legs made of water.

"Pettigrew, Peter!"

The hat thought about Peter almost as long as it had thought about Sirius. I ask myself about that now, all the time. What did it see? What did it see in that small soft head, and why did it still say the word it said?

"GRYFFINDOR!"

Maybe the hat does not see what you are. Maybe it sees what you want. And Peter always wanted to be near brave people.

That is not the same thing as being brave. We learned the difference much too late.

"Potter, James!"

"GRYFFINDOR!" The hat did not even sit down properly on his head. Of course it didn't. James ran to our table like it was his birthday, shook four people's hands on the way, and sat down between me and Sirius as if that place had been kept for him since the beginning of the world.

"Snape, Severus!"

The thin boy with the oily hair went to Slytherin. Lily's face fell. He looked at her from the green table, and she looked back at him from the red one, and between the two tables there was a bridge of sadness.

Remember that bridge. It falls down later, and when it falls, it takes several lives with it.

That night I lay in a warm bed in a round tower and could not sleep. Three boys were sleeping around me. I could hear them breathing.

*They can never know,* I told the ceiling. *I will be careful. I will be quiet. I will be nobody's friend, and then I will be safe.*

That was my whole plan. It lasted about six weeks.

## 4. Four Beds

Let me tell you about our room, because for seven years it was the best place in the world.

It was round, at the top of Gryffindor tower. There were four beds with red curtains around them, and one window that looked out at the mountains. The room was always a mess. James's clothes on the floor. Sirius's music papers on the table. Peter's sweet papers under the bed. My books everywhere.

In winter, ice flowers grew on the inside of the window. You could hear the wind singing round the tower all night.

For the first few weeks I kept my plan. I was quiet. I worked. I said "no, thank you" so many times that James started calling me No-Thank-You Lupin.

"Coming down to the lake, Remus?"

"No, thank you."

"Chess?"

"No, thank you."

"Would you like to help me put ink in Sirius's shoes?"

I looked up at that one. I should not have looked up. James saw me look up, and after that he never really let me go.

It is hard to say no to James Potter. Not because he pushed you. Because he *noticed* things.

He noticed that I never had money for sweets. Suddenly there were always extra sweets in our room, and somebody had to eat them, come on, Remus, help me, my mother sends far too many.

He noticed that I read at night. One evening there was a small lamp by my bed. Nobody said a word about it, then or ever. That was James: loud about jokes and quiet about kindness. His son is exactly the same, people tell me.

And Sirius was a fire. You could not sit near him and stay cold. He talked in class. He sang bad songs after ten o'clock. He made war on the school rules as if the rules had killed his dog. Professor McGonagall said his name so often — "Black! *Black!*" — that the first years thought it was a magic word.

Peter followed the two of them everywhere, one step behind, like a small boat behind two big ships. He was not funny himself. But he was the best laugher in the school, and every clown needs somebody who laughs.

And slowly, without asking my permission, they became my friends.

It happened through small doors.

Somebody had to help James with his star maps, because he could not tell one star from another and did not care. Somebody had to keep a second copy of Peter's notes, because Peter lost everything he owned, twice.

And somebody had to sit by the fire with Sirius on the evenings when a black-and-green letter came from his mother.

Those letters came about once a month. He never opened them. He held one at arm's length, looked at it, and put it in the fire. Then he sat and watched it burn with a face like a closed door.

The first time it happened I did not know what to do, so I did nothing. I just stayed in the chair beside him until the letter was ash. He did not say thank you. But the next time it happened, he came and sat down next to me first.

That is how boys do these things. Nobody says anything. Somebody just does not leave.

By December I had broken my whole plan. I was somebody's friend. I was three people's friend.

And so, of course, the problem came. The problem came every month, white and round, over the mountains.

The full moon.

## 5. My Secret

I should tell you what I am. The rest of the book does not work if you do not know.

When I was four years old, a werewolf bit me.

A werewolf is a person. That is the first thing people get wrong. For twenty-seven or twenty-eight days of the month, a werewolf is a man who buys bread and forgets his keys and is afraid of the dentist. Then the moon becomes full, and for one night he is not a man at all. He is a wolf with a man's memory taken out of him. He does not know his friends. He does not know his own name.

The man who bit me had a name: Fenrir Greyback. He hated my father, who worked at the Ministry and had said something true about him in public. So Greyback came to our house in the night and bit the son.

I was four. I remember a window, and a face, and then nothing for a long time.

After that, everything in my life had a moon in it.

My mother and father sold our house and moved. Then they moved again. Not because of the neighbours' questions — although there were questions — but because of the little wooden room my father had to build each time, with the heavy door and the strong lock, at the bottom of the garden.

That was where I spent one night in every month, from the age of four to the age of eleven.

I cannot tell you much about the nights themselves, because I do not remember them. That is the true thing about my illness that the books never say: the wolf remembers, the boy does not. I only know the mornings.

I woke up on a wooden floor. There was blood in my mouth — my own blood — and new cuts on my arms and my chest. There was pain everywhere, as if the whole world were made of broken glass. And there was my mother on the other side of the door, crying quietly, waiting until it was safe to come in.

A werewolf alone hurts himself. There is nobody else in the room to bite.

Now you understand the letter.

Children like me did not go to school. It was not a rule that anybody had written down; it was simpler than that. No school would take the risk, and no parent would send a child to a school that did. My parents had stopped talking about my future by the time I was ten. When people asked, they changed the subject the way you move a chair over a hole in the floor.

Then Albus Dumbledore came to our house and drank tea in our kitchen and said that I could come to Hogwarts.

I heard it from the top of the stairs. My mother did not believe him. She said the word "impossible" four times. And Dumbledore said — I have kept these words all my life, and I have needed them all my life — "He is a boy who has a problem once a month. He is not a problem who is sometimes a boy."

He had made a plan. In the summer before I came, the school planted a tree.

It was a huge, angry tree on the grounds near the lake, and it hit anybody who came close to it. Everybody in the school thought it was there because the school liked strange plants. It was there because of me. Under its roots there was a tunnel, and at the end of the tunnel there was an empty house in the village, with boards on the windows.

Every month, in the evening, Madam Pomfrey the school nurse walked me down to that tree. Every month she waited outside while I went down the tunnel. Every month she came back in the morning and carried me out.

Two days in the hospital wing after that. Then back to class, grey and slow, with my story ready.

*My mother is ill again. Yes, she is better now, thank you.*

I thought it was working. I thought they believed me.

I forgot that I was living with the three most curious boys in Britain.

## 6. Small Kindnesses

That first year had a shape. Three weeks of being happy, then one week of being afraid.

The happy weeks were better than anything I had known. I want to put some of them down here, because later in this book there are chapters where nobody is happy at all, and I would like you to have seen us at the beginning.

We ate too much at every meal. We learned to turn a match into a needle, and James did it first, and Peter did it last, in March. We went out on the frozen lake in January and Sirius fell through the ice and had to be pulled out by his hair, and he laughed so hard in the hospital wing that Madam Pomfrey put a spell on his mouth.

We were terrible at not being caught. We were caught almost every time. In our first year the four of us lost Gryffindor about two hundred points, and won them all back at the end of the year because James was good at every game the school played.

And there was Lily.

Lily Evans sat down next to me in the second week of October, when I was the quiet ill boy in the corner, and asked me what I thought about our homework.

That is all she did. She asked a monster his opinion about homework.

If you have never been eleven and sure that you are a monster, you cannot know what that does. She talked to me as if I were ordinary. She shared her sweets. She collected people the way other children collect cards — shy first years, homesick students, strange boys with no friends. I was one of her strays before I was anybody's friend at all.

She did not like James. That took me some time to understand, because James was the sun in our room and I could not imagine anybody looking at him and not liking the light. But Lily looked at him and saw a boy who was clever and rich and never punished, and she did not like what he did with all that.

She was right. I will come back to it.

The frightened weeks were the ones around the moon. Two days before, I stopped sleeping. My hands shook at meals. Every noise was too loud. The wolf came up through my body like water coming up through sand, slowly, for two days, and everybody thought I was worried about a test.

That was the year I learned how much work a lie is.

You cannot tell one lie. A lie needs feeding. Every lie needs a small brother to explain it and a small sister to hold it up. *My mother is ill.* Then: which parent came to collect me? Nobody. Then: why do you never get letters from home in those weeks? Because. Then: why did Madam Pomfrey say "again, dear" in that voice?

I lay in bed making my lies fit together, and in the next bed James was asleep with his glasses still on his face.

And here is the thing I did not think about, not once, not for a second, in all those months. While I was building my careful little house of lies, three eleven-year-old boys were sitting in the same room every day, watching me build it.

They were not stupid. Sirius was the cleverest boy in our year, whatever his marks said. James noticed everything. And Peter — I know how this sounds now, but it is true and I will write it — Peter noticed the small things that the other two missed. He had spent his whole life watching bigger people to see if they were angry. He was very good at it.

The lamp arrived by my bed in November.

I have not told you what happened with the lamp. I came upstairs one night and it was there: a small brass lamp with a green glass shade, exactly the kind you need for reading in a bed with the curtains closed. There was no note.

I looked at James. James was reading a Quidditch magazine upside down.

"Somebody must have left it," he said, without looking up.

"Somebody."

"Yes. The castle is full of them."

I said nothing else, and neither did he, and I used that lamp for seven years. I have it now. I am writing this book by it.

## 7. The Boy Who Counted

It was Sirius who started counting.

Second year, October. I came back from "visiting my mother", grey as old snow, and found the three of them sitting on James's bed with a piece of paper.

They stopped talking when I came in.

You know that silence. You walk into a room and the air is still warm from words about you.

"How is your mother?" said Sirius. His voice was too light.

"Better, thank you."

"That's good." He was not looking at the paper. He was looking at me. "That's the fourth time she has been ill this year. Poor woman. And always so exactly ill. September, October — every month, Remus. Every single month."

My hands went cold.

"That's not — sometimes it's my grandmother —"

"And the funny thing," Sirius went on, in the same light voice, "the funny thing is the moon. James. Tell him about the moon."

James looked unhappy. He was holding the paper as if it were burning his fingers. I could see my own name on it, and a line of dates, and small drawings of circles.

Circles. Moons.

"It's just —" James stopped. "Remus, we were worried. You always come back hurt. We thought maybe somebody at home was hurting you. So we wrote down the days. And then Peter said — Peter noticed —"

Peter. Little Peter with his round soft face. He looked at me and then looked quickly down at his own hands.

"I noticed the moon was always full," he said to his hands. "My mum has a calendar with the moons on it. In the kitchen."

The room was very, very quiet. Down in the common room somebody laughed at something. Far away, in another world, where nobody's life was ending.

You must understand what was standing in front of me at that moment. It was not three curious boys. It was my whole life.

Werewolf children did not go to school. Werewolf adults did not get work. There were laws — real laws, written down by real men at the Ministry — about "the control of dangerous half-humans".

Dangerous half-humans. That was me at twelve: a dangerous half-human with a reading lamp beside his bed.

If they told anybody, I was finished. Not "in trouble". Finished. I would be on a train home in two days and there would be no other school, ever, for the rest of my life.

So I did the thing I had planned in my head a hundred times for exactly this moment.

I lied.

I laughed — I still remember how bad it sounded, like a cough — and said that the moon was nothing, that the dates were nothing, that Peter's mother's calendar was a funny thing to keep in a kitchen. And I felt the lie die in the air in the middle of the room.

Sirius watched me until I stopped talking.

He was twelve years old, but that look was a hundred years old. It was a look from a house where everybody lies beautifully at dinner every single night, and every child learns the sound of it before he learns to read.

"Remus," he said quietly. "Stop. We know. We have known for a week."

He waited. Then he said it.

"You are a werewolf."

And there it was. The word. Out of my head and into the air, in my own room, in front of my three —

I could not even think the word "friends" any more. Because now it was finished, and in a moment one of them would go quietly down the stairs to a teacher, and I would spend the rest of my life on the other side of that little wooden door at the bottom of my father's garden.

I closed my eyes.

"We think," said James, "that it is completely brilliant."

## 8. We Think It Is Brilliant

I opened my eyes.

"Brilliant?" I said. "It is — what?"

"Well, it's terrible," said James quickly. "The pain and all of that, obviously. That part is terrible. But Remus —"

He stood up. He always stood up when he was excited, and he was always excited.

"You turn into a *wolf* every month. Every month. And then you come back to class two days later and get better marks than all of us. That is the hardest thing I have ever heard of anybody doing."

He thought for a moment.

"Also," he said, "it explains why you always know when the sausages at dinner are old. Wolf nose."

Peter laughed. Sirius laughed.

And I stood in the middle of our round room and started to cry, and could not stop.

Nobody made a joke about it. Sirius took my arm and sat me down on the bed like an old woman. Peter pushed his sweets into my hand — his own sweets, the last ones from the box, and if you knew Peter, you know exactly what that meant.

James sat down on the floor with his back against the bed and waited.

It took me a while.

"You can't tell anyone," I said, when I could talk. "Please. Not anyone, ever. Not your parents. If the school parents find out that there's a werewolf sleeping in the tower, they'll —"

"Tell anyone?" Sirius looked truly insulted. "Remus. We are your friends. Your secret is our secret now."

*Your secret is our secret now.*

I have turned those words over in my hands for forty years, the way you turn a stone you found on a beach.

Sirius kept them all his life, through things you cannot imagine. James kept them until his last night in this world. And Peter — Peter kept them too, and kept them, and kept them, until the one day when a secret was the only money he had left to buy his life with.

But that was later. That night I only cried, and ate Peter's sweets, and was, for the first time since I was four years old, not alone.

Then they asked questions. Of course they did. They asked questions for two hours, and I answered every one, because after twelve years of saying nothing it was like putting down a bag I had carried up a hill.

Did it hurt? Yes.

How much? A lot.

Did I remember it? No.

Was I dangerous to them? Yes. Very. That one I said twice, and slowly, and I told them about the empty house and the boards on the windows and why the door had to be locked, and I watched James's face while I said it, because I was still afraid of the moment when a friend hears the true size of it and quietly steps back.

James did not step back. James asked what the house was like inside.

"Cold," I said. "There's a bed. I break the bed most months."

"Every month?"

"Most months."

"And you're alone in there. All night."

"That's the whole idea, James."

He did not say anything else about it that night. He got up, and said something rude about Peter's socks, and everybody went to bed.

But I was awake for a long time in the dark, and so was he. I could tell. There is a way a person breathes when he is looking at the ceiling and thinking hard about a problem.

I know now what he was doing over there. He was not feeling sorry for me. James Potter did not feel sorry for people; he thought it was a low kind of feeling, one step away from looking down at them.

He was already working.

## 9. A Book in the Library

Now I must explain something about my three friends, because what they did next is not a normal thing.

A normal friend, when he learns about your illness, is kind to you. He asks how you feel. He is a little careful around you, as if you were made of thin glass. And then slowly — not because he is a bad person, but because it is heavy — he stands a little further away.

My friends were not normal.

For them, my illness was not my problem. It was *our* problem. And James Potter did not believe that "our problems" existed. He only believed in our projects.

It began a few weeks later, in the library.

James dropped a huge old book on the table in front of me. Dust flew up like smoke and the librarian looked at us over her glasses.

"Animagus," he said.

"Keep your voice down," I said. "And — what?"

"An-i-ma-gus." He said it slowly, as if I were old and could not hear well. "A wizard who can turn into an animal. When he wants to. And back again. Professor McGonagall is one. She turns into a cat. You have seen her do it."

"I know what an Animagus is, James. Why is it on my table?"

He leaned over the book. His glasses were shining. Sirius sat down on my left. Peter sat down on my right. I was surrounded.

"Because a werewolf," James said quietly, "is only dangerous to people. A wolf does not hurt animals. We read it in three books. If we were animals — real animals, big ones — we could be *with you*. On the nights. In that house. You would not be alone any more."

I want you to feel the whole weight of this idea, so I am going to put it in plain words.

Becoming an Animagus is one of the hardest things in magic. Grown wizards try it and fail, and some of them are hurt for the rest of their lives. There is a famous story about a witch who lived thirty years with a cat's tail.

It takes years of work.

And doing it in secret, with no teacher, is against the law. You have to write your name in a book at the Ministry. If you do not, and they find you, that is prison. Not a fine. Prison.

Three boys of twelve and thirteen decided to do it in secret, with no teacher, against the law. For me. So that I would not be alone at the full moon.

"No," I said. "No, no, and no. It is far too dangerous. You will hurt yourselves. And if they catch you, it is prison, James — real prison — and it will be because of me."

"Nobody will catch us," said Sirius, and yawned.

"You cannot know that!"

"We're quite hard to catch," said Sirius. "We have a lot of practice."

"Peter." I turned to him, because Peter was the one who was afraid of things, and I thought he would help me. "Peter, tell them."

Peter looked at the table. He was very white.

"I said all that," he said. "This morning. I said all of it."

"And?"

"And James said we could do it." He looked up, and there was something in his face I did not understand then and understand very well now. "James thinks I can do it."

That was the whole answer. That was the answer to almost everything about Peter Pettigrew, and it took me twenty years to hear it properly.

*James thinks I can do it.*

The librarian came and told us to be quiet or leave. We left. And out in the corridor, in the cold, James stopped and turned round and asked me one question.

## 10. Does It Hurt?

"One question," said James. "Then I'll shut up about it."

He took off his glasses. He did that perhaps once a year, when he was serious, and it always made him look younger and stranger.

"On those nights," he said. "Does it hurt?"

The corridor was cold and empty. Somewhere below us a door closed. Peter was watching me with big eyes. Sirius was leaning on the wall, and for once he was completely still.

I could have lied. I was very good at it by then. I had a whole warm little house of lies and I had lived in it since I was four.

"Yes," I said. My voice came out very small. "It hurts."

"Then we're doing it," said James.

He put his glasses back on, and the talk was over.

They started that same week.

I said no for about a month more. I said no in the room, and in the library, and once in the middle of a Potions class, in a whisper, while Sirius was trying to look up a word in a book he had hidden under the table.

Then I stopped saying it.

I am going to be honest about why, because this book is not worth writing if I am only honest about other people.

I stopped because some deep, hungry part of me did not want them to stop.

All my life I had been a closed door. And here were three people trying to break the door down, not to get anything from the room, but only so that I would not have to sit in the dark by myself. You try saying no to that. You try saying no to that when you are thirteen years old.

So instead of saying no, I helped.

I was the best reader of the four of us, so I found the books. That was my part, and I did it well, and everything I read frightened me.

The work took years, the books said. You had to hold a leaf in your mouth for a whole month — a particular leaf, in a particular month, and if it touched water the month was wasted. You had to say words at the right moon. You had to change your own body with nothing but your will.

And if you made one mistake — one moment of fear at the wrong second — the books had pictures of what happened.

I did not show those pictures to Peter. Peter was frightened enough.

That is a small thing, but please write it down somewhere in your head, because it matters at the end: from the very beginning, Peter was frightened.

He wanted to stop in the third year. He wanted to stop again in the fourth. And every time, James put a hand on his shoulder and said the same sentence.

"You can do it, Pete. We're not going without you."

And Peter stayed. Not because he believed in himself. Because James believed in him.

There is a kind of courage that a person owns, and there is a kind that a person borrows. They look exactly the same from outside. You cannot see the difference across a room, or across a school year, or even across ten years of friendship.

You see the difference on the day the lender is not there any more.

Peter's whole life was built on borrowed courage. And when the man who lent it to him died in a hallway in Godric's Hollow, the debt came due, and Peter had nothing in his hands at all.

But I am running ahead again. Forgive me. Old men do that, and old men telling this particular story do it worst of all.

Let me go back to the good years. There are two more of them, and I would like to stay in them a little longer.

## 11. Three Years of Trying

It took them three years.

Three years of stolen nights in the library. Three years of strange leaves under the tongue — do not ask me about the leaves, the whole business is disgusting — and secret words said at the right moon, and small failures we laughed about, and big failures we did not.

Here is what three years of it looked like from my chair.

Sirius walked around for a week in June with grey dog hair on the backs of his hands. He wore gloves and told the teachers he was cold. Professor McGonagall looked at him for a long time and said nothing, and I have wondered ever since how much that woman knew and decided not to know.

James's eyes turned brown-gold for two days in our fourth year. He told everybody he had eaten a bad sweet. Half the school believed him, because half the school always believed James.

Peter cried in the bathroom.

That is the picture from those years that I cannot put down. Third year, near the end. I went to find him because he had missed dinner, and he was sitting on the floor of the bathroom on the fourth floor with his back against the wall.

His hands would not change. Not at all. Not one finger, not for a second, while the other two were already halfway there.

"Maybe I'm not strong enough," he said. "Maybe you should all just go on without me."

"Don't be stupid," said Sirius from the door. He had come to find him too. He said "stupid" to Peter in a way he never said it to anybody else: kindly, like an older brother.

"It isn't strength," Sirius said. "It's wanting. You have to want it with your whole body. What do you want most, Pete? In the whole world?"

Peter was quiet for a strange, long moment.

"To be with you three," he said at last. "Always."

Well. He got that wish, more or less.

Be careful with wishes.

I helped where I could. I read, and I checked their words, and once — I am not proud of this — I stole a book from Professor Slughorn's office because it had the last part of the process in it and the library's copy had been taken away.

And every month, while they worked, the moon came, and I went down the tunnel alone, and came back two days later with new cuts, and they looked at the new cuts and said nothing and worked harder.

That is a thing about those three that I did not understand until I was much older. They never once talked about how long it was taking. Nobody ever said "this is a year now" or "we said last summer we would be finished". They just went on.

Boys are not famous for finishing things. These boys finished this.

And here is the part I think about at four in the morning, when the moon is coming and I cannot sleep.

They did not have to.

Nothing would have happened to them if they had stopped. I would never have said one word about it. There was no promise written down anywhere, no ceremony, nothing but a conversation in a cold corridor with James's glasses in his hand.

They were thirteen, and then fourteen, and then fifteen. They broke the law for three years, in secret, at night, after their homework, for one friend who would have forgiven them completely if they had given up in the first month.

Then, one night in October in our fifth year, Peter woke me up.

## 12. Three Animals

"Moony," said Peter. "Moony, wake up. Come downstairs."

It was after midnight. Our common room was empty and dark red. The fire had gone down to coals. The three of them stood in a line in their night clothes, and James had a huge and stupid smile on his face.

"Watch," he said.

And my three friends folded up like paper birds.

I do not know how else to say it. They went down, and smaller, or bigger, and *changed*, and where three boys had been standing in the common room of Gryffindor tower there now stood:

A stag. A big grey dog. And a rat.

I sat down on the stairs. My legs simply stopped working.

The stag was James. It was tall and impossible, with young antlers, and it came towards me on careful thin legs. Its eyes had two round patches of light in them, in exactly the place where his glasses would have been. I swear that is true. I have never told anybody, because nobody would believe me.

The dog was Sirius, of course it was Sirius: big and grey and loud even when it was silent. It knocked me flat on the stairs and licked my whole face. Even as a dog he had no respect at all for anybody's personal space.

And the rat sat small and still by the fire, with its nose moving fast, watching us.

A rat. Of the three of them, Peter's animal was a rat.

Now listen to me, because this part matters.

In our world people tell children that your animal shows your true self. So it is easy — it is so terribly easy — to say now: *of course. A rat. We should have known. Rats run. Rats hide. Rats leave a sinking ship.*

But on that night, the rat was the bravest thing in the room, and I will defend that sentence against anybody.

Little Peter Pettigrew, who was afraid of loud doors and deep water, had done one of the hardest things in magic. Three years. No teacher. Breaking the law every single day. And he had done it last of the three, alone at the end, with nobody left to fail beside him.

The rat sat by the fire and its whiskers shook. I picked Peter up in my two hands and told him he was a marvel.

He was, that night.

Both things are true, you see. That is the thing nobody tells you about traitors. They are not born on the day they betray you. Before that day they are your friend — really, truly your friend, warm at your fire, brave with borrowed courage.

If Peter had died at fifteen, he would have died a hero. I would have cried at his grave for the bravest small boy I ever knew, and I would have meant every tear.

He did not die at fifteen.

We sat by the coals until the sky went grey. They changed and changed back, again and again, laughing into their hands so that the tower would not wake up. Sirius chased his own tail on purpose, to make Peter laugh, and it worked, and then he could not stop, and we had to hold him.

Near dawn, James gave everybody their new names.

James named everything. It was his way of loving the world.

## 13. Four Names

"Right," said James. "Names."

It was about four in the morning. Nobody was going to sleep now. Peter had made tea in the fireplace, badly, and we were drinking it out of the wrong cups.

"Why do we need names?" said Peter.

"Because if we shout 'James, look out' in the forest," said James, "then somebody in the village hears the word 'James'. And there is only one James in this school with a face like mine."

"So we need animal names," said Sirius. "Fine. I want something good."

"You get what you're given."

James stood on the table. He was still in his night clothes and his hair was worse than usual, and he pointed at Sirius with a teaspoon.

"You," he said, "are a big grey dog with feet like soft cushions. You go past a sleeping teacher and nobody hears you. You are *Padfoot*."

Sirius thought about it.

"Padfoot," he said. "I like it. It sounds like a criminal."

"You are a criminal."

"I know. That's why I like it."

"Peter." James turned round on the table. "You have a long thin tail like a worm. You are Wormtail."

Peter's face fell about an inch, and he laughed to cover it, and said that it was a horrible name and that he loved it.

I have thought about that inch of face, on and off, for many years. It was a boy's joke among boys' jokes, and the other two got the names of large brave animals, and he got the worm. He never once complained. But I think it went in, the way small things do, and stayed.

"And you," said James, and pointed the teaspoon at me. "You are Moony. Because — well. Obviously."

"Obviously," I said.

"And I," said James, "am Prongs."

"Prongs?" said Sirius. "Because of the antlers?"

"Because of the antlers."

"You gave yourself the best one."

"I gave myself the *accurate* one," said James, and fell off the table.

Moony. Wormtail. Padfoot. Prongs.

Four stupid names, invented at four in the morning by four boys who loved each other and had drunk too much bad tea.

If you have ever held the Marauder's Map in your hands, you have read those names across the top of it in green ink. Now you know where they came from, and now you know something else too: that map is signed by two dead men, a traitor, and me.

But that October night nobody was dead, and nobody was a traitor, and there were four cups on the fireplace, and outside the window the sky was going white over the mountains.

I want to stop here for a moment. Please let me.

When you read a story like this one, you read it forwards, and you know the ending, so every happy scene has a shadow on it. I understand that. I put the shadow there myself, in the first chapter, on purpose.

But we did not live it forwards. We lived it now, and now, and now.

On that morning, the four of us were fifteen years old. We had done an impossible thing. We were going to be together for ever, and we knew it the way you know your own name, and not one of us had any reason at all to think otherwise.

I am the only one left who remembers that morning. So I am telling you: it was real, and it was good, and it was not a lie just because of what came after.

Nothing that happened later reached back and took that morning away. That is not how time works, whatever grief tells you at three o'clock in the morning.

Two weeks later, the moon came up over the mountains, and for the first time in my life I did not go into the dark alone.

## 14. The Night We Ran

How can I explain the nights to you?

Here is what a full moon was, before.

Evening. Goodbye, I am going to see my mother. The walk down to the angry tree with Madam Pomfrey's hand on my back. The tunnel. The cold house with boards on the windows.

Then waiting. The waiting was the worst part. I sat on the broken bed and watched the sky go silver through the cracks in the boards, and felt the wolf come up through my bones like water coming up through sand.

Then nothing. Blackness.

Then morning. Floor, blood, pain, the smell of old dust, my own hands coming back to me one finger at a time. Two days in bed. And then the same thing again in twenty-nine days, for ever, until I died.

Now here is a full moon, after.

The wolf comes up, and the world does not go black. The world goes *simple*.

Because there is a stag standing in that room, and a huge grey dog, and the smell of them is not the smell of people. The dark voice inside a wolf has one word for people, and the word is *food*. But these were not people. These were *pack*.

I cannot tell you how the wolf knew. But it knew. These are mine.

And then the door opened.

They had planned it like a small war, my three friends. Everything had a reason.

Padfoot could hold the wolf back if a night went wrong, because a big dog speaks a language a wolf understands, and because he was strong.

Prongs could push the heavy tunnel door open with his antlers and carry half the forest on his back.

And Wormtail was the key, in the most exact way.

You remember the angry tree that hit everybody who came near it. There was a small root at the bottom of it, and if you pressed that root, the tree went still. A boy could never get close enough to press it. A rat could.

Remember that too, please. For years, Peter was the one who opened the way. Every single month, everything began with Peter going quietly through the dark grass and pressing a secret place that nobody else could reach.

We built him into our lives as exactly that.

And so, at fifteen, I ran.

We ran the school grounds under the moon. We ran the forest, where the trees are a thousand years old and some of the paths shine in the dark. We ran to the village and up the hills behind it: the dog and the wolf shoulder to shoulder, the stag ahead of us like a king, and the rat riding between the antlers like a small mad captain.

I woke up in the mornings tired and whole. No new cuts. No blood on the floor. Grass on my clothes, and once, a memory — a real memory, my own, not the wolf's — of the four of us standing on a hilltop at three in the morning, looking down at the sleeping lights of the village.

The wolf lifted its head and sent a long cry into the sky. Not a cry of pain, for once. Just a voice, saying: here I am. Alive.

Madam Pomfrey could not understand why I was suddenly so well.

"These visits home are good for you," she said.

I nodded. I was still very good at nodding.

Was it dangerous?

Say it plainly, Remus. You owe the truth to this page.

Yes. It was dangerous. A werewolf ran the grounds of a school full of sleeping children, and three boys of fifteen believed they could control it. We were lucky. Month after month, we were lucky. And luck is not a plan.

Once — one time — the wolf caught a smell from the village and turned that way, and Padfoot stood in front of it. There are marks on Sirius from that night that stayed on him for the rest of his life. He called them his favourite marks.

"Got them dancing," he told people, and winked at me across the room.

## 15. The Castle at Night

If you take one thing from this book, take this: we knew that castle better than anybody has ever known it, before or after.

Seven years of walking around at night will do that.

We knew the corridor that was only there on Thursdays. We knew the mirror on the fourth floor with a passage behind it. We knew the statue of the one-eyed witch, and the tunnel under her that comes out in the sweet shop in the village, and we used it about four hundred times.

We knew the room that becomes whatever you need. Peter found that one. He was hungry and he was looking for cake, and he walked past the same piece of wall three times, and a door appeared, and behind it there was a room full of cake. He came back to the tower crying with happiness.

We knew the kitchens, and the little creatures who work there, who fed us at any hour of the night and were very happy to be asked.

And we knew the roofs. Sirius knew the roofs *especially*. He liked high places, our dog. He said that in his parents' house every window looked down at other people, and he was finished with looking down at anybody.

I will give you one night as an example, because a list of places is not a memory.

March, our sixth year. Two o'clock in the morning. We were on the roof above the astronomy tower, which is the highest place a student can get to and stay alive.

Peter had brought food. Peter always brought food. James had brought a blanket, because James noticed things, and because Peter got cold.

Sirius lay on his back on the slates with his hands behind his head and looked at the stars, and said:

"There it is. That's mine."

"What's yours?" said Peter.

"That star. The bright one. It's called Sirius."

"You're joking."

"Black family. We're all named after stars." He said it lightly, but he did not take his eyes off it. "Every single one of us. My brother, my cousins. It's a family of people named after things that are very far away and very cold."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Is it the brightest?" said Peter.

"Brightest in the sky," said Sirius. "Yes."

"Well, then," said James, from under the blanket. "They got that bit right."

That is all that happened. Four boys, a roof, a cold night, one sentence from James Potter that took about two seconds to say.

I watched Sirius's face when he said it. Sirius did not answer, and he did not laugh, and he went on looking at his star.

I have been in rooms with famous wizards. I have heard men make speeches that made grown people cry. I have never in my life seen anybody give another person more with fewer words than James gave Sirius on that roof, and he did not even know he had done it. He was already asking Peter for the rest of the bread.

We went down at four in the morning and got caught by the caretaker, Mr Filch, on the fifth floor, and got three weeks of punishment each.

Worth it. Every hour of it.

Those years are why I can write this book at all, you know. Whatever else the four of us did, we had those roofs. When people say to me now — and they do — *how can you speak about them without hatred* — I think about a cold roof and a boy who was named after a star.

## 16. The Map

By our sixth year we knew too much for four heads.

Facts were falling out of us. Peter forgot which stairs disappear on Fridays and spent an hour standing inside a wall. James could not remember whether the passage behind the mirror came out above the kitchens or below them, and we lost a whole night finding out.

So James said the words that changed everything.

"We need a map."

It took us a year to make it. And I will say this calmly, as a man who later taught in that school: it was the finest piece of magic our year produced. Better than anything we did in class. Better than most things the teachers could do.

A piece of parchment that shows the whole castle. Every room. Every passage. Every staircase, including the ones that move.

And every person.

That was the part nobody had done before. Little moving footprints, each with a name beside it, walking along the corridors in real time. Teachers. Students. Ghosts. Cats. The caretaker. Nobody hides from it. Nobody.

Each of us gave it something.

James gave it the magic that made it see. His magic was the engine of everything we ever built; he could do things with a wand at sixteen that grown men could not do at forty, and he was lazy about all of it.

I gave it order. I worked out how a castle with a hundred floors folds onto one page, and how the page opens and closes, and I wrote the rude message it shouts at any stranger who tries to open it. I was the politest of the four of us. That is exactly why my insults were the best.

Sirius gave it its character. The map talks back to people. It argues. It is proud of itself and it has no manners at all, and if you have ever met it you have met Sirius Black.

And Peter gave it the hours.

I want that written down properly. Peter drew eight hundred doors. He checked every staircase twice and wrote down what it did on which day. He sat in the same chair evening after evening, doing the part of the work that has no glory in it and never once complained, because we were doing it together, and together was the only place he was ever happy.

Then, on the front, in green ink, we wrote the words that I can still see with my eyes closed.

*Messrs Moony, Wormtail, Padfoot and Prongs are proud to present THE MARAUDER'S MAP.*

The Marauders. That is where the name comes from. It means something like night robbers, and we called ourselves that as a joke: four gentlemen thieves of a sleeping castle.

Nobody outside our room ever called us it. It was a small country with four citizens.

And I must tell you honestly what we used the most powerful watching machine in the school's history for.

Nothing important.

Stealing food. Avoiding teachers. Finding out which corridor a certain girl was walking down — that was mostly James, watching one name, *Lily Evans*, moving through the castle like a small green star.

We made a machine that could see every person in that castle, and we used it to carry cake.

But a tool does not stay with the people who made it. Filch took the map from us in our seventh year and locked it in his office, and years later it found its way into the hands of James's son.

And, my God, the things that map watched happen.

It watched a dead man's name walk the corridors at night. A name that could not possibly be there. It showed the truth to anybody who looked at it, month after month, and for a long time nobody understood what it was saying.

The map never lies. We built it that way, four honest boys, sixteen years old.

It never once came into our heads that one day one of our own names would be the lie.

## 17. Severus Snape

I have to bring in Severus Snape now, and I have to be fair to him, and it will not be comfortable.

You met him on the platform: the thin boy in old clothes, Lily's friend from her own street. She had known him since she was nine. He was the one who told her what she was — the first person who ever said the word "witch" to her and meant it kindly.

He came to Hogwarts already knowing more dark magic than most seventh years. He also came to Hogwarts in clothes that had been washed too many times, and with hair that he never seemed to be able to fix, and a way of walking that said *do not look at me*, which is of course an order that every school in the world obeys backwards.

James hated him from the first week. Sirius hated him harder.

Understand that this was not a small school quarrel. It was real, and it was ugly, and — I am being honest at last, which is the whole point of this book — it was not equal.

James and Sirius were golden. Everybody loved them. They were clever, they were funny, they were good at the game everybody cared about, and they had never once in their lives been afraid of a teacher.

Snape was poor, strange and alone, and they were cruel to him.

Yes, he gave back everything he could. Yes, he was inventing curses at fifteen that grown men would not use. Yes, he walked with a group of Slytherin boys who used the worst word we have — *mudblood*, which means dirty blood, and is what those people call witches and wizards whose parents have no magic. Like Lily.

All of that is true. And it is still true that two against one is two against one, and that half the school laughing is half the school laughing.

I was a prefect from our fifth year. I had a badge. My job, in actual words written in an actual book, was to stop exactly this.

I sat nearby with a book and said nothing.

That is my kind of cowardice, and I want it on the page early, in a quiet chapter, so that you can watch it work later, when it costs more than a boy's dignity.

Snape hated James and Sirius. But me — me he *studied*.

He was the only person in that school who was really looking at me. Every month I disappeared. Every month my three friends lied for me a little too easily, and he could hear it; a person who lives inside his own watchfulness hears when a lie is well practised.

So he began to follow. He watched the hospital wing. He watched the grounds in the evening. He counted the days, the way Sirius had once counted them.

But Sirius had counted out of love. Snape counted the way you sharpen a knife.

Once, in our sixth year, he said something to me in a corridor. Nothing violent. He walked past me two days before the moon, and I looked as I always looked, and he said, quite quietly:

"You should ask them for a better story, Lupin. That one is worn out."

Then he went on to his class.

I stood there and my heart went so fast that I had to put a hand on the wall.

I did not tell the others. I have asked myself for forty years why not. I think it was because telling them would have made it real, and because I was afraid of what Sirius would do if he knew that Snape was circling me.

I was right to be afraid of that. I was simply too late.

Two weeks later Sirius did the worst thing he ever did in his life, and he did it in about five seconds, with one sentence, in a corridor much like that one.

## 18. The Worst Night

I have been putting this chapter off. You can probably feel it.

But this book is about how trust breaks, and the first crack came long before the war. It came from Sirius, in our sixth year, and it very nearly came through me.

I need you to understand what Sirius was that year.

He was sixteen. He was beautiful and angry. Things at home had become the kind of bad that ends with a boy leaving for ever — that is the next chapter but one, and it happened four months after this. He was carrying a black anger around the castle and it was looking for a door.

And Snape, in a corridor, was a door.

It was the day before the full moon. I was already grey and slow and my hands were shaking, and I had gone up to bed early. So I was not there. Everything I know about the next ten minutes I know from three people who were.

Snape came at Sirius about me. Where does Lupin go, Black? Every month. What are the four of you hiding? He was smiling. He thought he was close to something, and he was right.

And Sirius told him.

"You want to know where he goes?" he said. "There's a root at the bottom of the big tree by the lake. Press it with a long stick and the tree goes still. Then go down the tunnel and see for yourself."

That is all it takes. One sentence.

One tired, angry, showing-off sentence in a corridor, and a boy walks into a tunnel. At the end of that tunnel there is no Remus Lupin. There is a full-grown werewolf in a locked house on the night of the moon.

And then the boy is dead. Or worse: bitten, changed, given my life instead of his own.

And the werewolf that did it is killed, because that is the law, and there is nothing in the law about the werewolf being sixteen and asleep in his bed two hours earlier. And the school closes. And every door that Albus Dumbledore had ever opened for people like me slams shut for a hundred years, and stays shut, because of one night and one dead boy.

Snape went to the tree that evening. He pressed the root. He went in.

And James went in after him.

He heard what Sirius had done — Peter told him in the common room, with a white face — and he did not even stop to shout at Sirius. He ran. Down the black tunnel, sixteen years old, no plan, nobody coming to help.

He caught Snape at the end of it and pulled him back.

They both saw me. The wolf came for them both and very nearly had them.

I woke up on the wooden floor in the morning, as always. Pain, as always. Blood, as always.

And Dumbledore was sitting on the broken stairs, waiting for me. He had come himself, and he was sitting in that filthy house in his fine robes, and his face told me before he said one word.

I will not dress up what I felt. There is no sentence fine enough to make it better, so here it is plainly.

For one night, without knowing it and without agreeing to it, I had been a loaded gun in a schoolboys' quarrel.

Everything I had been afraid of about myself since I was four years old had nearly come true. Not because I was careless. I was never careless. It nearly came true because my best friend opened his mouth in a corridor.

You can be careful your whole life. And somebody who loves you can still spend you like a coin.

## 19. What Stayed After

Dumbledore made it all go away.

I do not mean that unkindly. He did what a headmaster had to do. Snape was ordered to say nothing, ever, to anybody. James was told that he had done a brave thing. The school never heard one word about it.

And there was no punishment for Sirius that anybody outside that office ever saw.

I did not speak to him for five weeks.

I want to tell you about those five weeks, because in every other version of this story they are one line, and I lived in them.

We slept in the same round room. Four beds. I could hear him breathing at night. In the morning we walked to the same classes and sat at the same table and I passed him the salt and did not look at his face.

He apologised. Sirius Black, who apologised to nobody in this world, apologised to me over and over, in corridors, on stairs, once in the middle of a lesson in a note that Professor Flitwick read out to everybody by accident.

He was white and he did not sleep and he lost half a stone.

And I believe, to the end of my days, that he meant every word of it. He had not wanted Snape dead. He had not wanted me to become a murderer at sixteen.

He simply had not *thought*.

Here is the terrible truth of it, and it is easy to believe, and I ask you to hold it carefully, because it explains everything that happens on Halloween five years later.

It did not come into his head that I was in the trick at all.

He was a boy from a cruel house playing a cruel joke on a boy he hated. In his mind there was a tree, and a tunnel, and Snape's frightened face at the end of it. There was no room in that picture for me. That the wolf in the house *was Remus*. That his joke had my body inside it.

James forgave him first, and fastest, and completely, because James's love did not have any locks on it. That was James's greatest quality and his worst, and both of those come back in this book, in a hallway, with a wand left on a sofa.

Peter did not know what to do. Peter never knew what to do when the big people were fighting. He carried messages between us for a week, badly, and made everything slightly worse, and cried once.

And in the end, of course, I forgave him too.

Do not imagine some cold friendship after that. It was not cold. It was seven years of warmth with one splinter in it, and the warmth was real. We went back to the roofs. We ran under the moon the very next month, all four of us, and I put my head against the big grey dog's shoulder in the dark and did not think about the corridor at all.

But something had gone into me that night and it did not come out.

I will say it in the plainest words I have, because it is the load the last third of this book rests on:

*One of us can do it.*

One of us can, in one careless, angry moment, put another one of us into the fire.

Five years later, in the war, the whispers started. There is a traitor among your friends. Somebody close to you is selling us.

And every one of us lay awake at night and quietly weighed the other three. I did my weighing too. God forgive me, I did.

And the scales in my head barely had to move, because there was already a name on them.

Not Peter. Nobody ever weighed Peter.

Sirius had put his own name on those scales at sixteen, with one sentence in a corridor. And in the end that old splinter helped to kill us all.

## 20. Lily Evans

Enough darkness for a moment. Let me tell you about Lily.

Without her there is no story. No marriage, no Harry, no reason for that last house on that last street. And besides, remembering her is good for me. Some people are lamps. Thirty years later the light still works.

Lily Evans was Muggle-born. Her parents had no magic at all; they were dentists, kind and completely lost, and they came to the platform every September looking as if they had walked into somebody else's dream.

She was the best in our year at Charms and probably the best at everything else. She laughed loudly and argued louder. She could not walk past somebody sitting alone.

And James loved her.

From the beginning. I want to say from the train, honestly, from before he knew one single thing about her except the colour of her hair.

Here is the part that the pretty version of this story leaves out: for five years she could not stand him.

And she was right.

Say it plainly, Remus. At fifteen, James Potter was the kind of boy who threw a curse at somebody in a corridor because he was bored, and called it funny, and everybody laughed because he was handsome and because he was brilliant on a broom.

He showed off in front of Lily the way he showed off in front of everybody, only louder. And Lily looked straight through the shine at what was underneath it — mostly at how he treated Snape, her oldest friend — and told him, in front of people, more than once, exactly what she saw.

"You are a bully, Potter. You are an arrogant, showing-off bully. I would not go out with you if the only other choice were the giant squid in the lake."

The whole courtyard heard that one. Sirius laughed about it for a week.

James laughed too, in public.

That night in the dormitory he lay on his bed with his glasses off, throwing a small ball at the ceiling and catching it. Throw. Catch. Throw. Catch. For about two hours.

Peter fell asleep. Sirius fell asleep.

At last I said, into the dark:

"She isn't wrong, you know."

The ball stopped.

There was a long silence. Then James's voice, quiet, with no audience anywhere in it:

"I know."

That is the sentence people miss about James Potter.

He knew. And then — and this is rarer than any magic on our map — he set about becoming somebody else.

Not overnight. There is no spell for it. But from the end of our fifth year into our sixth, the corridor curses stopped. The showing-off went quiet. He took his one real gift — noticing people, which was always better magic than anything he did with a wand — and he began pointing it at people who were not himself.

He started leading instead of performing. By our seventh year the younger students came to him with their problems in the same way they went to Lily.

And the terrible thing, the thing that ended a friendship older than ours, is that in exactly those years Snape went the other way.

The same time that pulled James towards the light pulled Severus down: deeper into his crowd, deeper into the dark magic he loved, further from the girl from his own street.

Two boys who hated each other, walking past each other in opposite directions, at the age of sixteen. Nobody in that castle noticed it happening except one red-haired girl, who was watching both of them, and could not stop either.

## 21. One Word

It happened in June of our fifth year, by the lake, after an exam.

I was there. I was sitting under a tree with a book, which is where I was for most of the important moments of my life.

James was bored. That is the whole beginning of it: a hot afternoon, an easy exam finished, four hundred students on the grass, and James Potter with nothing to do and an audience.

He saw Snape walking past with his bag.

What happened next took about four minutes and I could tell you every second of it, and I am not going to. It has been written about elsewhere and I have no wish to put it on paper again. Two boys against one. A crowd laughing. Snape in the air, upside down, with everybody watching, and the kind of thing done to him that a person does not forget while he lives.

Then Lily came.

She came across the grass fast, and she was not shouting, which was much worse, and she told James to put him down.

"Let him down, Potter."

"Certainly," said James, and let him fall.

And here is the thing that has stayed with me for forty years. Lily then turned round to help Severus up.

She turned her back on James Potter, in front of the whole school, to help her friend, who was on the ground with grass in his hair and half the year still laughing at him.

And Severus Snape looked up at the girl who had just defended him, and could not bear that it was her, and needed somebody in the world to hurt as badly as he was hurting.

"I don't need help from a filthy little mudblood."

The laughing stopped. All of it. Four hundred people, and I have never heard a place go so quiet so fast.

Lily's face did not change. That was the worst part. It did not change at all.

"Fine," she said. "I won't ask again."

And then she said one more thing, quietly, and only five or six of us were close enough to hear it.

"You and your friends make me sick."

Then she walked away.

He came to the tower that night. Not our tower — he waited outside the door of Gryffindor tower for hours, and the Fat Lady in the picture would not let him in, and in the end Lily came down in her dressing gown.

I was on the stairs. I did not mean to hear it. I heard all of it.

He said that he was sorry. He said it many times.

"Sorry," she said at last. "You're sorry."

"Lily —"

"I can't do this any more," she said. "You call people like me that word. You. And your Death Eater friends — don't tell me you don't know what they are, I'm not stupid, I've seen who you sit with."

He said something about James. He always came back to James.

"This isn't about Potter," she said. "This is about the road you're on."

That is the sentence I remember, because it was true, and because she was fifteen when she said it. Not "I hate you". Not "you insulted me". *This is about the road you're on.*

She forgave him everything for four years. The crowd he ran with. The curses he collected. The things he already knew that no boy should know.

One word ended it — because the word was not an insult from an enemy. It was where the road was going, said out loud, and she heard the whole road in it.

She never spoke to him again. Not once, in the six years she had left.

## 22. The House of Black

In the summer after our sixth year, at two o'clock in the morning, somebody knocked on James Potter's front door.

James's parents opened it. They were old and rich and kind to everybody, the sort of old money that has gone soft and warm like a favourite chair. And on the step, in the rain, stood Sirius Black. Sixteen years old, one bag, a bleeding lip, and a face like a closed door.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't know where else —"

They had him at the kitchen table with soup in front of him inside ten minutes.

He never lived anywhere else again until the war. The Potters put his school things on the family account. They gave him the room next to James's. And Mr Potter gave him a set of keys to the front door — real metal Muggle keys, because he thought a boy should be able to open a door with his own hand.

What happened in the house he left, on that last night, I never learned. He did not tell it and we did not ask.

But I can tell you the shape of the years before it, because we watched them from the outside, every September, every Christmas.

The Noble and Most Ancient House of Black. Say it with the capital letters; they did.

A family that had been magic for so long that they believed magic *was* them. That blood came in grades, and theirs was the finest, and that Muggle-borns like Lily were dirt holding a wand.

Their family words meant *Always Pure*. Their house in London was dark and locked, full of snake-shaped door handles and the heads of dead servants on the walls. I am not inventing that. Ask anybody who ever went inside.

For that family, Voldemort was not a horror rising over the country. He was, at last, a man who said out loud what they said at dinner.

And their eldest son was Sirius. Loud, laughing, Gryffindor Sirius, who covered his bedroom walls with pictures of Muggle girls and Muggle motorbikes, and used a spell so that they could never be taken down, because his mother could not bear to look at them.

Every summer he went back to that house. Every September he came back to us a little harder around the eyes, with new silences in strange places.

We heard his mother's voice once, on the platform, in our third year. I will not try to describe it. It was the sound a whip makes after it has learned words.

His little brother Regulus was softer and sadder. He wanted to be loved inside that house instead of outside it, and year by year he drifted the other way — towards the family, towards the motto, and at last, at eighteen, towards the mark on the arm.

The Death Eaters got the younger son.

On the night Sirius ran, his mother burned his name off the family tree. There was a real tree, painted on a real wall in that house, and she put her wand to it and burned a hole where her first son had been.

He laughed about it. He laughed about all of it, our dog. Laughter was his whole armour.

"Best thing my mother ever did for me," he said. "Very freeing. I recommend it."

But I want to put one more picture beside that laugh.

Christmas, seventh year, at the Potters' house. All four of us were there — Peter too, Peter was always there — and Mrs Potter did what mothers do. Presents for everybody. And for Sirius, exactly the same number as for James, wrapped the same way, signed *with love*.

I watched Sirius Black open a soft parcel with a hand-knitted jumper inside it, dark red, and go completely still. Animals go still like that when they do not want a moment to end.

Sixteen years in the noble and most ancient house of his blood. I think that jumper was the first thing anybody had ever made for him with their hands.

He wore it until it fell to pieces. Years.

I want that on the page before everything else that is coming: before the prison, before the horror, before the twelve years. However loudly he laughed, underneath it he was always a boy at a table with a bowl of soup, listening to the rain on somebody else's roof, learning at sixteen what a house is actually for.

He guarded the people who taught him that the way a dog guards a gate.

Hold on to that. In four chapters, men will tell you that he sold them. They told me. And I believed it for twelve years.

## 23. Seventh Year

In our last autumn at Hogwarts, somebody pinned a newspaper page to the board in the common room.

The headline said: TWO MORE FAMILIES MISSING. MINISTRY ASKS FOR CALM.

Under it, in ink, a seventh-year had written: *They ask for calm because they have nothing else to give.*

That was our seventh year.

Inside, the castle stayed warm. Outside, the world went dark at the edges, like a piece of paper starting to burn.

Voldemort was not a whisper any more. There were attacks now. Disappearances now. And over the houses where he had been, his mark was burned into the sky in green: a skull with a snake coming out of its mouth. He wanted everybody to know his work. Fear was half of what he was building.

The war had a sound inside the school too, low and constant.

Which families had lost somebody. Whose father was "being questioned" at the Ministry. Which Slytherin boys had started wearing long sleeves in the summer heat — because there is one reason to cover your left arm, and we all knew it, and nobody could prove anything.

Snape had stopped pretending by then. He walked the corridors with boys who would be in prison for murder within five years, and the message of the group of them was simple: *soon*.

And in the middle of all that, two ridiculous things happened.

The first was that Dumbledore made James Head Boy.

James Potter. Whose file of school crimes was too fat to close. Sirius laughed for two days and then went quiet, and then said, "Well, obviously," which was the same thing said better.

And the school did not fall down. It turned out that all those years of leading a Quidditch team and leading us had built exactly the man that a frightened castle needed. He knew every first-year's name by October. He walked the Muggle-born students back to their common rooms after dark, and he did it so lightly that being protected felt like being let in on a joke.

The second ridiculous thing was Lily.

And Peter said the truest sentence anybody said in seven years, and we filed it under jokes.

## 24. The Roof

It was a night in May, by the fire, and the war talk had got real.

Names of the missing. Guesses about who was passing information. What the Death Eaters did to the families of the people who fought them — not to the fighters, to the families, because that was the new thing that year and it worked.

Peter was looking at the coals.

"We don't have to decide anything, though," he said. "Do we? I mean — maybe it will all be over before it needs us."

"And if it isn't?" said Sirius. He said it kindly. He was not attacking him.

Peter did not answer for a while. The fire cracked.

Then he said: "You three are good at things. I'm only brave next to you."

And he laughed his small laugh, to turn it into a joke, and reached for another sweet.

We laughed too. Of course we did. It was a modest thing said by a modest friend, and modest friends say things like that, and you laugh and pass them the bag.

*I'm only brave next to you.*

He was telling us. I know that now.

People do tell you. Almost always, at some point, quietly, in plain words, people tell you exactly what they are. And you nod and pass the sweets, because you love them, and because it is warm by the fire.

School ended the way school ends: suddenly, all at once. Trunks in the corridors. Names written in each other's books. Hagrid crying into a handkerchief the size of a tablecloth.

On the last night, the four of us broke the rules one final time.

Up onto the roof — Padfoot's roof, above the astronomy tower — with a bottle of something Sirius had stolen from a teacher, and we sat there and watched the sun come up over the lake. The giant squid was making slow circles in the pink water far below.

Nobody said very much for a long time.

Then James stood up. Eighteen years old, glasses flashing in the sunrise, absolutely certain, the way only James Potter could be certain about anything.

"Whatever happens out there," he said, "we stay us. The four of us. Agreed? Nothing gets in."

"Agreed," said Sirius.

"Agreed," I said.

"Agreed," said Peter.

Four for four.

Then we went down the stairs and out of the castle and into the war, and the war took us — one, two, three, four. Every single one of us, each in a different way.

But I am the only person alive who knows what that morning was actually like. So let me put the important thing down here, at the end of the happy part, where it belongs.

All four of us meant it.

Even him. When Peter Pettigrew said "agreed" on that roof, with the sun coming up and his friends around him, he meant it with his whole heart.

That is the hardest thing I carry. It would be so much easier if he had been lying up there. If there had been a plan in him even then, a cold little door already open somewhere at the back.

There was not. I have gone over that morning ten thousand times, and there was nothing wrong with it.

He was our friend on the roof. He was our friend at the wedding, a year later, asleep in a chair with cake on his shirt.

Between those two pictures, nothing dramatic happened to Peter Pettigrew at all. Nobody put a curse on him. Nobody threatened his mother. There was no dark night when he chose.

There was only a war, and a small man in it, and a slope so gentle that you cannot see it while you are standing on it.

That is Part Two. Take a breath before you start.

## Part Two: The War

Here the story changes. I must warn you now, because nobody warned us.

School stories have rules. The danger is the size of a child. The year ends in summer. The castle always stands. You have just read a school story, and even its worst night had walls around it.

The war had no walls.

We were eighteen when we walked out of the castle. By the age of twenty-one, half the faces from our common room were dead, or hiding, or behind a mask.

I will try to tell it in order. But sadness does not keep good order. Somewhere in these chapters a rat begins to drown, and I do not see it. I am looking at the moon, at the funerals, at everything except the smallest of us, going quietly under the water.

Read on. It does not hurt less if you stop.

## 25. Dark Days

Nobody declares a war like that one. There was no morning when the radio said *it has begun*. There was only water, rising.

At first it was news with strange holes in it.

A family found dead in their house, with no marks on them, and the Muggle police unable to explain it. A bridge that fell down in perfect weather. A whole village that lost one night out of its memory and could not say what had happened to it.

The Ministry of Magic said: these are separate events. The Ministry said: there is no reason to be afraid.

The Ministry, as the boy wrote on our notice board, had nothing else to give.

The truth walked around beside the official words. Voldemort was building an army.

Giants. Dark creatures. And, most of all, wizards: old pure-blood families who wanted the world he was promising, and frightened people who wanted to be on the winning side, and cruel people who wanted permission.

That is all a dark army has ever been, in your world or in ours. The convinced, the afraid, and the cruel, marching in one line.

His soldiers called themselves Death Eaters. They wore masks. They burned his mark into the sky over the houses where they had been.

And here is the fact that made your stomach go cold: nobody caught them.

Witnesses forgot what they had seen. Judges changed their minds between one day and the next. Prisoners walked out somewhere between the arrest and the cell. The rot was inside the walls — inside the Ministry, inside old families, inside the shop on our own street.

By 1979 you could feel it everywhere you went. The war was not in some far country. It was at the next table. It listened while you ordered coffee.

What do you do about that at eighteen? At nineteen?

We did what we could think of.

James and Lily married in the autumn of 1978. I will give the wedding a chapter of its own; happiness gets its own chapters now, because there are not many left.

James put his family's gold into the fight, quietly, the way his father would have done it. Safe houses paid for. Escapes paid for. The families of dead fighters kept fed and warm, month after month, with no letter and no name attached.

We only learned how much of it there had been afterwards, from the people he had saved.

I took the work a werewolf can take. Small jobs. Short jobs. My illness has a way of ending a job about two months after it starts, and after a while people ask questions that you cannot answer, so you leave before they ask.

And Peter took a desk at the Ministry.

It was a junior job. Papers, files, messages between departments. The kind of job that nobody looks at twice.

We were glad about it. One of us with a steady wage, we said. Somebody sensible at last.

Hear the sound of that now, though we could not hear it then. A desk in the Ministry, where the papers go past. Where the corridors talk. Where a small man that nobody looks at twice hears a very great deal.

Then, one evening in a back room above a pub, a tired wizard we all knew put four cups of tea in front of us.

He was one of our old teachers. He told us that Dumbledore was quietly gathering people who meant to actually fight — not to write letters, not to wait — and that the four of us had been asked for by name.

All that reckless courage in one small room, and not one of our hands was steady on the teacup. That is what I remember.

We had been playing at this war in our heads since our fifth year. And here was the door, actually open, actually asking.

James looked round at the three of us. It was the old look. The look from the roof at sunrise.

"Well," he said. "We did say agreed."

## 26. The Motorbike

Sirius bought a motorbike that year.

Of course he did. A huge Muggle machine, black, loud enough to wake a village. And then he put a spell on it so that it could fly, which was illegal, dangerous and completely unnecessary — three of his favourite qualities in one object.

Lily said it was the stupidest thing she had ever seen. Then she asked for a ride, and came back an hour later with her hair standing straight up, laughing so hard that she had to sit down on the step.

But I want you to understand what that machine actually was.

His whole family had gone to the other side.

Not "supported the other side". *Gone* to it. His cousin Bellatrix was already one of the worst of them, a woman who enjoyed the work. Another cousin had married into it. His uncles gave money. His mother, in that dark house in London, had a picture of Voldemort's mark on her wall the way other families have a picture of the sea.

And his brother — little Regulus, who used to follow him around at Christmas — was eighteen years old and had the Dark Mark burned into his arm.

Sirius against Voldemort was never politics. It was a boy burning his own family name off his own arm, the way his mother had burned him off her wall.

Every ride on that roaring Muggle machine was a letter home.

There is one more thing about Regulus, and I will put it here, although I did not learn it until much later and neither did Sirius.

Regulus Black died in 1979. The family said he had left the Death Eaters and been killed for it, and the world said that boys like that always end badly, and everybody moved on.

The truth, which came out years afterwards, is that the youngest son of the Noble and Most Ancient House of Black found out something about his master that nobody else in the world knew, and went alone, at eighteen, to take it from him, and died doing it. In water, in the dark, in a cave by the sea, with nobody watching.

Sirius never knew. He went to his grave believing that his brother had been nothing but a frightened boy who ran the wrong way.

I think about that more than I should. Two brothers, both brave, both fighting the same man, and each of them certain that the other one had chosen the easy road.

Whatever else this book is about, it is about that.

In the summer of 1979 Sirius came off the motorbike in a field in Kent, at night, with three Death Eaters behind him. He broke his arm and lost his wand in the grass and had to be pulled out by James, who arrived shouting, and who then took an hour and a half to stop shouting.

I saw them come in. Sirius was grey and laughing. James was white and furious.

"You went alone," he said. "Again."

"I was faster alone."

"You were nearly dead alone."

"But I wasn't." Sirius sat down. His arm was at a wrong angle and he was pretending it was not. "Prongs. I'm all right."

"You keep doing this," he said. "You keep putting yourself out in front where they can see you. Like you're — I don't know. Like you're paying for something."

Sirius did not answer that.

But I know what I would have written on it, if anybody had asked me.

*A corridor. A tree. A tunnel. One sentence, at sixteen.*

## 27. The Order of the Phoenix

The Order of the Phoenix met wherever it could. Back rooms. Kitchens. Once, believe it or not, a cave.

If you are imagining an army, stop.

I could count us without taking my shoes off. About twenty-five people against a movement. A resistance run by a schoolteacher, paid for out of a dead man's gold, led by the only wizard that Voldemort had ever been afraid of.

Small. Unnoticed. Mild. The perfect watcher.

He carried messages. He kept lists. He sat in the corner of a pub for three hours over half a pint, hearing things, and nobody in the room could have told you afterwards that a man had been sitting there.

## 28. My Own Kind

The Order sent me where it could not politely go.

There are werewolf packs at the edges of our world. You will not find them on a map. They live in camps in the hills, in old farm buildings, in places where nobody asks questions because nobody comes.

They are people. I have to keep saying that, because your world and mine have both spent a long time forgetting it.

They are people who were bitten, and then lost their work, and then lost their families, and then lost their houses, and then found each other, because there is nowhere else on this earth for them to go.

The laws that hunted me had hunted every one of them. The difference between me and them was one old man who came to my parents' kitchen and drank tea and said yes.

That is the whole difference. I have never once been able to make it mean anything more than luck.

And Voldemort went to them with promises.

He was clever about it. He did not offer them money. He offered them the only thing they actually wanted.

*Under me, there will be no registry. No locked doors on the night of the moon. No forms to fill in, no work refused, no landlord's face when he finds out. Under me, you will not hide. Under me, you hunt.*

Do you understand how good that offer is, if you have spent thirty years being told to be sorry for existing?

Dumbledore hoped that a werewolf of his own could argue against it. So several times a year I disappeared into those camps under a false name, and slept on the ground, and argued for a world that had never once been kind to me.

I was not very good at it.

I want to be honest about that as well. I lost more arguments than I won. What could I say? I could not promise them anything. The best I had was: *the other side will use you and then throw you away, and this side, however bad it is, is at least not evil.*

That is a thin thing to say to a man who has not had work in six years.

There was one man in the northern camp who listened to me for three nights. His name was Aled. He was about fifty and he had been bitten at twenty-two, and he had a photograph of two daughters that he had not seen since 1968.

On the third night he said: "You went to school."

"Yes."

"They let you in."

"One man let me in."

"And when you finished," he said, "did they let you work?"

I did not answer him. He nodded, as if I had.

He joined Greyback's people that winter. I heard he died in 1981, and I do not know on which side, and I am not sure it matters much.

Now mark what all this meant at home, because it is a wheel in the machine that kills everybody at the end of this book.

I disappeared for weeks. I could not say where I had been. I could not say why. Dumbledore's orders.

Secrecy was the Order's blood. We worked in small groups, and each group knew only its own piece, so that a captured friend could not give away the rest. That was wise. That was how it had to be done.

And it meant that all of us, all the time, were disappearing from each other behind walls of permitted silence.

Remember the shape of that wall. It comes back in October 1981, and I walk into it, and I never see what is behind it until it is far too late.

## 29. The Wedding

They were married in the autumn of 1978, in the garden of the old Potter house.

I am going to spend a whole chapter on one afternoon. I promised you that happiness gets its own chapters now, and there is another reason too: when everything after a day is ash, an afternoon is exactly the size of thing that a survivor keeps.

It was small. It was as small as a war makes things.

Not anything near the cake. He had his reasons and nobody asked for them.

## 30. A Warm Room and a Kind Voice

I must do a difficult thing now. I must go inside Peter.

Understand what this chapter is. It is not knowledge. It is the work of many years.

I have laid every fact I own side by side — things he said, things he did, things he confessed at the very end with my wand at his chest — and I have walked around them for ten thousand nights, asking the only question that people ever really ask after a betrayal.

Not *why*. *How*.

How does the boy from the train become the man at the door?

Here is my answer. I warn you: the horrible part is that there is nothing horrible in it. It is all so small.

Start here. For seven years at school, Peter Pettigrew stood in the light of three unusually bright people.

And everybody — teachers, students, us — looked at him and privately thought a version of the same sentence.

*What is he doing with them?*

Do you know what it costs to hear that question in every second glance, for seven years?

We thought our friendship answered it. I think now that our friendship *asked* it, every single day. And he had to answer it every single day, and his answer was work. Laugh first. Agree fast. Fetch things. Say the kind thing. Stay useful. Stay near.

We read all of that as love, and it was love. It was also, and we never saw this, a kind of exhausting fear: fear of the only thing Peter truly could not survive, which was being put out of the light.

Then school ended, and the light changed.

At school, being near James and Sirius cost him nothing but homework.

In the Order, the bill came.

Being near them now meant fighting masked men on rooftops. It meant standing in front of Voldemort himself — and you remember what the stories said. *James and Sirius stood in front of him three times and lived.*

The stories never said what Peter did in those rooms. I never asked. I ask now.

I think he stood behind his friends' shoulders and looked into that white face and those red eyes, the way a mouse looks at a cat, and understood something that brave people are protected from understanding.

Because the brave do their sums wrong. That is the truth of it.

James, Sirius, the Prewetts, all our beautiful dead — every one of them privately believed that they would probably live. That is the lie that courage runs on, and it is a good lie, and it works.

Peter had no such protection. He was a small man with accurate eyes, and he did the arithmetic honestly, and the arithmetic said: *these people are going to lose, and I am going to die with them. Not saving anybody. Not for anything. Just dead, in a corridor, because I followed my school friends into their war.*

And the other side had noticed him.

That part I only put together later, from what came out at trials after everything ended.

The Death Eaters were clever about people. Fear is a kind of attention, and they had studied us. They did not waste anybody on Sirius Black, and they did not waste anybody on me.

They looked for the soft place. And they found a tired man with a Ministry desk and worried eyes.

And they did not threaten him first.

In some pub, on some evening, a man with a smooth, kind voice sat down beside frightened little Peter Pettigrew and told him that people had noticed him. That his gifts were wasted where he was. That the winning side rewards men who are realistic.

They offered him exactly what we had given him at eleven years old. A place near power, and safety from it. The rat rides between the antlers. The forest is afraid of the stag, and nothing can touch the rider.

You want a monster in this chapter. I understand. I wanted one for years; hate is so much lighter to carry when it has claws.

But I promised you the truth, and the truth is a small man with wet eyes at a corner table, saying nothing wrong at all on that first evening. Just listening. Just warming himself, one evening, at a second fire.

## 31. It Was Not a Fall

I said that the horror of it is that there is no horror in it. Let me finish that thought, because everything else in this book depends on it.

It was not a fall. It was a lean.

Nobody sells four lives in one afternoon. That is not how it is done, and I have come to believe that it is almost never how it is done, in our world or in yours.

He leaned. An inch a month.

From fear towards safety. From safety towards keeping a small secret. From a small secret towards a first small payment.

And every inch of the way — I am certain of this — he told himself the story that frightened people always tell.

*I have not done anything yet. I can still stop. It is only talk. I am still their friend.*

He was at the wedding after the leaning had already started. Cake on his shirt. Crying when Hagrid cried. Still our friend, in his own head, honestly still our friend, planning nothing at all.

The door to a bad place, reader, is not a door. It is a slope, and there is no single step on it that is worth dying over.

That is the whole design of the thing. That is why brave people miss it. They watch for the great moment of choice, the temptation with trumpets, the man in the black mask offering gold on a table.

There is no such moment. There is a tired man, and a warm room, and somebody listening to him kindly, and a thousand tiny kindnesses that he does to himself, one at a time.

And then, one October night, there is a cottage with its door blown off, and a baby crying over two bodies.

And the man who did it is *still telling himself the story*.

At the very end — twelve years later, in the Shrieking Shack, with his own life in his hands — Peter looked at me. At the last of us. And he said the thing that I believe was the true confession under all the crying:

"You don't understand. He would have killed me."

Yes, Peter. He might have.

That was the price of the roof at sunrise. That was what *agreed, agreed, agreed, agreed* actually cost, and three of us had always known it and signed anyway.

You signed for the seat by the fire. And when the bill came, you paid it with our lives instead of your own.

That is the how. After ten thousand nights, it fits into three sentences.

He was never wicked. He was never brave. And it turns out that in a war, the second thing, missing, does all the work of the first.

I have one more thing to say in this chapter, and then we can go back to the story.

People ask me — Harry asked me, once, carefully, the year I taught him — whether I hate him.

The honest answer is that I have never been able to hate Peter Pettigrew properly, and that this has been one of the loneliest facts of my life.

Everybody else can. Sirius could, and did, for twelve years, and it kept him alive. Harry's friends can. Strangers in shops can.

I cannot, because I am the one who remembers him crying on the bathroom floor because his hands would not change, and I know that the boy on that floor and the man at the door are the same person, and that there was no moment in between where one turned into the other.

If you take anything from this chapter, take this. Watch the small choices in the people you love. Not the big ones. Nobody fails the big ones; the big ones come with music and everybody stands up straight.

Watch what they do when it is only an inch, and nobody is looking, and nothing is at stake at all.

## 32. Friends Die

In the summer of 1981 the war stopped being a sea and became a hand, closing.

I will give you the numbers first, coldly, because we lived them coldly. There comes a point where the heart simply refuses to do ceremony any more.

The Order had been about twenty-five people. By that summer we had buried a third of them.

Benjy Fenwick. They found pieces of him. I told you that he used to say sorry to owls; hold both of those facts in your hands at once, and that is the war.

Gideon and Fabian Prewett, back to back. Five against two, and the five needed all five.

Dorcas Meadowes, killed by Voldemort himself.

Edgar Bones and his whole family. That was the new thing that year: the Death Eaters had started including families, as a message. The message was received.

And in July, Marlene.

Marlene McKinnon had been at the wedding. She had helped Lily dress. She was loud and generous and completely without fear, and she had been Lily's closest friend since they were eleven — the friend from before James, the one who knew Lily's Muggle house and her difficult sister and her whole unmagical heart.

The McKinnons were attacked at night, at home. All of them. Marlene, her parents, her brothers. By the time anybody knew, there was nothing left to defend.

I was at James and Lily's house on the morning the news came.

I watched Lily read the letter standing up. Then I watched her fold it, very carefully, into four. She put it on the table and smoothed it flat with her hand.

And then she went on smoothing it, over and over, long after it was flat, because her hand needed something to do and there was nothing left on this earth for it to do.

Harry was crying upstairs. He was a year old that month. She went up to him, and through the ceiling we heard her walking him and singing something Muggle, and her voice was completely level.

James sat with his glasses in his hands and did not move.

Sirius stood at the window like a man guarding a house that has already been robbed.

That was also the summer when the deaths stopped being random.

Moody saw it first, of course. Watching was his religion.

Our work was failing in a pattern. Safe houses found too quickly. Guards attacked at handovers that only four people had known about. The McKinnons had been moved quietly two weeks before the attack — and the attack came to the new address.

Dorcas's location had been a secret shared with a handful of people. The handful kept getting smaller, and the deaths kept finding the middle of it.

There was a word for what that pattern meant. For a while nobody would say it, because saying it ended the world we were fighting for.

Moody said it, in the end, at a meeting in somebody's kitchen with the curtains sealed shut.

"We have a spy. Inside the Order. And he is close to the Potters."

*Close to the Potters.*

Because that was the other thing, the thing I have not told you yet. By that summer the war had a direction, and the direction was James and Lily's front door.

Voldemort was hunting them. Them specifically. Their son specifically.

Why, I did not know then. That terrible why has its own chapter, three away from here.

What we knew that summer was simpler and quite enough: the most hunted family in Britain was being followed by somebody who sat at our meetings, drank our tea, and knew our faces.

Somebody we loved. There was nobody else it could be. That is what *inside* means.

## 33. Locked Drawers

I want you to sit where we sat, in that kitchen with the curtains sealed, and feel the poison start to work.

Look round the table.

Everybody there had bled for this. Everybody there was above suspicion. And the arithmetic said that one of them was writing our funerals.

So the mind begins to sort.

You do not tell it to. You order it not to. And the loyal, tired, grieving mind goes on quietly sorting your dearest people into two piles: *possible* and *impossible*.

Here is what my mind did. I am going to confess it as plainly as I confessed Peter's part, because this book does not get to have two different standards.

My mind said: *Sirius is possible.*

Not likely. Never likely. But possible.

He was a Black. His blood, his brother, his cousins — the whole dark, snake-handled house of them — were on the other side, and everybody in that kitchen knew it.

And I had seen something once. A corridor, sixteen years old, one sentence about a root and a tree. I had seen with my own eyes that under enough pressure, Sirius Black could spend a friend's life without thinking about it first.

The old splinter. It had waited fifteen years for exactly this infection.

And at the same table, Sirius's mind was working too.

I know now what it decided. *Remus is possible.*

The werewolf. He disappears for weeks. He cannot say where he goes — orders, he says. He goes among the dark packs, his own kind, the ones that Voldemort is offering a better world than we can ever offer them.

And when did my missions start going wrong? Check the dates.

His mind, like mine, betrayed nobody in public. It just sorted.

We were still brothers. We still guarded each other's lives; twice that year he pulled me out of places I would not have walked out of alone.

But between us now, behind the eyes, each of us kept one small locked drawer with the other's name inside it.

And Peter passed both of our inspections without one second's pause.

I have searched my memory of those months. I have searched it under bright lights, the way police search a room, looking for one flicker of doubt about Peter, from anybody, ever.

There is nothing.

He cried at the funerals. He was frightened in exactly the amount that a harmless man should be frightened. In meetings he asked the slightly slow questions of somebody doing his best to keep up.

Once — this is the memory that visits me most — once, at the end of a meeting, he could not get his coat off the hook because his hands were shaking, and Sirius did it for him and made a joke, and Peter laughed and said, "I'm no good at this, am I," and Sirius said, "Nobody's any good at this."

He had come to that meeting from the other side. I am nearly certain of the date.

The spy hunt looked everywhere except at the floor.

And here is what I understand now, thirteen years too late, and it is the third of the three things this book has to give you.

Suspicion is not the same as watchfulness.

For one entire year every one of us was watchful. We watched the way Moody had taught us to watch. CONSTANT VIGILANCE. And the watching itself was the wound that Voldemort used.

Sirius and I were two innocent men, and each of us decided that the other one was the traitor. On evidence, reader. Real evidence. Dates and silences and family names. Better evidence than anybody ever had against Peter Pettigrew in his life.

The locked drawers did not protect us. They were the machine of the disaster.

Distrust does not find traitors. Distrust *makes* them, because it eats exactly the thing that would have caught the real one.

If any two of us in that sealed year had simply said the thing that could not be said out loud — *I have a drawer with your name in it. Show me I am wrong. Here is mine* — the whole machine stops.

Peter lived on our silence. He fed on it twice: once as our friend, and once as a name on a list of everybody except himself.

The rat's whole way of staying safe is the question that nobody asks.

## 34. Harry

Harry James Potter was born on the thirty-first of July, 1980.

I held him when he was three days old.

He already had James's impossible hair. From the first hour — hair with its own opinions about everything. And then he opened his eyes at me, and they were Lily's. Exactly Lily's. Green as the first thing that grows after a fire.

I gave him back quite quickly. I remember being afraid of my own hands.

Sirius was made godfather in that room. He stood holding the baby with a face I had seen twice before: the red jumper at Christmas, the wedding in the garden. The face of a gate swinging open.

James put an arm round him and said, "If anything ever happens to us, Padfoot —"

"Nothing is going to happen to you," said Sirius.

He said it in a voice that closed the sentence like a door. Nobody argued with him. It was easier not to.

That first year of Harry's life was, I think, the happiest year James and Lily had.

That sounds impossible. Read the last three chapters again: it was the worst year of the war. Their friends were dying every month. They almost never left the house without checking the street twice.

And they were happy. Both things at once. That is the part that people who have not lived through a bad time never quite believe.

A baby does not care about the war. A baby needs feeding at four in the morning. And a war is a thing that happens outside, and a baby is a thing that happens at your kitchen table, and at four in the morning the kitchen table is the whole world.

James was ridiculous about him. Absolutely ridiculous.

He bought Harry a toy broomstick for his first birthday — a tiny one, a foot off the ground, and Lily said it was too dangerous and then spent an hour watching Harry knock a vase over on it and laughing until she cried.

He made coloured smoke out of his wand to make Harry laugh. Red smoke, gold smoke, smoke shaped like a stag.

I want you to hold on to that particular detail. It is going to come back in a hallway, in fifteen months' time, and when it does I want you to already know what that wand had been doing an hour earlier.

And Peter was there, of course. Peter was there for all of it.

He was very good with the baby, in fact. Better than Sirius, who held Harry the way you hold something made of glass. Peter could feed him and change him and walk him up and down a room for an hour without complaining.

Once — this memory has teeth in it — I came in and found Peter asleep on the sofa with Harry asleep on his chest, and Lily put a finger to her lips so that I would not wake either of them.

That was in the spring of 1981.

By that spring he had been passing information to Voldemort's people for something over a year.

I have to write both of those sentences in the same chapter, and I have to leave them next to each other, because that is where they belong. That is where they always were.

He loved that baby. He would have told you so, and it would have been true.

And he was carrying, in his pocket, the beginning of the thing that killed him.

There is a photograph from Harry's first birthday. Somebody put it in an album that came to me later. All of us in the garden, squinting in the sun. James holding Harry up in the air. Lily with her hand over her eyes, laughing. Sirius making a face at the camera. Peter at the edge, half in shadow, smiling.

I have looked at that photograph more times than I can count, at his face in it, looking for something.

There is nothing there. He is just a friend at a birthday, smiling because everybody is smiling.

That is what I most want you to understand about the whole of this book. There was never anything to see.

## 35. The Prophecy

Now the terrible why.

Why, out of every fighting family in Britain, did the war narrow down to one door?

I learned the whole truth years later, in pieces, from Dumbledore himself. That summer we knew only the shape of it. But you may as well have all of it now.

Some months before Harry was born, a prophecy was spoken.

A real one. They are rare, and they are stranger than the kind you buy at a fair. The person who speaks one does not remember doing it afterwards.

It said — not straight out, because these things never come straight out — that as the seventh month died, a boy would be born to parents who had three times stood against the Dark Lord. And that the boy would have the power to end him.

Two families in Britain fitted those words like a key fits a lock.

The Longbottoms: Frank and Alice, quiet, married, three times against him, expecting a boy at the end of July.

And the Potters.

Voldemort should never have heard one word of it. But the prophecy was overheard — only the first half of it — by a servant who ran to his master with what he had got.

And so a man with endless power and endless fear sat down with a riddle. And he did what fear always does. He chose the answer that looked most like himself.

The Longbottoms were an old wizarding family. Lily was Muggle-born, so her son was a half-blood — like Voldemort himself, whose father was a Muggle, a fact he had killed men for knowing.

He looked into the riddle and saw his own reflection. And he pointed his whole war at a baby in a knitted hat.

There is one more thing about the man who overheard it, and I will tell you now, and then I will leave it alone, because it is not really this book's story.

It was Severus Snape.

He carried the first half of that prophecy to his master. He did not know whose child it would turn out to mean; nobody could have known that in the room. But he carried it, because that was the road he was on, and Lily had told him in our fifth year exactly where that road went.

And when he found out whose door his information had opened, he went to Dumbledore in the night, and begged for her life, and changed sides, and stayed changed until the day he died sixteen years later.

I did not know any of that until long after everything in this book was over.

He never once told James or Sirius or me, and I understand why. And I have decided, in my old age, that I am glad I did not know while I taught in that school beside him, because I do not think I would have behaved well, and he was carrying enough.

When Dumbledore came to James and Lily — late in 1980, on a night when I was away in the camps — he brought them the worst sentence that any parent can hear.

*He has chosen your son.*

He told them to disappear. Completely. No more missions, no more meetings, no more Order work for James and Lily Potter. A locked house. Closed curtains. A hidden life.

For Lily the choice was no choice at all. She was a mother.

For James — and I want this understood, because his stillness cost him more than all our running cost us — for James it was a kind of death before the death.

He was twenty years old and the best fighter of any of us, and he was forbidden to fight. James Potter's whole soul was built for standing between danger and other people. And now the war had turned him round: everybody else was standing between danger and him.

He wrote to me that winter. I still have the letter. One line of it says:

*The worst part, Moony, is being the thing that everybody I love is in danger for.*

## 36. The Hidden House

Now I must stop the story and teach for two pages.

Everything that is left turns on one piece of magic. Learn it here and the ending will need no explaining.

It is called the Fidelius Charm.

It hides a truth by putting it inside a single living person.

Not behind a wall. Not in a code. Inside a person, and that person is called the Secret-Keeper.

Here is how it works. Take a fact: *the Potters live in the cottage at Godric's Hollow*. Put that fact inside the Keeper with the charm.

From that moment, the fact leaves the world.

Nobody can find it. Nobody can work it out, follow it, force it, or guess it. Voldemort himself could press his face against their front window and see only an empty ruin. An army could search that village for a hundred years and walk through their garden every morning and see nothing but grass.

The only way anybody will ever learn it is if the Keeper opens his mouth and chooses to tell.

That is the whole charm. It is one of the finest pieces of protective magic that our world has ever made, and it does not fail. Charms are honest.

But look at the shape of it.

It has exactly one moving part.

You must pour every drop of your family's safety into a single human being. And so the question of whether your wife and your son live or die becomes, completely and for ever, the oldest question in this book.

The question from the train compartment. The question from the round room in the tower. The question from the sealed kitchen.

*Which of your friends do you hand your life to?*

I want to be careful here, because it is easy to read that and think: what a stupid, fragile way to protect anybody.

It is not stupid. Think about what they had.

They had a house that could not be defended, a baby, a man hunting them who had never failed to kill anybody he wanted to kill, and a war they were losing. The choice was not between the Fidelius Charm and something safer. There was nothing safer. There was nothing else at all.

And they had something that they had been building since they were eleven years old, and it was the strongest thing any of them owned.

So the plan was not madness. The plan was: *we are four people who would die for each other, and we always have been, and we will put the whole thing inside that.*

If you had asked James Potter to name the safest object in Britain, he would not have said a vault at Gringotts. He would have said his friends.

He had every reason. For ten years there had been no evidence against that answer anywhere in his life.

And I want to say one more thing before the next chapter, because in the next chapter everybody decides, and after that there is nothing to decide about ever again.

The Fidelius Charm is a machine that runs on trust.

That is its beauty and that is its horror. Every other kind of protection in our world can be broken by somebody stronger than you. This one cannot.

This one can only be broken from inside, by somebody who loves you, choosing to speak.

They cast it in the last days of October, 1981.

## 37. The Switch

The obvious Secret-Keeper was Sirius.

Everybody knew that it would be Sirius. In the end, that was the whole problem.

This chapter is four decisions, made by four people. Every one of them is reasonable. Together they make a machine, and none of the four could see it.

**The first decision.** Dumbledore offered to do it himself.

He came to the cottage and said: let it be me. Voldemort could not pull a secret out of Albus Dumbledore; nobody in the world could.

James said no. With love, with thanks, but no.

Some of that was the old certainty. *We look after our own.* In ten years the Marauders had never once needed rescuing by anybody outside the four of us.

And some of it was darker, and I only learned this much later. By then the whisper about a spy had reached even Dumbledore, and Dumbledore trusted nobody completely, and James could hear that in the offer. It stung.

If the choice was between handing his family to the great man who suspected everybody, or to the brother who had held the rings at his wedding — for James Potter that was not a choice at all.

**The second decision.** So: Sirius. It was settled for about three days.

And then Sirius sat in the cottage kitchen and took it all back.

Lily was making tea. Harry was asleep upstairs. It was the last ordinary evening that family ever had, and it was ticking away quietly on the shelf while they talked.

I have the scene from Sirius himself, told to me twelve years afterwards in a voice I did not recognise. I will give it to you almost in his words.

"Think about it the way they'll think about it," he said. "The whole world knows James doesn't move without me. I'm the obvious lock on this door. So obvious that the door might as well have a sign on it. He will come for me first, James. First hour of the first day."

And then the pause. And then the whole tragedy in one breath of honesty.

"And I can hold out against a lot. I don't know if I can hold out against *him*. Nobody knows that about themselves. I won't be the one thing standing between you and — no. Not me."

**The third decision.** And then the idea. The clever, loving, fatal idea.

"Use Peter."

He leaned forward.

"You see it, don't you? It's perfect. It's the map all over again — nobody watches the small ones. Voldemort will hunt *me*. I'll run, and I'll run loudly. Let him spend everything on breaking the obvious lock. And the whole time the real key sits quiet in the pocket of the one man alive that he would never think to search."

Read that again.

It is a good plan.

That is what I need you to sit with, because everybody afterwards called it carelessness, and it was not carelessness.

Every part of it is something true that he had noticed. People really do overlook Peter. Sirius really was the obvious target. A false trail really does add one more wall.

And look at which half Sirius kept for himself. His half of that plan was *being hunted by Voldemort*. He volunteered for it with both hands, at twenty-one, without being asked.

Men have been made into saints for less.

He aimed every drop of his cleverness and his courage at saving them. And because one fact inside his machine was false — one single fact that nobody alive doubted — the cleverness and the courage carried the death warrant to the door and handed it over.

**The fourth decision.** James laughed and agreed.

Lily asked twice whether Peter himself was willing. That was Lily: thinking, even then, about the weight on the smallest back.

And Peter said yes.

Here is where I stop being able to imagine it.

I have stood in every corner of that kitchen ten thousand nights. The fire. The tea. Harry's toy on the floor.

And Peter Pettigrew, already months in Voldemort's service, already carrying the deaths of better men on his soft hands, listening while his three dearest friends — with bright, relieved, trusting faces — invented for themselves the one plan that took away every wall between his master and that room.

And then asked him. Begged him, please, to accept it.

He did not have to hunt for the secret. The secret walked over to him, put its arms around him, and thanked him for the favour.

## 38. A Wall of Glass

And now the last decision of the four. The small hinge that everything swings on. The one that was pointed at me.

The switch had to stay secret to work. Of course it did; a false trail only draws the hunter while the world believes it is real.

But the circle of people who knew was drawn at exactly three. James. Lily. Peter.

Sirius knew that the plan had been made, but not the timing.

And I — I, Moony, one of the four names on the map — was left outside it completely. Nobody said why. Nobody had to. I felt the shape of the unspoken thing like a wall of glass.

The locked drawers, reader. The sealed kitchen. *The spy is close to the Potters.*

Sirius had done his quiet sorting a year before, and his drawer had my name in it. The werewolf who disappears among the dark packs. Who cannot say where he goes.

Tell Remus nothing. Not out of hate — never out of hate. Out of love doing the ugly arithmetic of a war, which is a loving reason for doing an unforgivable thing.

And I, who had a drawer of my own with his name in it, felt myself shut out of the circle and drew exactly the same conclusion about him.

So in the last week of October 1981, the two innocent men circled each other in the ruins of a ten-year friendship, each quietly grieving the other as somebody who had probably gone over.

And the rat sat in the pocket of the secret.

I should tell you about the last time I spoke to Sirius Black before the world ended.

It was the middle of October. We had a pair of mirrors — James had made four of them in our fifth year, so that we could talk to each other while sitting in different detentions, which tells you everything about what our talent was spent on.

I called him. It took a long time before his face came into the glass.

"Moony."

"You look terrible," I said.

"I'm working."

"Where are you?"

There was a pause. It was not a long pause. It was about half a second, and half a second is nothing, and I heard it the way you hear a wrong note in a song you know very well.

"Around," he said.

"Right."

"Where are *you*?" he said.

And I heard myself do exactly the same thing. Half a second. Then: "Around."

We talked for four more minutes about nothing. Quidditch. His arm. A man we both knew who had lost a brother.

Then he said he had to go, and the glass went dark, and I sat on the end of a borrowed bed in Yorkshire and looked at my own face in the mirror instead of his.

That was the last conversation. Two men who had shared a room for seven years, each listening to the other for the sound of a lie, and each of us hearing exactly what he had been listening for — because we were both lying, and the lies were both innocent, and neither of us could say so.

I have gone back to that mirror ten thousand times.

Four minutes. Either one of us could have said the sentence. *Something is wrong between us and I do not know what it is.* Eight words. We were the two people in the world most likely to say a hard thing to each other and survive it.

Neither of us said anything at all. We were polite, and then we hung up.

## 39. The Last Week

What does the end of the world look like from one week away? I can tell you exactly. It looks like nothing.

I still have my diary from that October. It is the most terrible object I own, and there is no terror in it at all.

Tuesday: rain. Letter from Mother. The Prewetts' memorial moved to the ninth.

Wednesday: camp business. I was between missions, sleeping on a friend's floor in Yorkshire, eating badly.

Thursday: *tried to arrange a visit to J & L again. Put off again. Third time. Something is wrong, or I am.*

Friday: nothing. Saturday: nothing.

*Put off again. Third time.* One line of dry ink is all that the great regret of my life gets, because I did not know that it was the last week. Nobody stamps the last week for you.

Since the charm, visits to the cottage went through arrangements, for safety. You asked, and a time was fixed. My asking, that autumn, kept sliding.

Never refused. Nothing is ever refused among friends. Just moved. *Soon, Moony. Things are mad just now. You understand.*

And I did understand. That was the poison working exactly as it was designed to work. I understood perfectly.

Here is where the four of us stood in that last week. Look at the map of it, because the map is the ending.

James and Lily were inside the charm, inside the cottage, inside a safety that was total and complete — and that depended on one man.

James was going mad with it. He paced. He wrote letters. His last one to me is dated the twenty-eighth. It is four sentences long, and none of them matter: Quidditch, a joke about Harry's toy broom, *come after the moon and we'll fix a proper date*.

Four sentences of ordinary love from a man with three days to live. I have read that piece of paper more times than every book I own put together.

Sirius was running his half of the plan. Being visible. Being loud. Being bait. Riding the motorbike high over the motorways, sleeping in a different place every night, letting himself be seen a long way from Godric's Hollow.

Alone, because the plan needed even his friends kept at arm's length. Carrying what he believed was the most dangerous job in the war. Believing, that whole week, that his own courage was standing between Voldemort and everybody he loved.

Every mile of it pointed away from the true door. He might as well have been guarding the moon.

Peter had gone quietly "into hiding". The story fitted the plan. The plan was his alibi. The alibi was our own invention.

And somewhere in those days, in some dark place that I refuse to imagine because even my hatred will not go in there, he stood in front of his master and opened his mouth and said an address.

That is the moment, if you are looking for one. Not Halloween. Halloween was only the carrying out of it.

Perhaps eight words, spoken by the boy we fed on the train. And in exchange — the accounts of history are exact here — he received his own continued heartbeat, and nothing else at all.

No gold. No power. The price of James and Lily Potter was one small man's permission to go on being afraid.

And I was on a floor in Yorkshire, an hour away by broom, checking the lunar calendar, feeling sorry for myself.

My last clear feeling about the four of us, before the world ended, was that they had all drifted away from me and that it was unfair.

I fell asleep on the thirtieth of October composing, half angrily, the letter I would send James after the moon.

*Old friend, whatever is going on, the four of us are older than this war —*

I never sent it. There was nobody left to send it to.

## Part Three: The Longest Night

Everything in this book so far took ten years. Everything that is left took about eight hours.

One night — Halloween, and then the first grey morning of November, 1981 — broke the four of us so completely that when the sun came up, there was no world left in which we had been happy.

Two of us were dead. One was on his way to a cage in the sea. One was a hero and a rat. And one of us was left standing in a borrowed room, to spend twelve years believing the wrong story with his whole heart.

That one was me. People always said I was the lucky one.

I have told you everything in order, as I promised. Here is the end of the order.

## 40. Halloween

He came on foot, they say.

Down the middle of the lane, through the falling leaves, past the lit windows of Muggle children in paper masks. It was the one night of the year when a walking horror draws no eyes at all.

Halloween gave him that. He always did like a piece of theatre.

The charm fell away in front of him like a curtain pulled aside. The Keeper had told, and what the Keeper tells, the world may enter.

Where every eye for a year had seen an empty ruin, he saw it: lit windows, a garden gate, a pumpkin on the step. The hidden house of the Potters, standing again in the world of things that can be found.

With its door.

I was not there. No friend was there; that was the design of the thing.

What follows is put together from the evidence of that night, from Dumbledore, and from the one living witness — who was one year old, and who met that memory again years later, in the presence of certain dark creatures that force the worst hour of your life back up into the light.

Harry paid for these facts. Remember that while you read them.

James died first.

And he died exactly as anybody who ever knew him would have written it, and I mean that as the highest sentence I can build.

He heard the door. He shouted up the stairs — *Lily, take Harry and run, I'll hold him off* — and he walked out into the hallway to buy time.

His wand was on the sofa.

Do not go quickly past that. I have kept that detail for years in the place where other men keep prayers.

It was Halloween in a safe house. He had been making coloured smoke with that wand an hour earlier to make a one-year-old laugh; Lily wrote it in a letter to Sirius, and the letter still exists.

And when the end of the world came through his front door, James Potter — twenty-one years old, the finest duellist of our year — stepped towards it with empty hands.

Buying seconds. Seconds are all that stairs need, if you love the people on them.

There was no version of James — none, in any world — who stays behind his wife and child while the door comes down.

Voldemort killed him in the hallway of his own house. I hope it was too fast to hurt. That is my oldest hope, and I have no way to check it, and I have decided to keep it anyway.

Upstairs, Lily had no way out. The trap was complete: the same falling charm that let him in had sealed the windows.

She put Harry in his cot. She put herself between the cot and the door.

And now listen very carefully, because the exact words became the axis that the whole world turns on.

He offered her her life.

*Stand aside, girl.* Three times. The testimony is specific: three times he told her that she did not need to die, that his business was only with the boy.

That was not mercy. There was no mercy in him; I will not let anybody read that scene wrongly. Somebody had asked him for her life, and years later I learned who had asked, and that knowledge changed the map of those years all over again. But it is past the edge of this book.

What matters is the offer, and what she did with it.

She refused. Three times.

*Take me instead. Take me. Not Harry — please, not Harry.*

She was twenty-one years old. She had no wand in her hands either.

She could not have known what those words would do. No book anywhere promised that it would do anything at all. It was not a spell. It was not a plan. It was nothing but a mother, standing in a doorway, declining to move.

And when he killed her — and they say his voice was *annoyed* — and turned his wand at last on the child in the cot, and spoke the killing words that had never once failed him in all his years of murder —

The curse broke.

It hit the boy, and it came back, and it tore Voldemort out of his own body like a hand pulled out of a glove.

The house came down around the two of them.

And in the smoking ruin of that little room, under half a fallen roof, with a cut on his forehead in the shape of lightning, Harry Potter sat up in his cot. Alive. Crying for his mother.

Alive.

## 41. The Ruined House

The books that your world writes about ours begin here, with the miracle.

The Boy Who Lived. The night the war ended. Bells and owls and strangers drinking in the street.

All true. The darkest wizard of the age undone by a baby; there has never been such a night, and there probably never will be again.

But this book is not those books.

From inside our story, here is Halloween whole: the safest house in Britain opened like a tin. The two brightest people I will ever know dead on their own floors at twenty-one, with no wands in their hands, buying seconds. A miracle of love standing in the wreck of it.

And the whole night bought, unlocked and delivered by the small friend of their childhood, in exchange for the beating of his own heart.

Somewhere in England at that hour, Peter Pettigrew heard that his master had gone into a house that he himself had opened, and had not come out.

And he began at once — you must give the survivor's instinct its dark cleverness — he began at once to think about doors.

Sirius knew before anybody told him.

He had arranged to check on Peter that night. It was part of the plan: the guard looking in on the vault.

He flew the motorbike to the hiding place and found it empty.

No Peter. No body. No sign of any fight at all. Cups on the table. The bed made. A lamp left burning.

Just gone. Softly. The way rats leave.

And in that second, he told me twelve years later, the whole year turned over in his head like a picture righting itself. The leaks. The ambushes. The deaths that kept finding the middle of every circle as it got smaller.

Not Remus. *Not Remus.*

Oh God. Peter.

He flew to Godric's Hollow with his heart already knowing.

Try, if you can bear it, to ride behind him. Low over the October fields, the cold rain like needles, the bike screaming.

Then the village. Then the lane.

And then the thing that could not be there.

The house was *visible*. From the air. To his eyes.

He knew exactly what that meant. He had helped to design what that meant. The charm holds until the Keeper tells. If you can see the house, then the secret has been spoken, and there was only one living mouth that the secret was in.

The roof was torn open like paper. Smoke was still standing in the rain.

He found James in the hallway.

I will not stay in this paragraph long, and he never stayed in it at all. In twelve years of prison and two years of freedom, Sirius Black described that hallway to me exactly once, in one sentence.

The sentence was: *"His glasses were still on."*

Whatever else that night burned out of him, I believe that is where the burning started. Kneeling in the doorway of the house he had been given his own keys to. At the body of the boy from the train. His brother by choice, still wearing his glasses, still lying between the door and the stairs.

Lily was upstairs, in the little room.

And in the wreck of the cot, wet and screaming and bleeding from the forehead, was Harry.

## 42. Take the Motorbike

For one moment — hold this moment, it is the last unbroken piece of Sirius Black — for one moment, standing in that ruin with the baby in his arms, there was still a road.

He was the godfather. It was legal. It was written down. It was what James's will asked for.

A cottage somewhere. A war orphan and a war-orphaned man. The bike sold, a pram bought, a job found, whatever it took.

Harry brought up by the man his father loved most in the world, who would have died for him daily and called it wages. I say that as a fact. I knew him.

Then Hagrid came into the garden.

He had been sent the moment the protections fell, and he had Dumbledore's orders in his mouth.

*The boy goes to his mother's Muggle sister.*

There were reasons. There was old protection in the blood, plans already forming behind those half-moon glasses. Dumbledore saw further than anybody and, as always, told nobody the whole of anything.

And Sirius stood in the rain holding everything he had left in the world, and argued.

Hagrid — the gentlest giant there has ever been — wept, and would not move. Orders are orders, Sirius. The boy's to go to his aunt's.

And Sirius gave in.

He put Harry into Hagrid's arms.

And then he did the thing that history read as a clue and that I know was a goodbye.

He gave him the motorbike.

*Take it. I won't need it.*

The bike he loved. The letter home on wheels. The one thing he owned that meant anything at all — handed up into a giant's hands, along with the future.

People said afterwards that it was a man throwing off weight before running.

It was a man throwing off *everything*.

He had done his sums in the rain and got a total, and the total was this:

*James is lying in that hallway because of me. My plan. My clever plan. My mouth that said use Peter.*

The godfather's road — the pram, the cottage, the dying for him every day — belonged to a man who could still carry a future. And Sirius Black had decided, standing in the wet dark with the smell of the burnt house in his clothes, that he was no longer such a man.

One thing was left that he was fit for. One service he could still do for the hallway and for the little room upstairs.

Find the rat.

Hagrid rose up into the rain on a flying motorbike, with the Boy Who Lived asleep against his chest, and went south towards the lit rooms where the story you already know was beginning.

And down in the dark garden of the ruined house, alone with the two quietest people he had ever heard, the last Marauder still standing turned his face towards the city and went hunting.

I want to say one thing about that decision, because I have had thirty years to think about it.

He was wrong.

He should have kept the boy, or fought Hagrid for him, or gone to Dumbledore and shouted until somebody listened. He should have done anything except hand over his godson and walk away to be a weapon.

And I understand exactly why he did not, and if I had been standing in that garden I might have done the same thing, and Harry lost eleven years in a cupboard because of it.

That is what grief does in the first hour. It looks like a man deciding things clearly. It is the opposite.

## 43. Twelve People on a Street

It took Sirius one day to find him.

Give the hunt its due: one day, in a country this size, with the whole Ministry also out looking — for Sirius, as it happened, although he did not yet know that.

The rat-catcher was faster than all of them.

He knew Peter's habits. He knew Peter's hiding places. He knew Peter's mother's street. He had watched over Peter for ten years, and it turns out that the same knowledge serves love and revenge equally well.

It is the identical map. Only the direction of travel changes.

He cornered him at midday on the first of November, on an ordinary Muggle street in London.

Shops. Buses. Mothers with prams. The Muggle world was having its Tuesday, and — this belongs in the picture — our world was going mad with joy all round it. Owls flying in daylight. Men in cloaks drinking in the street. Shooting stars over Kent.

Because for every other witch and wizard in Britain, that was the first morning of peace.

The bells were still ringing when the last two Marauders faced each other on the pavement.

Sirius had his wand out.

Here his account and the world's account agree completely. The only difference is what everything meant.

He meant to take Peter in. Or to finish it there. Twelve years later he was honest enough to tell me that he no longer knew which, that the hallway and the glasses were the whole of his mind by then.

It did not matter. He never got to choose.

Because Peter — and now, reader, watch the small man of this book perform, one time, at the very end, in front of witnesses —

Peter chose everything.

He backed away. And he began to shout.

Not at Sirius. *For the street.* For the two dozen Muggle heads that were already turning.

"Lily and James, Sirius!"

His voice cracked and carried. Every word was placed exactly where a witness would remember it.

*"How could you? Lily and James!"*

And then, behind his back, hidden from the crowd: his own wand.

He blew the street open.

One curse into the pavement — into the gas pipe under it, the Muggle investigators decided, as far as they were allowed to decide anything.

Twelve Muggles died where they stood. Mothers. Shopkeepers. A boy with a delivery bicycle.

Twelve names that history barely kept. Killed as punctuation. Killed to finish a sentence that was putting the blame on another man.

And in the smoke and the screaming, Peter Pettigrew — who had spent three years of his childhood learning in secret, for love of a friend, to turn himself into a small unremarkable animal —

cut off his own finger,

dropped it in the wreckage,

folded up into a rat,

and went down a drain.

Into the London sewers, with the rest of the story in his teeth.

Look at the craft of it one last time. Our whole book is inside it.

The instant animal reading of the situation: the arithmetic of staying alive, done in one heartbeat, and right in every figure.

The public voice borrowed from better men. He shouted at Sirius exactly what Sirius was feeling at that moment — *how could you* — the accusation stolen before it could be made. He had borrowed courage all his life; at the end he borrowed the words too.

And the finger. Because a man who runs is hunted, but a dead man is mourned.

Peter understood, right down to the bone, that safety is not being defended. Safety is *nobody looking for you*.

Nobody had looked for him in his whole life. He turned that into a fortress.

They gave that finger the Order of Merlin, First Class, later. They sent it to his mother in a box.

## 44. No Trial

And Sirius?

When the Ministry men arrived — twenty of them, wands out, at the edge of the crater — they found the last Marauder standing in the wreck of everything.

And he was laughing.

I need you to stand still while I try to carry that laugh across to you, because it was what made everybody certain.

Everybody said the same thing. The Muggle witnesses. The wizard witnesses. Twenty Ministry men. And every time the story was told again it got worse: the Black son, the traitor, the killer of a whole street, standing among the bodies, laughing.

It closed the case before there was a case. There was no trial. Nobody ever thought one was needed.

Why would you try a man that a whole street had heard accused? At whose feet lay twelve dead people and a friend's finger? And who *laughed*?

But you have read this book.

You were on the train. You saw the boy clap for himself when nobody else would. You know that laughter was the only armour Sirius Black ever owned, and that it had been nailed onto him in a house where crying was fed to the heads on the wall.

And you know what he had just watched happen.

The friend he had offered to be hunted for — the friend whose innocence was the last thing standing in a burnt life — had in five seconds put James's and Lily's deaths onto him in front of witnesses, killed twelve people as a decoration, and escaped down a drain wearing the shape that the four of them had built together, as children, out of love, for me.

The map never lies. But the animal was a lie.

Every part of the trap he was standing in had been cut out of the wood of their friendship.

What is left in the middle of a man when that finishes arriving?

I have been near that place. On the night I learned the truth, I felt the edge of it. There is nothing there to talk to. Talking belongs to a world that still makes sense.

What came out of Sirius Black in that crater was the sound of the world's sense ending, wearing the only face his childhood had ever taught it to wear.

The newspaper printed it as the laugh of a monster.

Print this instead: it was the sound a dog makes, alone, over the ruin of its house.

They took him without a fight. I do not believe he noticed being taken.

They sent him to Azkaban that same week.

Azkaban is a fortress in the North Sea, and its guards are not men. They are creatures that feed on happiness. They take every warm thing out of a prisoner until there is nothing left but the worst hour of his life, playing over and over, for ever.

Most people do not last ten years there with their minds still working. Sirius Black was twenty-two.

No trial, reader. Mark that in the margin in your own hand: *no trial*. A signature from a Ministry man with wartime powers, and the case was closed.

The cell door shut on the boy who had knelt in that hallway at his friend's glasses.

Outside, the whole country was drinking to the end of the war, and the account was considered settled.

Black the traitor. Pettigrew the hero. Potter the miracle.

Three lies out of three.

The bells rang for days.

## 45. The Empty World

Now I have to write the chapter that I have been avoiding for forty years. Mine.

Where was I on the night of the thirty-first?

You know where. A borrowed floor in Yorkshire, an hour away, feeling sorry for myself.

I slept through the end of the world.

That is not a figure of speech. I want you to feel the plain size of it. The house fell. The street learned about it. Owls went up all over Britain.

And Moony slept. And woke up on the first of November to an ordinary ceiling, and lay there for a minute, and his last thought about the old world was that it looked like rain.

The news came the way it always comes to the person one step outside the circle. Late, second-hand, and all at once.

Not a friend at the door. A newspaper.

I stood in a shop doorway with the paper shaking in my hands. There was a photograph of the ruined cottage on the front and the headlines were stacked up like graves.

And in about ninety seconds, standing up, in public, I read the following, in this order.

That James was dead.

That Lily was dead.

That Harry was alive.

That Voldemort was gone.

And that all of it had been done — sold, arranged, betrayed — by Sirius Black.

Ninety seconds. Four names. My whole population.

Here is the thing I have never written down before.

It made sense.

That is my shame, and I am going to hold the light steady on it.

When the words *Black betrayed them* went in, something in me — not all of me, but a real part, a part with a vote — said: *ah.*

The locked drawer swung open by itself, and everything I had put in it came out in order.

The family. The blood. The brother behind the mask. The corridor at sixteen, one sentence, my own body spent like a coin. The cold half-second in a mirror in October. The visits that kept being moved. The circle drawn shut with me outside it.

I had been building the case against my brother for a year without ever saying to myself that I was building it. The newspaper asked for a verdict. And the part of me with a vote said *guilty*, and the case snapped shut with my grief inside it.

Peter's real cleverness was never the lie itself.

It was choosing the lie that we were all already halfway to believing.

He did not have to build the trap. He had watched us build it. He had watched the suspicion go back and forth between Padfoot and Moony for a year, each of us quietly mourning the other as a man who had probably gone over.

At the end he simply stepped out of the way and let the door swing shut, and went down his drain.

The frame fitted because we had cut it ourselves. All he supplied was the push.

So I believed it. Completely. For twelve years.

And do the arithmetic of what that did to my grief. I could not even mourn cleanly.

James and Lily: dead. Peter: dead too, I believed. A hero, torn to a finger for facing the traitor. And Sirius, the brother of my whole youth, not dead but worse — turned backwards, so that every memory was poisoned at the root.

The train. The Hat. The jumper. The roof. The wedding.

For twelve years, every time a good memory of him came up, I had to kill it on arrival. *That was a spy performing.*

I mourned three friends and executed the memory of the fourth, daily, for twelve years.

And he was innocent in a cage in the sea the entire time, keeping himself sane — he told me this afterwards — on one single thought.

*I know that I am innocent. They cannot eat that.*

Two men in two prisons, both of them furnished by Peter Pettigrew.

## 46. Twelve Years

What was left of my life picked itself up slowly, and I will give it to you in one chapter, because this book is about four of us and there were no longer four of us.

Dumbledore spoke for me where he could. The werewolf laws did the rest.

Work found and lost. Found and lost. A classroom in a small private school for one term. A warehouse. A bookshop. Once, for four months, a job putting somebody's library in order, which was the best of them and which I still think about.

Every time, the calendar betrayed me in the end. You cannot be ill for three days every month for ever without somebody putting the dates together. And in our world, when they put the dates together, they do not sack you politely. They stand a little further back while they do it.

Rooms rented near the tops of stairs, because the tops of stairs are cheap.

Clothes that got older faster than I did.

And the moons. Alone again, in locked sheds and cellars, with the landlord told some story about a nervous illness.

No stag. No dog. No small mad captain riding between the antlers. The wolf woke up each month and found its pack gone, and it took the news out on my body, exactly as it had done when I was six years old.

I was twenty-two. Then I was thirty. Then I was thirty-three.

If you had asked me in those years what I was, I would have said: the one who was left. The keeper of a story with no living listeners, and the central fact of it wrong.

And Harry?

Harry grew up an hour away and a world away. Behind old protective magic, in his aunt's house. In a cupboard under the stairs, as it turned out, although nobody found that out for a long time.

Dumbledore's protection kept the danger out. Nobody checked what it was keeping in. That is a bill of Dumbledore's own and people are still arguing about it.

I did not go to him.

Put that in the margin too, in the honest column. The last friend did not go to the boy.

There were reasons. Good ones. Dumbledore's plan. The old magic in the house. The werewolf laws — what claim did a monster on a calendar have on a child who was being kept safe by his distance from our burnt world?

Twelve years of excellent reasons.

It is remarkable, I have found, how the finest reasons always arrive exactly between us and the door we are afraid to knock on.

Peter taught me that about cowardice in the end. Mine wore better clothes than his, and cost less, and I have never entirely stopped hearing his voice on the subject.

I want to put down one more thing about those twelve years, because otherwise this chapter is only sad, and it was not only sad.

In 1985 I was living in a room in Bristol and I had no work at all. There was a family downstairs — Muggles — with a boy of about seven who could not read.

His mother worked nights. I taught him. Two hours every afternoon for most of a year, at the kitchen table, with the radio on.

He is a grown man now with children of his own. He does not know a single true thing about me, including my name; I gave a false one, as I always did in those years.

But he can read.

I did not save anybody in the war. I made speeches to men in camps who joined the other side anyway. I was not there on the night my friends died, and I believed a lie about my brother for twelve years.

That boy in Bristol can read.

If you are keeping accounts for me at the end of this book, and I suspect you are — put that on the other side of the page.

Twelve years.

And then, one summer morning in 1993, a newspaper printed a photograph of a family on holiday in Egypt. Red-headed, cheerful, nine of them squinting in the sun.

And a boy at the front, with a rat on his shoulder.

And in a cell in the North Sea, a man who had kept one thought alive for a decade looked at that front page. At the small unremarkable animal in the picture.

And saw that it was missing a toe.

## 47. A Teacher Again

Sirius Black escaped from Azkaban in the summer of 1993.

Nobody had ever done it. The guards of that place do not sleep, and what they eat cannot be hidden from them, because they eat happiness.

What saved him, in the end, was the exact shape of his ruin.

His one sustaining thought — *I am innocent. Peter is alive. The rat is with the boy* — was not a happy thought. The guards had no appetite for it. It was cold and pointed, and it lay in his cell like a nail.

When the photograph from Egypt reached him, the nail became a compass.

The rat on the boy's shoulder. One toe short. Going back to Hogwarts in September with the red-headed family's youngest son.

Into the same school as Harry.

He turned himself into a dog, walked out through the bars as a starved black dog, and swam the North Sea.

That same autumn, Dumbledore offered me a job teaching Defence Against the Dark Arts.

I have wondered ever since how much of that offer was kindness and how much was a move on a chessboard. The old man putting the one person who knew Sirius Black's mind, his tricks and his shapes, between the escaped murderer and the boy.

Both, of course. Knowing Dumbledore, it was always both.

So I went back.

Twelve years a shabby stranger, and then one September morning I walked in under that enchanted ceiling again, and taught in the same rooms where the four of us had passed notes, to the children of the people we had fought beside and buried.

And I met Harry.

He was thirteen. He had James's face and James's impossible hair, and James's exact way of standing when he had decided that something was worth being punished for.

And he had Lily's eyes. Which meant that every conversation I had with that boy was held under the gaze of both of my dead at once.

He did not know who I was. That was correct. The excellent reasons still held. I was Professor Lupin, a stranger, who taught him to fight fear and found that he was good at it — better than his father had been, because the boy had far more practice at being afraid.

I taught him the guardian charm against the soul-eaters. He was thirteen and it should have been impossible.

His took the shape of a stag.

I stood behind him the first time Prongs came silver out of that boy's wand. I am still not able to put that moment into a sentence, so I am going to leave it standing in the doorway of one and walk on.

All that year, Sirius circled the school as a dog, hunting the rat.

And all that year I said nothing about what the dog might be.

How could I explain what I knew without confessing what the four of us had been, and what I still was, and how completely the map of my own history had been turned over?

The old silence. The prefect with his book. Quiet cowardice in better clothes.

It very nearly killed the boy.

Let every account of that year say it plainly, including mine, especially mine: the truth arrived in spite of me, not because of me.

## 48. The Shrieking Shack

It arrived in June.

I was in my office, packing, because I had already decided to leave at the end of that year for reasons of my own.

And on my desk was a piece of old parchment that I had taken from Harry two months earlier, and had not been able to make myself destroy.

Our map.

I touched it with my wand out of habit, the way you touch an old photograph, and I looked at the little moving footprints in the grounds.

Harry. Ron Weasley. Hermione Granger. A large dog.

And walking beside them, in the neat green letters of our own sixteen-year-old handwriting:

PETER PETTIGREW.

I ran out of the castle without a cloak.

Down through the wet grass, past the tree, down the tunnel I had gone down alone once a month for seven years. Up the broken stairs of the old house where every wall already knew us.

And in the room at the top, for one hour, the Marauders held their last meeting.

Moony, thirteen years older, out of breath in a doorway.

Padfoot, thin as a skeleton, with his wand on a boy's pet rat.

And Wormtail — dragged out of that rat and back into a soft, balding man with wet eyes. Twelve years grown fat on a kind family's kitchen. Pleading in a voice I knew from a train compartment.

"Remus — Sirius — my old friends —"

He confessed everything. Cornered, with our wands on him, in front of Harry's eyes.

The listening. The leaning. The address spoken out loud in a dark place. The street. The finger. The drain.

And there is one thing I must tell you, because it is the last honest thing this book can say about Peter Pettigrew.

He did not argue that he was innocent. Not for one second.

He argued that he had been afraid.

He went round the room on his knees. He went to Sirius, who did not move. He went to me — *Remus, you were always the kind one* — and I did not move either.

And then he went to Harry.

To Harry. Thirteen years old, standing against the wall in his school clothes, looking at the man who sold his mother.

*"Harry — Harry, you look just like your father, just like James — we were the best of friends —"*

Sirius said, "How dare you speak to Harry."

And that was the moment when I understood, completely and for ever, what he was. Not in 1981. Not on the street in London.

Then. On his knees in that room, reaching for the one person in it who had lost the most, because the boy was the softest thing available and Peter had spent his entire life finding the softest thing in the room and standing next to it.

He was not asking Harry for forgiveness.

He was asking Harry for shelter.

And standing over him, the last two friends of James Potter got ready — together, at last in perfect agreement about something — to commit the murder that the whole world had been punishing the wrong man for.

## 49. Harry Stops Us

Harry stopped us.

Thirteen years old. He stepped between our wands and the man on the floor and said no.

Not out of pity for Peter. He was very exact about it, and I have kept the words:

*"I don't reckon my dad would have wanted his best friends to become killers — just for you."*

James's son. In the house of the wolf. Teaching the wolf what James would have wanted.

We lowered our wands. Both of us. Because the voice that told us to was, in every way that has ever mattered to me, the voice of the man in the hallway.

I have been asked since whether I regret it. Whether, if I had that hour again, I would look at a thirteen-year-old boy and say: stand aside, this is not yours to decide.

No. And I will tell you why, and it is not a noble reason.

If we had killed him in that room, Sirius would have died a murderer in fact as well as in name, and I would have spent whatever was left of my life knowing that the last thing the four of us ever did together was that.

Harry did not save Peter Pettigrew's life that night. He saved ours. Such as they were.

What happened next you may already know.

We came down out of that house in a strange, shining procession — Peter tied between me and Ron, Sirius half carrying Harry, everybody talking at once about tomorrow, about the Ministry, about the room Harry would have.

And the moon came up.

That night. Of all the nights of my life, that night.

I had not taken my potion. I had run out of the castle without it, because of a name on a piece of parchment.

I do not remember what happened after that. I never do.

I know that Sirius changed and fought me to keep the children alive. I know that in the noise Peter folded up small one more time and was gone into the dark grass.

Alive. Free. On his way — although none of us knew it that night — to the master he would help to build again, and to the further harm that lies past the last page of this book.

So the truth won nothing that night, in the world's accounts.

Sirius was still a hunted man. I resigned my post in the morning, because by breakfast the school knew what I was and the letters from parents had already started.

And the rat ran off to his reward.

Put that night beside Halloween 1981 and the pattern is exactly the same. Peter escapes. The innocent pay. The world goes on believing the wrong thing. Twelve years later, just as before.

And yet.

I have never once counted that night as a defeat, and I will tell you what was in it that outweighs the accounts.

In one hour, in a broken house, I got my brother back.

Out of the drawer where I had been executing him daily for twelve years. Alive. Innocent. His hand in mine over Peter's confession. The case against my own memory thrown out.

Harry got the truth about his father, and a godfather who loved him. They had two years — letters, and one or two stolen visits, and a single shining hour of *come and live with me*. Too short. But not nothing. Never tell me it was nothing.

And the four of us got, at last, what this whole book has been.

An accounting. Who stood where. Who opened which door. What the laughing meant. What the silence cost.

The truth arrived thirteen years late, on the last possible night, through our own old map, in a child's hands.

It has never once been early, in my experience.

But I have stopped believing that it fails to arrive.

## 50. What I Know About Friendship

It is late now, in every sense. The tea has gone cold. And I owe you an ending.

You have had the story: 1971 to 1994, the train to the Shack. If you came here for the war, the war is told.

But I promised you at the beginning that this was a book about how a friendship breaks. And a promise to a reader is a small Fidelius Charm: it only holds if the keeper chooses to tell.

So here, instead of an ending, is everything the four of us taught me. Three things. Take what is useful and leave the rest on the table with the cold tea.

**One. The love was real. All of it, the whole time, including his.**

I have checked that answer for forty years, the way you check a knot that once failed. It holds.

Peter loved us. The boy who cried when Hagrid cried, who gave me his last sweets, who drew eight hundred doors without complaining — that boy was not a mask over a monster. He was exactly what he looked like. A loving, frightened, ordinary person.

The horror is not that his love was false.

The horror is that love, in a person who has never once chosen the hard thing, weighs nothing at all at the moment of pressure. It is weather. It is warmth with no structure in it.

When they came for him, his love for us had no vote. Because a vote has to be *cast*, and casting costs something, and he had never in his life practised paying.

So watch — this is the only advice in this book, so I will say it slowly — watch what your people have paid for, not what they feel. Feeling is free.

**Two. The brave broke too, and each of us differently.**

I must be fair about the ways.

James's certainty — *nothing gets in* — could not imagine a door opening from the inside. His trust had no locks on it, and trust with no locks is how you end up in a hallway with your wand on the sofa.

Sirius's cleverness built a perfect machine on one wrong fact. Cleverness does that. It is always the fact holding up the weight that gives way.

And my patience — the politest cowardice in Gryffindor — sat quietly through every year in which the truth needed shouting. Nearby. With a book. Saying nothing.

Four friends. Four failures, one of them a crime.

That is the honest arithmetic of most disasters. One betrayal, standing on three ordinary human weaknesses that opened the road for it.

Judge Peter. I do. But if you close this book blaming only Peter, then I have wasted our winter evenings. His one push knocked us over because we had already, each in his own loving way, built the fall.

**Three. Suspicion is not the same as watching.**

For one whole year every one of us was watchful. And the watching itself was the wound the enemy used.

Two innocent men each decided that the other was the traitor, on evidence. Real evidence. Better evidence than anybody ever had against the man who did it.

Distrust does not find traitors. It makes them. It eats exactly the thing that would have caught the real one.

If any two of us in that sealed year had said out loud the sentence that could not be said — *I have a drawer with your name in it; show me I am wrong; here is mine* — the whole machine stops.

The rat's way of staying safe is the question nobody asks.

That is the accounting. What is left?

This is left.

It is a spring morning as I finish these pages, and on the desk in front of me lies a piece of old parchment. It was taken from us by a caretaker once. It is carried now by a boy with his mother's eyes.

If I touch my wand to it and say the old words — *I solemnly swear that I am up to no good* — four names come up in green ink across the top, in the handwriting of four sixteen-year-olds who sat up past midnight inventing themselves.

Moony. Wormtail. Padfoot. Prongs.

Harry asked me once why we never took Wormtail's name off it.

The answer is that the map never lies. And the map says what we were. Four.

Not three and a mistake. Four boys, one round tower, the best years of a bad century. The stag, the dog, the wolf and the rat, shoulder to shoulder on a hilltop above a sleeping village. Alive. All of it real, before any of the paying came due.

The truth does not subtract. It only adds.

What Peter became belongs to the map of the war. What Peter was belongs to ours. I am the keeper of both now, and a keeper — you have read this book, you know what that job costs — a keeper holds all of it, or he is not a keeper at all.

So: four names.

I am the last of them above ground and out of hiding. The moon still finds me every month. And on the bad nights, the very bad ones, I light the lamp that James bought for a sick boy in 1971, and I spread the map out, and I watch the little named footprints of children moving along corridors that we measured ourselves.

And I say the four names, in order, all four. And I do not explain to anybody why the fourth.

This book is the explanation.

Friendship was the country I was born in. It is burnt, and I will not give up my citizenship.

Mischief managed. Put the lamp out gently, whoever is last.

# СЛОВАРЬ И СПРАВКА

## Как читать эту книгу

Книга написана простым английским, но это настоящая проза, а не учебник. Несколько советов:

- **Не переводи каждое слово.** Сначала прочитай абзац целиком. Обычно смысл понятен из истории. Коснись слова, только если без него никак.
- **Повторы — не случайность.** Ключевые слова (*betray, trust, secret, moon, brave, promise*) возвращаются из главы в главу. К концу книги ты будешь знать их навсегда.
- **Врезка «New Words»** в конце главы — самые важные слова этой главы. Просмотри их до или после чтения.
- **Кнопка перевода у абзаца** покажет русский перевод целиком. Полезно проверить себя после трудного места.
- **Главы короткие** — по пять-семь минут. Две-три за вечер лучше, чем десять за раз.

## Про уровень языка

В книге около полутора тысяч разных английских слов — это словарь уровня A2. Предложения короткие, времена самые обычные: почти везде Past Simple.

Редкие слова в книге всё-таки есть, но это слова её мира: *wand, werewolf, spell, traitor, prophecy*. Они возвращаются десятки раз, и специально учить их не нужно — они выучатся сами.

Уровень одинаковый от первой главы до последней. Если первая глава далась, дастся и пятидесятая.

## Три вывода Римуса

Если из всей книги останется три мысли, пусть это будут они — Римус говорит их в последней главе:

1. **Любовь без поступка ничего не весит.** Смотри не на то, что человек чувствует, а на то, чем он когда-либо заплатил.
2. **Сломались все четверо, каждый по-своему.** Доверие без замков, ум, построенный на одном неверном факте, и вежливая трусость молчания — на них и держалось предательство.
