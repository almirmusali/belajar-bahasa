# THE GREATER GOOD

### The confession of Albus Dumbledore — intermediate English

---

## Читателю

Вы знаете этого человека. Длинная борода, очки-половинки, самый мудрый волшебник на свете, который всегда появляется в нужный момент и говорит правильные слова. Семь книг подряд он знал ответы на все вопросы.

Эта книга — о том, о чём он молчал. В «Дарах Смерти» Роулинг приоткрыла дверь: юный Дамблдор, гениальный и голодный до славы, влюбился в красивого тёмного юношу по имени Геллерт Гриндевальд. Два месяца они строили планы завоевания мира — «ради общего блага». Потом была дуэль на троих, мёртвая четырнадцатилетняя сестра на полу гостиной — и никто никогда не узнал, чьё заклятие её убило. Гриндевальд сбежал, развязал в Европе войну и сорок пять лет ждал, пока единственный волшебник, способный его остановить, найдёт в себе силы прийти. А Дамблдор всё не шёл. Почему — он признался лишь однажды, в самом конце. Здесь он рассказывает всё сам, от первого лица: старик с мёртвой чёрной рукой сидит ночью в кабинете и пишет единственную правдивую историю своей жизни. Это не «Фантастические твари» — здесь нет погонь и чудовищ. Только человек, его вина и почти сто лет молчания.

**Как читать эту книгу.** Никаких правил здесь нет — это роман, а не учебник. Просто читай историю. Всё остальное — советы, и каждый можно нарушать.

Читай абзацами, а не словами. Наткнулся на незнакомое слово — иди дальше: чаще всего смысл догоняет через строчку. Если слово всё-таки не отпускает — коснись его, и появится перевод. Переведено каждое слово книги, так что застрять невозможно.

Кнопка перевода рядом с абзацем — запасной выход, а не часть ритуала. Жми её, когда совсем потерял нить, и спокойно двигайся дальше. Важные слова этой книги — *guilt, power, love, forgive, silence* — сами вернутся к тебе десятки раз, и где-то к середине ты заметишь, что читаешь их не переводя. Так и задумано.

Иногда — не каждый раз, а под настроение — прослушай абзац кнопкой озвучки. Услышать фразу голосом — совсем другое дело, чем увидеть глазами: слова начинают звучать в голове, когда читаешь дальше сам.

Понравилось слово — поставь звёздочку. Оно уйдёт в «Избранное», и потом его можно погонять карточками в разделе «Словарь». Но это на потом; сейчас важнее история.

Эта книга чуть сложнее «Мародёров»: она начинается с уровня B1 и растёт вместе с тобой до лёгкого B2. Если местами трудно — это нормально: трудно и должно быть ровно настолько, чтобы было интересно. Одна-две главы за вечер — лучший темп: закладка запомнит место, полоска прогресса покажет, сколько позади.

Открывай первую главу. Старик уже обмакнул перо.

## People in this Story

**Albus Dumbledore** — the man who writes this book. The cleverest wizard of his age. He is dying, and he has one story left to tell.

**Gellert Grindelwald** — a golden-haired boy from Europe, later the most dangerous dark wizard in the world. The love of Albus's life. The worst mistake of it too.

**Ariana Dumbledore** — Albus's little sister. Broken by Muggle boys at six, hidden by her family ever after. She likes goats and humming. Remember her laugh.

**Aberforth Dumbledore** — Albus's younger brother. Not clever; right. He loves Ariana simply, and he will never forgive what happened to her.

**Kendra Dumbledore** — their mother. Proud, stern, exhausted. She holds the family together until she cannot.

**Percival Dumbledore** — their father. He did a terrible thing for a good reason and died in prison keeping the secret.

**Bathilda Bagshot** — the kind, talkative historian next door. One summer her great-nephew comes to stay.

**Elphias Doge** — Albus's plain, loyal school friend. He never asks the one question. That is his gift.

**Harry Potter** — a boy with a scar who appears near the end, standing in front of a mirror Albus knows too well.

## Part One: The Cleverest Boy in the World

*Every man has one story he tells about himself, and one story that is true. For a hundred years I have told the first kind, and I told it beautifully, because telling things beautifully was always my gift. This part is about a boy who was cleverer than everyone around him, and about what that cost the people who loved him. I was that boy, and I am not asking you to like him.*

## 1. The Hand

It is late at night, and the castle is asleep. I am not. Old men sleep badly, and dying men sleep worse. This year I have become both, which at least shows a certain economy.

My office is quiet. The small silver instruments on the shelves turn and click and breathe their little clouds of smoke. Nobody knows what most of them do. That was always the idea. Fawkes, my phoenix, sleeps on his perch with his head under one wing. The fire is low. A cup of tea stands by my elbow. It went cold an hour ago, because for an hour I have done nothing except look at my hand.

Let me describe my hand, because this book begins with it.

My right hand is black. Not bruised, not stained with ink — black and dead, like a glove pulled out of a fire. The fingers still move when I ask them politely. The skin feels nothing, not the quill, not the cold, not the warmth of the cup. The blackness ends at the wrist. For now. It is climbing, slowly, the way night climbs a hill.

A very clever man — a better man than most people believe — trapped the curse inside this one hand. He bought me time. About one year, he says. He does not say it kindly, because kindness is not his way. But he says it honestly, and honesty is worth more.

One year. I am one hundred and fifteen years old. I have one year left, and I find I am not afraid. I am something else, something I did not expect. I am in a hurry.

People call me the greatest wizard alive. They may be right; I have met the other candidates. They have given me many titles. Grand Sorcerer. Chief Warlock. Supreme Mugwump of the International Confederation of Wizards. Order of Merlin, First Class. I could fill a page with them, and the page would tell you nothing true about me at all.

Here is something true instead. I have been writing all my life. I have written thousands of letters, hundreds of papers, dozens of speeches. I once wrote a famous defence of a friend in eight languages, and I checked all eight myself, because I did not trust anyone else's grammar. Words have always come to me the way birds come to a quiet garden. And in a hundred years of words, I have never once written the true story. I have walked around it. I have pointed at it from a distance. I have decorated its locked door.

Tonight I am going to open the door.

I do not know who this book is for. That is an odd thing for a writer to admit, but I promised myself honesty, and I may as well begin now. Perhaps it is for my brother, who lives one village away and has not truly spoken to me for almost a hundred years. He would read three pages and feed the rest to his goats, and he would be within his rights. Perhaps it is for a boy I know, who has green eyes and too much weight on his shoulders, and who believes I am wise. Perhaps it is for a man in a tower far away, in a cell at the top of a prison, who is the only person left alive who was in the room. Or perhaps it is for no one. Dying men write letters with no address. It is one of our privileges.

I will tell you what the story is, so that you may decide whether to stay.

People call me the greatest wizard alive. Very well. I am going to tell you about the summer when I was seventeen. I am going to tell you about a girl who died on the floor of my mother's sitting room, and nobody ever learned whose spell killed her. And I am going to tell you about the man I loved, and did not fight for forty years. When I am finished, you may choose your own word for me. I have spent my life choosing words for other people. It is only fair.

Do not expect a sad book. I am not sad, most days. I have laughed a great deal in my life, often at the right things, and I intend to laugh in these pages too. A confession without laughter is only self-pity wearing black. My sister laughed more easily than anyone I have ever known, and this book belongs to her more than to anyone. It would be poor manners to make it gloomy.

Do not expect excuses either. I know how clever men confess. They kneel down so gracefully that everyone applauds the kneeling and forgets the crime. I have read many such books, and I have written small versions of them in a hundred conversations. Watch me carefully. When I start being graceful, do not trust me. It is an old habit, and old habits do not die just because their owner is dying.

There is one more thing you should know before we begin, because everything grows from it.

I was clever. I was clever the way some people are tall — without effort, without permission, visibly, from across a road. Before I was ten I had read every book in my father's house. At school I answered questions the teachers had not asked yet. All my life, in every room, I was the cleverest person present, and I knew it.

People assume such a mind is a blessing. It is a tool, like a wand. A wand does not care what it is pointed at. Neither, I am sorry to say, does a mind. My cleverness has served kindness, and it has served vanity, and once, for two months of one summer, it served something much worse, and it worked equally well every time. That is the frightening part. That is the part I most need you to understand.

For nearly a century, children have sat across this desk from me and believed that I am wise. Wisdom, I have found, is mostly a collection of scars with good manners. The young cannot tell the difference between a wise man and a careful one. I have been careful. Oh, I have been enormously careful. This book is about what happened the one time I was not, and about the years of care that followed, which the world mistook for greatness.

I will write at night, I think, through this last school year. The days are not mine; a headmaster's day belongs to everyone who knocks. But after midnight the knocking stops, and the castle holds its breath, and a man with one year and one story can do steady work. A chapter at a time. A truth at a time. The left hand is still new to this work, and the handwriting will get worse as the truth gets larger, which seems fitting.

You may wonder why I do not simply tell someone. Sit them down, pour the tea, speak. I have considered it. But speech is my oldest instrument, and I no longer trust myself with it. Spoken words can be softened mid-flight; I have watched myself do it for a century — the pause, the small joke, the graceful turn away from the cliff. Ink does not turn. Ink stays where you put it, and it dries, and in the morning it says exactly what you wrote at midnight. For a man with my talents, paper is the only honest room.

Fawkes has lifted his head and is watching me. Phoenixes do not judge; perhaps that is why I keep one. But they watch, and there is something in being watched by a creature that has died many times and does not fear it. Steadying. Like a hand on the shoulder — but I am ahead of myself. Hands on shoulders come later in this story, and they cost more than you would think.

The castle is silent. Somewhere below me, several hundred children are asleep, and they are safe, and I have spent my whole life making sure of that one small sentence. It is the best thing I have done. It is not the reason I am famous. The things I am famous for and the things I am proud of are two lists with almost nothing in common, and I suspect that this is true of most old men, and that most old men will not tell you.

I am telling you.

My black hand aches tonight, a deep, slow ache, like a bell heard underwater. The healers say I should rest it, and there is a small joke in that, for resting is all it does now. It was my writing hand. I have taught the left one its work, and the right lies on the page and holds it flat, and I find it fitting — I will not yet say why — that this story should be written under the eye of a burnt hand. There is a reason. It waits at the very end of the book, in a ruined house, in a little box of dust, in a ring. I ask for your patience. Even a dying man may keep one or two secrets until the proper page.

So. The ink is mixed. The quill is a good one, a gift from a student, and it has waited years for a task worth doing. The tea is beyond saving, and I will make no more, because this next hour belongs to the truth, and truth should not be interrupted for kettles.

Where does one start the true story, after a century of false ones?

Not with the duel the world still sings about. Not with the prison in the mountains, nor the words carved over its gate — my words, but we will come to that. Not even with the golden-haired boy who climbed over a garden wall one June morning and changed everything, though my hand — the living one — trembles a little just from writing this much about him, which after all these years is information in itself.

No. All of those are middles pretending to be beginnings.

I dip the quill. Fawkes settles his head back under his wing. The instruments tick on, measuring things no one else will ever measure, and the night is long enough, just barely, for the first chapter of the truth.

Let me begin where all true stories begin — with a family.

## 2. Mould-on-the-Wold

Our house stood at the edge of a small village called Mould-on-the-Wold. I was born there in the summer of 1881, and for ten years I was happy there, though of course I did not know it. Happiness is like health: you only see it clearly from a distance, when it is gone.

It was not a grand house. It leaned a little to the left, the stairs sang under your feet, and the kitchen was the warmest room, so the kitchen was where we lived. When I try to imagine peace — and I have tried, in some very dark years — I do not imagine mountains or the sea. I imagine that kitchen on a winter evening, with the kettle on, and all five of us inside, and the doors unlocked. Remember that last detail. The doors unlocked. There was a time when my family did not lock anything at all.

My father was called Percival. He was a big man with a big laugh, the kind of laugh that made strangers in shops turn around and smile without knowing why. He carried his wand behind his ear, like a carpenter carries a pencil, which my mother considered common and my sister considered the funniest thing in the world. He was not a brilliant wizard. He was a good one, in both senses, and there is a lesson in the fact that it took me fifty years to see the difference between those two words, brilliant and good, and to want the second one more.

My mother, Kendra, was tall and dark-haired and proud, with the straightest back in the county. She had come up from nothing, and had been given nothing, and out of that nothing she had built a family the way a general builds a defence. She loved us fiercely and economically. I do not remember her ever saying the word love. I remember her sitting up all night when I had dragon pox, and sewing until her fingers bled so that I would not look poor beside the other boys. It has taken me a lifetime to translate her language, and now that I speak it, there is no one left to speak it to.

Then came my brother, Aberforth, three years younger than I. He was born stubborn. As a baby he refused food he had not seen prepared. As a boy of five he argued with our father about the right way to stack wood, and he was correct, which was somehow typical. He preferred animals to people from the beginning, and I am no longer sure he was wrong. Animals, he told me once, do not say one thing and mean another. He looked at me while he said it. He was about nine at the time, and it remains one of the most accurate reviews of my character that I have ever received.

And then there was Ariana.

I must be careful here. I have had a hundred years of silence in which to turn my sister into a sad story, a lesson, a ghost. She was none of those things. She was a small quick girl with our mother's dark eyes, and she was — I insist on this, against everything that came later — the brightest of us all. Not the cleverest. Cleverest was my job, and I guarded it jealously. Ariana was bright the way a morning is bright. She laughed more easily than any of us, at the goats, at the rain, at my father's terrible jokes, at absolutely nothing at all. When she hummed — and she hummed all day, little tunes she invented and forgot — the whole house seemed to hum with her.

She and Aberforth belonged to each other from the start, in the easy way of children close in age. He built her things — a swing, a crooked birdhouse, a pen for the two goats he had talked our father into buying. She followed him around the garden with her fists full of dandelions for the goats, and he let her do the feeding, and stood by with the patience he never once showed a human being. I was the brother she admired. He was the brother she trusted. If you think a child cannot tell the difference, you have forgotten being a child.

Her magic came early and softly. Some children break things when the magic first stirs in them; I did — windows, mostly, and once, memorably, a church bell. Ariana made bubbles. Perfectly round, slow, shining bubbles that drifted out of her hands when she was happy and would not burst even when the cat bit them. She made the spoons dance at breakfast, a solemn line of them, marching around the milk jug while she conducted with one finger. And when she overdid it — when the magic ran a little hot, and the bubbles came too fast — there was a smell in the air like burnt sugar. Sweet, and slightly wrong, and gone in a moment.

I ask you to remember that smell. I will not ask it kindly the next time it appears.

And I? What was I, in that warm unlocked house?

I was the clever one. I had decided this early, the way other boys decide to be brave or tall. By seven I had read my father's shelf of spellbooks, understanding perhaps a third and memorising all of it. By nine I was correcting the village charm-mender, out loud, in front of customers, and enjoying it. Enjoy is the honest word. Knowledge came easily to me, but that was never the point. The point was the little silence that fell when I explained something, and the way grown wizards looked at each other over my head. The point, I am afraid, was applause. It usually was, with me. This book will say that more than once, so you may as well hear it early.

One evening stands for a hundred others. I was ten. I had learned — from a book, alone, which was my favourite way — to turn a teacup into a mouse and back. This is not remarkable magic for a grown wizard. For a boy of ten, without a wand of his own, using our father's borrowed one, it was close to impossible, and I knew exactly how impossible it was, and I chose my moment with the care of a theatre manager.

Supper was ending. The lamps were lit. I cleared my throat — I had practised clearing my throat — and performed my miracle.

The cup shivered, folded in on itself, and sat up: a small grey mouse with a handle-shaped curl in its tail. My mother put down her sewing. My father let out that great laugh and slapped the table so hard the plates jumped. Ariana clapped her hands and shrieked with joy, and the mouse, alarmed, ran a full circle around the table before I turned it back into a cup — which, out of pure luck, I managed on the first try.

Aberforth, aged seven, watched all of it and rolled his eyes. Then he went back to feeding crusts to the dog. One artist in the family, his eyes said, was already one too many.

My mother let the mouse business pass with one raised eyebrow, which in Kendra's language was a parade in my honour. Later, drying dishes, she said only: "Clever is a tool, Albus. What will you make with it?" I had no answer. I thought it was a strange question. I thought clever was the thing you made. It has taken me the length of this whole book to answer her, and the answer is on the last page, and she is not here to check my work.

He was not jealous — Aberforth, I mean. I want to be precise, because it matters later. Aberforth never once wanted what I had. He simply saw, earlier than anyone, what the applause was doing to me, the way a farmer sees weather coming. He had no words for it at seven. By the time he had the words, there had been a funeral, and he used his fist instead.

But that came later. That came so much later. First there were years, real years, of the kettle and the spoons and the burnt-sugar sweetness, of my father whistling in the garden and my mother's straight back at the stove, of three children growing in a house too small for secrets because we did not have any yet.

I did not know I would spend the rest of my life trying to return to that kitchen. No one tells you when you are inside your golden age. There is no bell, no owl, no letter with a seal, saying: pay attention, this is the part you will ache for. Happiness keeps no records. I have an office upstairs full of instruments that can measure nearly everything, and not one of them can find its way back to a Tuesday evening in 1889 when nothing whatever happened, and everyone I loved was in one room, alive.

The village itself was a Muggle village, with a handful of wizarding families folded quietly into it. On market days my father sold what our garden grew, and joked with the farmers, and was liked. We children were simply "the Dumbledore lot," a little odd, decently behaved. Ariana was too small for school and I refused to go, having read past the schoolmaster by six. So the village saw us at the edges — at the market, at the pond in summer — and what it saw, it approved of, and what it did not see, it did not imagine. That is the peace we lived inside. It was made partly of kindness and partly of a hedge.

Our garden ran behind the house, long and a little wild, down to where my mother grew vegetables in stubborn rows. Around all of it stood the hedge.

It was a good hedge, thick and green, taller than my father. It kept the wind off the beans and the neighbours' eyes off our oddness — for even in a friendly village, a wizard family keeps a certain distance. Muggle children from the lane sometimes rustled along the far side of it, hunting birds' nests, and their voices came through the leaves like voices in another world, and none of us thought anything of it at all.

Ariana liked the garden better than any room in the house. It was where the bubbles flew best, she said. No ceiling.

She was six years old, and the hedge was just a hedge, and I ask you — because this is the last page on which such a thing is possible — to leave her there a moment: a small girl in a summer garden, humming, her hands full of light.

## 3. The Hedge

The summer Ariana turned six, I was nearly ten, and I had discovered the village bookseller. He was a Muggle, and most of his stock was of no use to me, but he kept a shelf of old natural histories, and he let me read on the premises because I dusted the shelves in return. That is where I was, on the afternoon it happened. It is important that you know where I was. I was two streets away, sitting on a ladder, reading about bees.

I have been told, my whole life, by every person who ever learned the story, that a boy of nine at a bookshop is guilty of nothing. This is true. I knew it then and I know it now, and I can write it here in fair round letters: it was not my fault. Now watch how little that sentence weighs. There are scales inside a man where the verdict not guilty changes nothing at all. My whole family, in different ways, spent the rest of our lives learning that.

This is what happened. I have assembled it from what my father was told, from what my mother would say, and from the very few times Ariana herself could bear to speak of it. It is short. The worst stories usually are.

Ariana was alone in the garden, in the late afternoon, doing her bubble-magic. The day was hot, and the lane beyond the hedge was full of children out of school. Three boys — Muggle boys, from the village — heard her laughing and looked through the hedge, where the branches thinned near the gate.

Understand what they saw. A little girl with her hands cupped, and out of her hands, slow silver bubbles rising, bubbles that did not burst, bubbles that circled her head like planets and lit up when she giggled. They saw real magic, done for joy, by a child too young to know it needed hiding.

At first they were amazed. I hold on to that. For one minute, perhaps two, three boys stood with their mouths open and watched my sister the way you watch fireworks. If the world had been built slightly differently — and I have spent a great part of my life trying to rebuild it so — that is where the story would end. Wonder, and home for supper.

But wonder in the unprepared turns quickly, and it usually turns to fear. What they were seeing was impossible, and the impossible does not fit inside a nine-year-old's head, and what does not fit, frightens. And fear in boys — fear in men, fear in whole nations; I have watched it since on every scale there is — fear casts about for something it can do. Fear wants its hands busy.

They pushed through the hedge and told her to do it again.

She could not, of course. Magic in a child is not a tap. It is closer to laughter, or tears: it comes when it comes. Before her the bubbles had risen out of happiness, and the happiness was gone now, because three big boys were standing over her in her own garden, too close, and their voices had that edge that children hear long before they can name it. She tried. I am told she tried. Nothing came, or almost nothing — a flicker, a spark that stung one of them, or startled him. And to them that flicker was proof of trickery, or witchcraft, or simply of a thing that would not obey. Each boy alone would have gone home. Together — together they were brave, in the way that is the exact opposite of bravery.

They wanted to stop what frightened them. They were nine and ten and twelve. They stopped her.

I will not write more than that about those minutes, and I ask you not to imagine more. Restraint here is not delicacy. It is the last protection I can offer her, a century too late, and I intend to offer it.

My mother found her by the hedge before sunset. She was awake. She was not visibly hurt, or not badly. She did not cry, which my mother said afterwards was the moment her own heart stopped believing in comfort: a six-year-old who does not cry has gone somewhere tears do not reach.

She did not speak that evening, or that week. When she finally spoke again she was quieter, and she never again in her life spoke loudly. Loud voices — any loud voice, even a laughing one, even mine — made her flinch and go white, so that our whole family learned, without ever discussing it, to live at half volume. Doors were closed gently in our house forever after. If you had visited us years later you would have thought us a family of remarkably soft-spoken people. Most habits of gentleness, I have since found, are built around a wound.

And she stopped doing magic. That was the worst of it, though it took us time to understand, because at first it looked almost like healing. No more bubbles, no more marching spoons — well, she was frightened; it would come back. It did not come back. She had connected the magic to what was done to her — they had wanted to stop it, and so she would stop it — and she pressed it down, the way you press down a scream in the night, with everything she had.

But magic is not a scream. Magic is a river.

You may dam a river. The river does not apologise and go away. It rises. It gathers behind the dam, quiet, patient, heavier every day, and it waits for the weak place in the wall. A wizard who uses magic spends it, the way a healthy body spends its strength. A wizard who cannot — who dare not — carries it, and it grows, and it looks for the way out. In all the literature — and God forgive me, I read all the literature, twice, in three languages, before I was seventeen — there is no cure for a child who has locked the river behind a door and lost the key. There is only weather: calm days, and the other kind.

Could anything have been done, in those five weeks? I have asked the question with fifty more years of learning than my parents had, and the honest answer is: I do not know, and they had no chance of knowing. A healer might have helped. A healer would have asked questions. Every road towards help ran through the same gate — telling someone — and on the other side of that gate stood the Ministry, with its registers and its wards and its procedures for children who could not control themselves. My parents did what frightened people with no good roads do. They stayed still, and loved her, and hoped.

The first explosion came about five weeks after the hedge.

It was an ordinary evening. Supper on the stove, lamps lit, my father mending a boot. Ariana sat by the window with her doll, silent, as she was always silent now. And then the air in the kitchen tightened — I can find no better word; every hair on my arms stood up — and there was a smell of burnt sugar, but wrong now, scorched, bitter, the sweetness burnt black. The window beside her burst outward. So did the shelf of plates, the lamp, and the water jug, all at once, in one flat roar, and the room was full of flying glass and my mother was over Ariana faster than any spell I ever saw her cast.

Nobody was badly cut. The kitchen looked as if a giant had shaken the house. And in the middle of it sat my sister, untouched, awake, looking at what had come out of her with an expression I have spent a lifetime failing to forget. It was not fear of the broken glass. It was fear of herself. She was six years old, and she had just learned that the thing inside her did not need her permission, and she looked up at us — at me, I was nearest after Mother — to see whether we were afraid of her too.

I would like to tell you what my face showed her. I do not know. I was nine, and there was glass in my hair. I pray it showed her nothing, and prayer, for me, is a rare event.

My mother held her and said her name, over and over, in the level voice that was Kendra's version of weeping. My brother, seven years old, did the single most intelligent thing done by anyone that night: he went out, in the dark, and brought in the smallest goat, the one Ariana had raised from a bottle. He put it in her arms. And my sister buried her face in its dusty neck, and breathed, and came slowly back to us — and that, in one image, is the whole difference between my brother and me, and I have never pretended otherwise. That night I was already at the bookshelf, looking for the right volume. Aberforth went and got the goat.

My father stood in the doorway of the wrecked kitchen through all of it, and said nothing at all.

He was not a silent man. I have told you about his laugh. But he stood there in his shirt-sleeves with the boot still in one hand, and he looked at the broken window, and the burnt-sugar bitterness still hanging in the air, and his daughter shaking around a goat, and I watched — because I watched everything; it is the one gift that has never once let me rest — I watched him understand.

Understand what she had been carrying for five weeks. Understand what those minutes at the hedge had done, and that it was not over, and would never be over; that this was now the weather of her life. And under all of that, arriving slowly, like cold water finding its level, I watched him understand something else — something he must have been holding down since the day it happened, with a dam of his own.

I did not have the word for what came into my father's face that night. I was nine. I have the word now.

The word is Azkaban.

## 4. What My Father Did

Three days after that first explosion, my father put on his good coat in the middle of the week. He kissed my mother, who did not ask where he was going, which told me — even at nine, even then — that she knew. He stopped in the doorway and looked at us children for a moment longer than usual. Then he walked out of the unlocked door of our house, and by nightfall he had found the three boys, and he had done to them what he had decided to do.

I do not know exactly what it was. That sounds impossible, after a lifetime of finding out nearly everything, but it is true, and my not-knowing is deliberate — the first of several deliberate not-knowings in this book; you should start a list. The trial record calls it a vicious and prolonged attack upon three Muggle children. He used his wand. They lived. Whatever it was, it was enough that hard men in the courtroom went pale, and it was enough for Azkaban, and my father, given every chance to explain, to soften, to beg — my father, who could talk a stone into laughing — stood in front of the Wizengamot and explained nothing at all.

He said the attack was his doing. He said he would not say why. He said nothing else at any point in the proceedings, except one sentence, and the sentence was not addressed to the court.

I was there. My mother had been summoned, and had nowhere to leave me, and I have wondered since whether some part of her also wanted one of us to see, so that one of us could remember it accurately, and perhaps she chose the one whose memory never loses anything. I sat on a hard bench and watched my father in chains be sentenced to life in Azkaban. As they took him past us, he stopped — the guards allowed it, one small mercy in that merciless room — and he bent to me, and what he said was:

"Look after them."

Not goodbye. Not I am innocent, because he was not. Not even I love you, though that was in it, the way iron is in blood. Three words of instruction to a nine-year-old, and then they walked him into the dark, and I never saw my father again. He died in Azkaban years later, still without explaining, in a cell I have never visited and have thought about, at a guess, ten thousand nights.

Now. Why did he not explain?

Any explanation would have been about Ariana. Why did Percival Dumbledore attack three children? Because of what those children did to his daughter. And what, exactly, was his daughter, that such a thing could be done to her, with such results? What is she now — this little girl who cannot control what is in her, who bursts kitchens without a wand? The moment that question was asked officially, in that room, by those people, its answer had an address, and the address was St Mungo's Hospital, the closed ward, the locked ward, the ward from which children with broken magic did not come home. She would have been taken. Kindly, legally, permanently taken, for her own protection and everyone else's, and my mother would have been powerless, and my father would have finished what the boys at the hedge began.

So he chose. Between his freedom and her freedom, in a matter of days, without — I am certain — one hour of proper sleep, my father chose, and he went to Azkaban with his mouth shut, and he left the world one lie to remember him by.

For the world, offered no explanation, wrote its own, and the world's explanations run downhill to the easiest place. A wizard attacks three Muggle children, viciously, unprovoked, and will not say why? Then he needs no why. He is a brute who hates Muggles. The newspapers printed it in those very words — Muggle-hating madman — and the village believed it, and wizarding Britain believed it, and the name Dumbledore, which now hangs on plaques and chocolate frog cards, meant, for the whole of my childhood, exactly one thing: the family of the man who did that.

I would carry that name to school two years later, into corridors where whispers travel faster than owls. Boys I had never met knew one fact about me before I sat down. I will come to what that did, and what I built to answer it, because I built a great deal; ambition is nearly always a wall around some early cold. But this chapter is my father's, and I want to stay with him a little longer, because I owe him something I can only pay here.

I spent years — decades — judging my father, in the private courtroom where I am always in session. The judgments changed as I aged, which is how you know they were about me and not him.

At nine, I simply missed him, and was ashamed of missing a criminal, and was ashamed of the shame, and so on down a spiral staircase that children should never be sent down alone.

At seventeen — you will see the summer I mean — I secretly found him weak. Yes. I can write it now. Seventeen-year-old Albus, that unbearable boy, considered that his father had thrown himself away on a gesture. Revenge repairs nothing, said the boy who had repaired nothing; it was mere feeling, and feeling is what floods when thinking fails. The boy had plans, that summer, for a world where such choices would never be necessary, and a father rotting in Azkaban for sentiment did not fit the architecture. I ask his pardon. He has been dead for most of a century, and I ask his pardon here, in ink, where I cannot mumble it.

At forty, teaching other people's children, I decided my father was wrong but understandable — the verdict of a man who has read much and risked little. It is a comfortable verdict. It fit me for years, the way comfortable things do.

And now? Now I am a hundred and fifteen, and I have buried or failed nearly everyone in this story, and here is what I believe now, at night, with the honest ache in my black hand.

It was revenge. It was wrong. Those were children — cruel, frightened, but children — and what he did to them was not justice, because justice does not arrive at night with a wand and leave without explaining. And also: it saved her. Both of these are true at once, and they do not blend, and a man must hold them one in each hand for the rest of his life. And when I ask myself the only question that matters — the question I have asked every year since, in his voice, in mine: would you have done it? — I answered no for almost a century. I would have found the wiser way, the cleaner way, some way to protect her without ruin.

I no longer fully believe my own answer. I have since watched what I myself will do when everything I love is on the floor. The wiser way has a habit of arriving late. My father, whatever else he was, was on time.

Enough. He would already be embarrassed. He was a man who liked short speeches and long suppers, and I have given him the opposite all my life.

One more thing about him, and then the road. In Azkaban, prisoners were permitted letters, at the discretion of men whose discretion was not large. In all his years there my father sent us a total of four. They were short, and cheerful, and entirely false — the weather, his health, a joke about the food — four small performances of being all right, sent from the worst place in the world to three children and a wife who read them by the fire in silence. I learned by heart every word of all four. It was my first lesson in a truth I would spend my life relearning from the other side: that the people who love us lie to us, steadily, at great cost, to keep the cold off us a little longer. Remember the four letters when, much later in this book, an old man tells a boy that he sees a pair of woollen socks in a magic mirror. It is the same lie. It runs in the family.

What remained of us could not stay in Mould-on-the-Wold. The village knew our name from the papers; the three boys had families, and the families had friends, and stones do not always stay in the road where they belong. Beyond that — and colder than that — was a sum my mother did without a word: Ariana's explosions would come again. In a village that already watched us, the second or third burst kitchen would raise the question we could not survive. We needed distance, and new neighbours, and a house that could keep a secret.

My mother chose Godric's Hollow. A wizarding village, in the West Country, far enough that our story might arrive smaller than we did. She sold what could be sold, packed what could be packed, and did it all with her back straight and her mouth level, because grief, in Kendra Dumbledore, ran silent and deep underground, like the river under a mountain. I never once saw my mother cry. I have no idea what that cost her. I priced it, later, at everything.

We left on a grey morning in a hired cart, because Ariana could no longer safely travel by any magical means. Floo powder, Apparition, even a Portkey — anything that squeezed or startled the magic in her was a risk my mother would not take. So we went the slow Muggle way, wheels and hooves, our whole life roped down under sailcloth, past the bookshop, past the church, out along the lane where three boys had once gone hunting birds' nests.

I sat in the back among the furniture, facing the wrong way, watching our village shrink. Aberforth sat with the goats — they came; there had never been any question that the goats came — with one hand resting flat on each, because standing carts made them nervous.

And Ariana sat beside me, wrapped in our mother's grey shawl, and watched one thing only.

She watched the hedge. Not the house. The house slid by and her eyes never touched it. Only the hedge, that long green wall going smaller and smaller in the summer light, and she watched it with an attention I could not read then and can read too easily now, until the road turned down behind the mill, and it was gone.

Then she looked at her hands, and after a while, very quietly, she began to hum.

## 5. Godric's Hollow

Godric's Hollow is a small village in the West Country of England. Wizards have lived there for centuries, quietly, next to Muggles who notice nothing. Great names are buried in its churchyard. My sister is buried there too, but that comes later. In 1891 the village meant only one thing to us: nobody there knew our story.

That was the whole point of the move. In Mould-on-the-Wold everyone knew what had happened by the hedge, or thought they knew. In Godric's Hollow we could be new people. New people with locked doors.

The house stood at the end of a lane, behind a low stone wall. It had a small garden, an apple tree, and a shed that Aberforth filled with goats before we had even unpacked the plates. My mother chose it because the neighbors were few and the walls were thick.

Kendra Dumbledore built our new life the way other people build a fortress. Every stone had a purpose. I did not understand this then. I understood it much later, when I had built a fortress or two of my own.

She kept the system running with the discipline of a general. Shopping was done at fixed hours, when the lanes were quiet. Questions from shopkeepers were answered before they were asked, in the same words every time, so that the words wore a smooth path. If a version of us had to exist in the village, my mother wrote it herself, and kept it short.

The rules were simple and total. Ariana never went outside, except into the back garden, and only when no one could see. The curtains in her room stayed drawn. Visitors were met at the doorstep and turned away with perfect politeness. My mother could close a door in your face so gracefully that you thanked her for it.

To the neighbors, my sister had "delicate health." That was the official story, and my mother repeated it in a voice that did not invite questions. The village made up its own versions anyway. Villages always do. Some decided Ariana was a Squib, a wizard child with no magic, hidden away out of shame. My mother heard those whispers and let them live. A Squib was a safer story than the truth.

Once, in our first month, a village woman came to the door with a pie and her best curiosity. I watched from the stairs. My mother thanked her, praised the pie, agreed about the weather, sympathized about the vicar, and closed the door, and the woman was halfway down the lane before she understood she had learned nothing at all. My mother's back, as she carried the pie to the kitchen, was perfectly straight. It was always straight. I have seen queens with worse posture, and none with better reasons.

I want you to understand what the truth was, because "mad" is the wrong word, and I will not use it.

Ariana had good days. On good days she woke at dawn, before the village, when the lane was empty, and went out to the goats. She fed them from her hand. She hummed while she did it — no real song, just a small rising and falling sound, like a kettle far away. Aberforth said the goats gave more milk when she hummed. He said it seriously, the way he said everything.

On good days she sat in the kitchen and cut paper into stars, or watched my mother cook, or listened to me read. She liked being read to. It hardly mattered what — she listened to the sound more than the sense. I read her travel books, mostly, borrowed from Bathilda's shelves: deserts, temples, cities with towers of glass. She would stop me at certain sentences and make me read them again, twice, three times, as if saving them somewhere. She did not like being asked questions. Loud voices made her go still, like a rabbit in an open field.

But the magic was still inside her. That is what nobody outside our four walls ever understood. She had refused it, after the hedge, the way you might refuse to speak. And magic that is refused does not leave. It stays, and it presses, like water behind a locked door.

We learned to read the pressure. Her humming stopped first. Then she stopped eating. Then she began to watch the corners of rooms, as if something stood there. My mother would send Aberforth and me out of the house on those days. Sometimes we went. Sometimes we sat on the stairs and waited, because leaving felt worse.

Then it broke. I do not have better words for it, and I have had a hundred years to look for them. The air went tight, and there was a smell of burnt sugar, and then things — broke. A chair would fly into a wall and become firewood. Windows cracked in their frames, all of them at once, in every room. Once the whole kitchen wall split from floor to ceiling, and my mother stood in the dust, straight-backed, and said only, "Fetch the broom, Albus."

And in the middle of it, always, Ariana. Not angry. That was the terrible part. She was frightened — of the noise, of the breaking, of the thing coming out of her — as frightened as if the storm belonged to someone else. Afterwards she would cry, quietly, with her hands over her ears, and say sorry, sorry, sorry, to the chairs, to the wall, to my mother.

Only one of us could reach her then. It was never me.

Aberforth was eight, nine, ten years old in those years. A stubborn, plain, unremarkable boy — his teachers would say so later, and they were fools. He did not try clever things. He warmed milk in a pan, sat down next to her, not too close, and sang. His songs were awful. They were about goats, mostly. A goat who ate the Minister's hat. A goat who could not find her shoes. He made them up, verse after verse, in a flat voice, until Ariana's breathing slowed, and her hands came down from her ears, and she drank the milk. Some nights it took ten minutes. Some nights it took four hours. He never once ran out of goats.

And I? What did I do, the clever one, the eldest, the pride of the family?

I helped by being elsewhere. That was my phrase for it, and I hand it to you exactly as bitter as it tastes. When the pressure built, I found reasons to be in the garden, or on an errand, or deep in a book with my ears closed from the inside. I told myself I was staying out of the way. I was very good at telling myself things. It was, perhaps, my first real talent.

There is a kind of coward who runs from a fight. I have never been that kind. My kind runs from the kitchen, and calls it giving people room.

Where was I, while my brother sang? Upstairs, mostly, with the door shut and a book held like a shield. I had discovered that print could close over my head like water. Two floors down, a wall might crack; on my page, the goblin rebellions proceeded in good order. I read enormously that year. It is the one part of my defense I cannot regret, and the part I trust least.

One neighbor got past my mother's doorstep. Only one, in all those years.

Bathilda Bagshot lived across the lane, in a crooked house full of papers. She was already famous then — the greatest magical historian of the age, the author of books I would later teach from. In person she was a small, bright-eyed woman who talked the way rain falls, steadily, warmly, about everything at once. She had known everyone. She had letters from people whose statues stood in the Ministry.

My mother turned her away twice, politely. Bathilda came back a third time with a fruit cake and the observation that she had known misery in many famous houses and had never yet spread it further. Something in that sentence reached my mother. Kendra did not trust her — my mother trusted nobody, by then — but she half-trusted her, and half was more than anyone else received.

So Bathilda came sometimes, in the evenings, and drank tea, and talked enough for the whole silent table. She never asked about Ariana. She spoke to her instead — lightly, about ordinary things, cats and weather and the terrible sermons of the village priest — not caring whether she got answers. Sometimes Ariana hummed while Bathilda talked. In our house, that was music.

I record here my debt to that talkative, kind, careless old woman. She was the only person who treated us as a family rather than a secret. What her kindness would one day bring across her garden wall to me — that belongs to a later chapter, and I am in no hurry to reach it.

A year passed in that house before my letter came. I have compressed them here into a few pages, and I notice, writing this, that the compression is itself a confession. Those years were my family's whole life. To me they were a waiting room.

The letter arrived in July 1892, by a Ministry owl, on thick yellow parchment. Mr A. Dumbledore, we are pleased to inform you that you have a place at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. Every wizard child in Britain gets one. It surprised nobody. My mother read it, nodded once, and began making lists.

I would like to tell you I thought first of them — of my mother alone with the pressure and the broken windows, of Aberforth and his goats, of Ariana watching me pack. I have promised, in this book, not to lie.

What I felt, when I saw my name on that envelope, was relief. Relief like air after deep water. Somewhere there was a castle full of books and teachers and prizes, full of people who did not know about hedges or burnt sugar, and I was going to it, and I was going to shine, and for ten months of every year the locked house would be something I remembered instead of something I lived in.

I was eleven years old. Be gentle with that boy if you can; I have given up trying. He stood in the garden with his letter and felt the future open like a door — and did not once look back at the window where the curtains never moved.

I have been ashamed of many things in a long life. Few of them have lasted like the taste of that first, enormous, shameful relief.

## 6. The Letter from Hogwarts

Every September, for more than a century now, I have watched the first-years come up the drive and see the castle for the first time. Their faces open. I never tire of it, because I am watching my own face, in 1892, from the far side of a long life.

Hogwarts, to a boy from a locked house, was not a school. It was permission. A thousand rooms, and none of them silent with fear. Staircases that moved because they felt like moving. A library the size of our whole village. Portraits that argued with each other about goblin wars. Nobody drew the curtains. Nobody listened for a change in someone's breathing. At night the castle was loud with ghosts and wind and other children, and I slept better in that noise than I had ever slept at home.

They took us across the lake in boats, as they still do. I sat in the bow with three strangers and watched the windows come nearer — hundreds of windows, all of them lit, not one of them curtained. I made no promise to myself that night. Not in words. But something in me settled, the way a compass needle settles, and it pointed at that castle for the rest of my life.

I was sorted into Gryffindor, like my father before me. The hat took its time with me, I remember. I have often wondered what else it considered. I have never asked it. We maintain, the hat and I, a diplomatic silence on the subject.

Within a month, my teachers had noticed me. Within a year, they were passing my essays to one another.

I know this because Professor Herbert, who taught Charms, told me so — kindly, meaning to encourage a boy in cheap robes. "I showed your essay to Professor Black," he said. "We agreed we had not seen its like in thirty years." I walked back to my dormitory holding those words the way you hold something hot and precious. Not seen its like in thirty years. I was twelve.

By my third year, older students were coming to me. That is the detail I would ask you to sit with, because I sat with it, gladly, for seven years. Fifteen-year-olds bringing their Transfiguration problems to a boy of thirteen. Seventh-years, before their examinations, asking — casually, as if it did not cost them — whether I might glance at their theory of vanishment. I always might. I was generous with my mind. Generosity of that kind is the cheapest there is, and pays the best interest, and I recommend it to any clever child who wants to be loved without the trouble of loving.

You will notice the tone. The old man writing this is not proud of the boy. But I must be exact: the boy was not a monster. He was starving, and he had found food.

At home, praise was rationed. Not from coldness — my mother had no time, no strength, and no room in a day that was all defense. What our house made, mostly, was silence and fear, and I had eaten enough of both. Then I came to a castle where, if I worked — and working was the one thing I could always do — grown men and women looked at me with plain delight. Applause, I discovered, feels like the exact opposite of a locked door.

So I chased it. I did not understand, then, that I was treating a hunger, and that hungers grow with feeding. I only knew that when a teacher read my work aloud, the sound in my chest was the sound of being safe.

But the castle had heard of us. Of course it had.

It began in my first term, in a corridor. An older boy — a big, soft-faced boy whose name I could still write here and will not — looked at me and said, to his friends, quite clearly: "That's him. The Muggle-hater's son."

My father was in Azkaban. The newspapers had told their version: a wizard who attacked three Muggle children, unprovoked, out of hatred. Only our family knew what the truth was, and the truth was locked in a house in Godric's Hollow with the curtains drawn. I could not say: my father did it because of what they did to my sister. The sentence could not exist. If it existed, the Ministry would come for her.

So I stood in the corridor and said nothing, and the whisper followed me for seven years. It waited behind compliments. It sat in the pause after my name. Dumbledore — wasn't there something about the father?

Even the kind ones did it. A teacher praising my work might stop, an inch before the end of the sentence, while the family history crossed his face like a cloud crossing a field. I learned to watch for that pause. I learned to fill it, quickly, with something bright. I became expert at being one second more interesting than my own surname.

Another boy would have used his fists, and I will introduce you shortly to my brother, who did. I chose a different weapon, because it was the only one I had and because — I must be honest — it was the one I liked. I decided, with the cold clarity that eleven-year-olds are not supposed to have, that I would become so bright that the name Dumbledore would stop meaning my father. I would take the family name and win it back, prize by prize, mark by mark, until it meant me.

It worked. That is the frightening part of this chapter, and I would underline it for every clever child who reads this: it worked. By my fifth year, "Dumbledore" meant the boy who had rewritten the theory of Transfiguration before he could shave. The whisper never quite died, but it grew quiet, and embarrassed, and moved to the back of the room.

The old man's diagnosis, a century on: ambition born as self-defense is still ambition. The shield does not stay a shield. One morning you pick it up and notice the edge on it, and notice how much you like the weight, and by then it has been a sword for years. I built my brilliance to protect a secret and to bury a shame. But no one builds a tower and lives in the cellar. I climbed it. I enjoyed the view. Remember the view; it matters later.

Now let me write about Elphias, because this chapter is too full of me, which is accurate, but unfair to the year itself.

Elphias Doge arrived at Hogwarts the same September I did, green-faced from dragon pox, which he had caught just before term. The pox left his skin marked, and children are merciful as weather. They mocked him from his first week. They called him Dogbreath. They imitated his cough. They found his slowness in class hilarious, and his kindness — he was kind the way some people are tall, visibly and all the time — funnier still.

We were put next to each other in our first Potions lesson, the pox-marked boy and the Muggle-hater's son. The two least promising social positions in our year, seated at one bench. I have wondered since whether Professor Jigger did it out of cruelty or genius, and have decided he simply was not thinking about us at all, which is how most of fate operates.

Elphias liked me. I want to be precise about this, because it was the rarest event of my school years and I nearly missed it. The teachers were delighted by me. The students admired me, needed me, envied me, collected me. Elphias liked me — the way you like a person, not a firework. He liked me on my dull days. He liked me when I lost — I lost twice at chess in seven years, both times to him, and he was so distressed at winning that he apologized. He asked me questions and then listened to the answer instead of to my cleverness.

He was also the only person at school I ever told about my sister — a little of it, the surface of it, one night in fourth year when the dormitory was empty. He said, "That's hard, Albus." Nothing else. He never mentioned it again, and never treated me as if he knew, and I understood many years later what a feat of delicacy that was, from a boy the whole school had agreed to call stupid.

I repaid him poorly, with a friendship that was real but lazy, and he never once presented the bill. There is a kind of goodness that clever people learn from slowly, because it does not argue its case. Elphias Doge has been my loyal friend for over a hundred years, and I believe I understood what he was worth only in the last thirty of them. He would say — he has said — "You always were a bit slow, Albus." It is one of perhaps four jokes he makes. I treasure all four.

The year turned, as school years do, in a rhythm of feasts and examinations, and then it was June, and the train took me back.

My mother met me at the crossroads with the cart. She looked older by more than a year. Aberforth stood in the garden and lifted one hand, and did not come to carry my trunk, which was his way of making a point, and the point was fair. Inside, the house smelled of the same soup and the same waiting. Ariana came halfway down the stairs, saw me, smiled — she still smiled then, my sister, I want that written down — and said my name as a question, as if checking her memory against the door.

And the door closed behind me. I heard the latch go home, that small domestic click, curtains already drawn against the summer evening — and I stood in the dim hallway with my trunk, eleven months old with pride, and understood with absolute clearness that I was not home. I had just left home. Home had turrets and a library and people who clapped.

I hid the thought at once, the way you hide something stolen. But a thought is not deed; you cannot give it back. It stayed in me all that summer, quiet as a coin in a pocket: the knowledge of where I would rather be, and the guilt of it, which never left, and which I notice — my hand aches tonight, and I am watching an old man's writing wander — I have carried further than almost anything else. Guilt of that particular kind does not age. It is the freshest thing I own.

## 7. Brilliant

There is a cupboard in my office where the prizes live. I have not opened it in years, but I can list the contents from memory, which tells you something about me that the locked cupboard was supposed to hide.

Let me compress six school years into their honest shape.

They were happy years. That must be said first, and plainly, because what follows will sound like an accusation, and an accusation of happiness is a strange document. I woke every morning of those years glad of the day. I simply see now what the gladness was made of.

I won the Barnabus Finkley Prize for Exceptional Spell-Casting. I was made British Youth Representative to the Wizengamot, and sat once a term in London with old men who asked my opinion and then quoted it. I remember my first session: fourteen witches and wizards of great age and even greater pride, debating cauldron regulation, and a boy of sixteen in his best robes, waiting to be found remarkable. I was found remarkable. I had prepared for three weeks to be found remarkable in an unprepared way. The Chief Warlock called me "a mind of the century" before the century had properly started, and I told no one, and kept the phrase where I keep such things. I took the gold medal at the International Alchemical Conference in Cairo — I did not travel there; my paper did, and came back with the medal, which struck me even then as an efficient arrangement. Prefect at fifteen. Head Boy at seventeen. Top marks in everything, always, with a consistency that stopped being news.

Being a prefect taught me something I did not expect: I liked helping the younger ones. Not only for the audience — though the audience never hurt — but for the thing itself: a small confused first-year, a knot in his understanding, and my hands untying it. I note this fairly, because this book is a court, and even the accused may submit evidence. The boy had good in him. It simply was not what he was building with.

And the letters. I must tell you about the letters, because they were the purest pleasure of those years, and pleasure is evidence.

Famous wizards began to write to me. It started with my Cairo paper: a note from an alchemist in Prague, three lines, correcting my fourth equation and praising my fifth. I replied the same night, correcting his correction. By return owl came four pages, delighted, furious, brilliant, and we were off. Within a year I was in correspondence with half the serious minds of Europe. I was seventeen and exchanging letters with Nicolas Flamel, and I made sure everyone knew it. I would mention it sideways, with a small apology, the way you set down something heavy: "Flamel makes a similar point — forgive me, we correspond." The pause after that sentence was one of the great rewards of my young life. I collected such pauses. Some men collect dragon eggs.

Understand: the work itself was real. I loved the work — the click of a problem opening, the hour after midnight when a theory suddenly stands up and walks. That love was the best thing in me, and it never left. But wrapped around the clean love of the work was the other thing, the hunger with my name on it, and by seventeen I could no longer tell you where one ended and the other began. I am not sure I wanted to.

Meanwhile, at the far edge of all this light, there was a house with drawn curtains, and it was getting smaller.

I do not mean the house changed. I mean the place it occupied in my mind shrank, year by year, the way a town shrinks behind a fast carriage. My visits home grew shorter, and I grew skilled at the small sums that shortened them — a summer program here, a Wizengamot session there, an invitation it would be rude to refuse. Refusing my family was never rude. They had been taught not to expect me. I did the teaching.

My letters to my mother tell the story better than memory could. She kept them; I found them after; I have them still. The early ones are a schoolboy's, full of everything. Then, term by term, they change. They grow polite. They ask questions and leave no room for true answers — "I hope Ariana is well" — hope, that tidy little word, doing the work of a visit. They enclose news of prizes. They are letters from a distinguished stranger who wishes a widow well. Whoever reads them can watch a son turning into a correspondent, page by page. It is the only collection of mine I would burn.

Her replies grew shorter to match, and I chose to read the shortness as contentment. Reading people generously, when the generosity flatters you — that is a skill too. I had all the skills.

In 1895 Aberforth came to Hogwarts, and now there were two Dumbledores in the corridors, and no one ever took us for brothers.

He was sorted, he attended, he endured. School fit him like someone else's boots. He did the work without interest and without disaster, and lived for the holidays, for the goats, for home — for her. We passed each other between classes like men from countries that share nothing but a border. A nod, sometimes. I told myself he preferred it, which had the advantage of being true and the deeper falseness of all true excuses: I never once tested it.

What I knew of his school life was mostly what the staff let fall. He fought. Not often, but memorably — a sudden, short, farmyard style of fighting that ended arguments rather than won them. He was never willing to say what the fights were about, and took his punishments with a shrug. But I knew. The whisper that followed me had followed him too — the mad father, and by then some new muttering about the sister no one ever saw. I answered it with medals. He answered it with his fists, on behalf of people he would not name, in a school he did not love, and served his detentions, and wrote to Ariana every week — short letters, I learned much later, mostly about goats.

I saw one of the fights. Only one. A courtyard, a ring of fourth-years, and my brother in the middle of it, hitting a boy much larger than himself with the steady calm of a man splitting logs. A word had been said; I never learned which of our family it concerned. I was Head Boy by then. I broke it up, took points from everyone with beautiful fairness, and gave my own brother a lecture on self-control in front of his year. He looked at me while I delivered it the way you look at weather. We did not speak of it after. We did not speak at all.

Measure us, reader, and take your time. I came off best in every measurement the world knew how to make.

One scene from those years I have kept deliberately, the way you keep a candle in a drawer against the dark. I offer it here because this chapter is otherwise all cupboards and prizes, and because you must see her alive. That is this book's real work; the confession is only the frame.

The summer I was fifteen, on an evening when the house was calm, I sat with Ariana in the back garden. Dusk, the goats settling, my mother in the kitchen with the door open — she kept doors open when she could, my mother; it was her one luxury. Ariana sat on the step with her knees drawn up, humming her no-song.

I had learned a small charm that term — nothing, a toy: conjured lights, little floating spheres the size of plums, colored like the inside of shells. Not part of any lesson. I had built it myself out of a footnote. I was going to publish it, naturally; I published everything.

I made them for her. A dozen small lights, drifting over the garden in the blue dusk, pink and green and a gold I had worked two weeks to get right.

Ariana stopped humming. She looked up, and her whole face opened — and she laughed and clapped her hands, twice, the way she had clapped at six, at my card tricks and my dancing spoons in another kitchen in another life. For a minute — I have measured that minute against most things I have done since — she was simply a girl in a garden, and I was simply her brother, and the lights were simply pretty, and every locked thing was unlocked.

Then a door slammed. Somewhere down the lane — a neighbor, a gust of wind, nothing. The crack of it went across the evening, and Ariana went out. I have never found a better phrase than the one I made that night, watching: she went out like a candle. Her face closed, her arms came around her knees, the humming stopped and did not return. My lights drifted on above the grass, pink and green and hard-won gold, suddenly stupid. My mother came to the door. Aberforth came from the shed. I put the lights out.

That is the last time I clearly remember making my sister laugh. I was fifteen. She was eleven. There were three years left, and I spent them, as this chapter records, brilliantly.

School ended as it had run. At the Leaving Feast the Great Hall applauded, and teachers I admired most shook my hand, and the future stood open like a door with light behind it — London, Europe, the work, the name Dumbledore polished at last and meaning me.

Professor Herbert, who had started it all with one kind sentence over an essay, shook my hand last of anyone, and held it a moment. "You will be careful, Dumbledore," he said, "what you are brilliant at." I took it for a compliment of an original shape. Old men are forever handing us the key to the whole story, and we pocket it, and admire the handle.

I have set down the prizes. I have set down the letters, the medals, the pauses I collected. Now set down this, and let it stand against everything above it, and everything to come:

I loved my sister. You must believe that, because everything I am about to tell you argues against it.

## 8. Plans

The map hung over my bed all that final year, on the inside of the curtain of my four-poster, where only I could see it. Europe and the Near East, in brown ink, with our route marked in green. Elphias had wanted red. I had explained, at some length, why green. He had let me win, as usual, and drawn in the line while I talked.

The grand tour: that was the tradition. A young wizard of promise finished school and went to see the wizarding world — a year of it, maybe two. Elphias and I had been building ours since fifth year, and by that last winter it had stopped being a plan and become a country we already half-lived in.

We would start in Greece, for the oracles and the ruins of the old open-air theatres where, Bathilda's books said, magic and theatre had once been the same art. Then by sea to Egypt: Cairo, where my medal had traveled without me and where the alchemists of the conference had promised — in writing; I kept the letters in the trunk with the maps — to open their workshops to me. Up the Nile to the tombs, where the curse-breakers worked, to see wards older than Hogwarts. Then north through Persia for the flying-carpet libraries, and west again, and finally Romania, for the dragons. Elphias wanted the dragons above everything. He had wanted dragons since he was eleven; the pox, he said, had been the closest he ever came, and he intended to improve on it.

We planned in the evenings, after my Head Boy rounds, in the corner of the common room that seventh-years hold by ancient right. Elphias would spread the maps; I would produce the letters; we would argue about the order of countries with the seriousness of generals, though our army was two boys and a collapsible cauldron. Around midnight the arguing would soften into simply naming things — the names alone were half the journey. Alexandria. Carpathia. We said them the way other boys said the names of girls.

We had money saved — mine from prizes, his from three summers of dull work in his uncle's cauldron shop. We had it counted, divided into stages, and recorded in a ledger in Elphias's careful round hand. We had letters of introduction. We had, above all, Elphias's packing lists, which were an art of their own. He drafted them all winter and read them to me for review. Seventeen kinds of preserved food. A collapsible cauldron and a spare, "in case of the first." Dragon-proof gloves, ordered a year early, in two sizes, "in case my hands grow." I mocked those lists gently and often. I would give a great deal now for one evening in that dormitory, with the fire down to embers, being read a packing list by Elphias Doge.

For him, the tour was the adventure of his life. For me it was that, and one thing more, which I did not say aloud and did not need to; I have never needed anyone's help to deceive myself efficiently.

The tour was my door out.

I had done the sums without ever writing them down. A year abroad, perhaps two. Then the career — London, the Ministry courting me, or a chair somewhere, or Flamel's standing invitation to Paris. Somewhere in all that brightness there would be money, and money would mean help engaged for my mother, a matron, a healer sworn to silence — the details stayed conveniently soft. What was hard and clear was the shape of my future, and the shape was: elsewhere. The house with the drawn curtains had a place in that future. The place was the size of a monthly letter.

I even had a sentence prepared for anyone tactless enough to ask. "My brother finishes at Hogwarts in two years; the household will be settled by then; my mother has help." It was smooth from use, though no one ever asked. The person I said it to most often lived behind my own eyes and was easy to convince. He wanted to be convinced. It is the only audience I have ever bribed.

I did not hate my family. Please understand this, whoever you are. I loved them, in the way that a man in a river loves the shore — truly, and from the current, while it carries him somewhere else. I had been swimming toward the open sea since I was eleven.

In the spring of 1899, my mother's letter came.

She wrote rarely, and always briefly; ink cost money and complaint cost more, and she permitted herself neither. This letter was brief too. But I had, whatever else was wrong with me, a good ear, and I heard it straight away: the sentences had changed.

> Things are somewhat harder this winter. Ariana has had a difficult season, and the house has suffered for it. Aberforth's absence tells on her more than it did — she asks for him, and for you. Do not concern yourself; we manage as we always have. Your news of the Wizengamot gave great pleasure here.
> Your loving mother.

Difficult season. The house has suffered. My mother's language for a winter I can now imagine and refuse to describe: the pressure building with no Aberforth to sing it down, the burnt-sugar air, and a woman past fifty, alone, mending walls.

She asks for you. Four words, in a letter of sixty. My mother had never asked me for anything in her life. This was as close as her pride could bend, and I knew it when I read it. That is the point of this chapter. I knew.

I sat with her letter on the edge of the lake, in the spring sunshine, and there was one clean moment — I remember it precisely, because it was the moment of choice, and I have visited it many times since — when I saw what the letter asked, and saw myself going home, and saw the tour and the career and the whole lit future waiting behind glass. The moment did not even hurt. That is what shames me most about it. Choosing against them had become so practiced that it no longer felt like choosing.

I answered by return owl. I answered — generously. I sent nearly all of my prize money from that spring, a sum that startled even me, with a warm, well-made letter: the money was for repairs and for help in the house, and they were to spare nothing, and I would be home after the examinations, and then there would be time to arrange things properly. I did not go home for Easter. There was a Wizengamot session I could not miss. I could have missed it.

The session, since you ask, concerned the regulation of experimental charms. I spoke twice. I was praised. I do not remember a word of what I said, and I remember every word of the letter I did not answer with my presence. Memory, whatever else it is, is a fair judge of what mattered.

I sent gold instead of myself. I was generous, and generosity was cheaper. I have turned that sentence over for a hundred years and found no way to improve it, and I have stopped looking, because its ugliness is the truest thing in it. Gold was exactly what my gift was worth. My mother did not need gold. She needed a son at the table and another wand in the house, and she had asked — she, who never asked — and I posted her my winnings, and slept well, and went back to my Transfiguration notes with the special lightness of a man who has paid a debt in the wrong coin and got a receipt.

The examinations came in June and I dispatched them like letters. Between papers, Elphias and I walked by the lake and rehearsed the tour to each other one more time — Greece by August, Egypt by October, dragons within the year. The boat tickets were bought. They lived in Elphias's breast pocket, because between the two of us he was, we agreed, the one who never lost things.

Seven examiners in seven subjects made small delighted noises over my papers. The Transfiguration examiner asked whether he might keep my written answer, to show his colleagues; I said yes with a modesty I had rehearsed. Nothing in the world was heavy that June. Even the weather held, day after golden day, as if it had been engaged for the occasion.

On the last morning, my trunk stood packed at the foot of the bed: books at the bottom, the brown-ink map on top, folded green-line out. Head Boy's badge wrapped in a sock and buried, retired. The map I had taken down last, the night before, folding the green line inward, like something alive going into its box. Seven years were folded into that trunk, and every one of them had gone the way I intended, and I came down to the Leaving Breakfast that morning as close to happiness, I think, as I had ever been. The whole Hall smelled of toast and endings. Elphias sat across from me and read me, for the final time as schoolboys, the packing list. I remember I was laughing at the spare cauldron when the owls came in.

Morning owls were nothing; the Hall got a hundred a day. But long practice had taught me the difference in sound between a hundred owls and a hundred owls and one, and I looked up, and one grey owl came down the length of the Hall, past Gryffindor table, wings set, and there was no morning in me anymore. It planted its feet by my plate, among the toast crumbs, and held out its leg, and waited, the way owls wait, caring nothing for what it carried.

The envelope was of heavy paper, and its edges were black.

Black borders meant then what they mean now. The Hall went on around me — benches, laughter, the sixth-years' plans, Elphias's voice asking something from a long way off — and I sat with the envelope in my hands and did not open it, for one heartbeat, two, three, as if the news inside were not yet true, as if the last morning of my old life could be held open a moment longer by a boy refusing to break a seal.

I broke the seal.

## Part Two: The Summer of Fire

*Every fire I have ever known began the same way: something dry, something golden, and a long summer without rain. I was the dry thing. He was the gold. This part of my story lasts only two months, and I have needed most of a century to be able to tell it.*

## 9. The Black Dress

My mother was dead.

The letter said it in the careful words the Ministry keeps for such things. A magical accident. Our deepest sympathy. Please come home at once. I read it twice at the breakfast table, and the noise of the Great Hall moved far away, like sound under water.

Doge sat across from me. The boat tickets were in his pocket. Our trunks were packed for Greece. He saw my face and stopped talking, which, for Elphias, was an act of great love.

"Albus," he said. "What is it?"

I could not say the words. I turned the letter so that he could see the black edge.

What had happened I learned later, mostly from Bathilda Bagshot. Ariana could not tell it, and she never did. It was an ordinary June morning. My mother was in the kitchen. My sister was with her, and something — a sound, a shadow, nothing at all — frightened her.

It was one of Ariana's explosions. She had been calm for weeks, Bathilda said. Then the magic broke out of her, the way it always did, without warning and without aim. My mother was standing in the wrong place. That is all. That is the whole story of my mother's death, and I have never found a way to make it larger or smaller than that.

Nobody was at fault. I would like to write that this made it easier. It made it worse. When there is a guilty person, grief has somewhere to go. Ours had nowhere. It moved into the house with us and took the best room.

Bathilda found them. Ariana was sitting on the kitchen floor, not hurt, holding my mother's hand. She was humming, very quietly, the way she hummed when she was afraid. I am glad, even now, that it was Bathilda who opened that door. Not Aberforth. Not me.

The school sent us home the same day. I remember almost nothing of the journey. I remember Aberforth's face, which was white, and his hands, which stayed closed into fists all the way to Godric's Hollow.

Bathilda met us at the door. She had drawn the curtains, lit the lamps, and cooked a supper that nobody ate. She pressed my hand and called me "poor boy." I remember thinking, with the cold clear part of my mind that never stops working, that I was not a boy any longer, as of that morning. Nobody had asked me.

Aberforth went straight up the stairs to Ariana. Through the floor I heard his voice, low and easy — the voice he kept for her and for the goats and for nobody else. Then I heard her crying. Bathilda whispered that she had not cried until then. My brother was fifteen. He was better at the most important work in our house than I have ever been at anything.

I sat downstairs with the lamps and the cold supper and began to make lists. The funeral. The money. The house. Somebody had to, I told myself, and that was true. It was also true that lists were easier than the stairs.

I should say something about my mother here, because I have buried her in six paragraphs, and she deserves better.

Kendra Dumbledore was proud, dark-haired, and tired in a way I did not recognise as tiredness until I was tired myself. For eight years she ran a locked house on very little money and no help. She kept a broken child safe, fed, and secret. She did it alone, with a straight back, and she never once asked her clever son to come home and share it.

As a boy I thought she was hard. As a young man I thought she was cold. It took me half a lifetime to understand what I had been looking at. It was not coldness. It was a soldier's discipline, held for eight years, in a war nobody was allowed to know about. I understand her now. Understanding always comes to me by owl, years late, when the person is gone.

Ariana could not come to the funeral. We did not even argue about it. A funeral is a crowd, and voices, and strangers' eyes — everything she could not bear. But she had no black dress, because she never went anywhere. So Bathilda took an old dress of my mother's, cut it down, and sewed through the night.

In the morning the dress lay on the kitchen chair, small and neat and terrible.

Ariana put it on. She could not go, and she knew she could not go, and she put it on anyway. She sat by the upstairs window all that day with her hands folded and her back very straight — our mother's back. Nobody told her to do it. Of all the things I saw that summer, that one is the hardest to write.

The funeral was small. Godric's Hollow buries people quietly. A few neighbours came, the ones who had been told for years that my mother was simply a private woman. They stood in the sun and whispered. I caught one whisper — a question about "the daughter" — and I looked at the woman until she stopped. It was the only magic I performed that day.

At the graveside I stood between my brother and my best friend and thought about nothing. I have a very good mind. It can do many things. That day it stood in front of a hole in the June earth and could do nothing at all.

Two days later, Doge came to say goodbye, and could not say it. He stood in our kitchen and turned his hat in his hands. Elphias was never quick. What he had was better than quickness.

"I'll stay," he said. "A year. Greece has been there rather a long time, Albus. I expect it can wait."

For one moment I let myself see it: a friend within reach, one person to play chess with, one voice from the world outside. Then I saw the truer picture. Elphias in our dark kitchen, week after week, feeding his year into this house the way I was about to feed mine. The house would take it. The house took everything.

"No," I said. "You will go. You will write to me from every ugly hotel in Europe, and I will correct your Greek by owl."

He laughed, and then his face fell. "It isn't fair," he said. "You worked harder than any of us."

"Fairness is a game for children," I said, or something equally grand. I was seventeen and still spoke sentences that belonged in books. He embraced me, badly, because we were English, and then he went. I watched him to the corner of the lane. I was more grateful to him than I have ever been to anyone, and I envied him so much my teeth ached. I learned that morning that gratitude and envy can live together very comfortably, like two ugly neighbours.

Then the house was mine.

I do not only mean that I inherited it, although I did. I mean that the whole weight of it came down and rested where I stood. There was some money, less than I had hoped. My mother, I discovered, had been performing a small miracle every month for eight years, and like most real miracles it had gone unnoticed. There were my prize winnings — the gold I had so generously sent instead of myself. Counted together, it was enough, if nobody ever wanted anything.

Head of the family. I wrote those words on a Ministry form, aged seventeen, at the kitchen table. My handwriting, I noticed, stayed beautiful. Handwriting has no shame.

Aberforth found me there among the papers.

"I'm not going back in September," he said. No greeting. He never wasted words on approaches. "I'll stay home with Ariana. You go and be famous. Everyone wins."

He meant it simply. That is the thing about my brother which took me thirty years to learn: he has never in his life said a thing he did not mean.

And he was right. There is no honest version of this book in which he was not right. Ariana trusted him. He knew her good days and her bad days the way a farmer knows weather. With him she laughed out loud. With me she was polite, the way one is polite to a kind teacher. Warm milk, silly songs, patience — he had the whole art of her. He had it without trying, and I could not learn it, and I had learned everything else in the world.

"You will finish school," I said.

We argued. Or rather, he argued, and I pronounced. I had the vocabulary; he had the truth; with us two, sadly, vocabulary always won. I spoke of duty, of what our mother would have wished, of his education, of the proper order of things. I was calm, reasonable, and immovable. In the end he went up the stairs and slammed his door — fifteen years old, and right.

Now the real reason, since this book exists for nothing else.

If Aberforth left school and took the house, then the house was forever. The arrangement would have a name, and the name would be permanent. But if he went back to Hogwarts, then all this was temporary. Then I was still a brilliant young man, briefly delayed, helping at home between triumphs. Sending my brother back to school kept a painted door on my wall, and I could go on believing the door was real.

He offered me freedom. I refused it, in order to protect my idea of freedom. It was the worst bargain I ever made, and I have made several very bad ones.

That night I sat up late at the kitchen table. The house at night had a sound I would come to know well: two people breathing behind two doors, and under that, the small tick of the kitchen clock. My tea had gone cold an hour before. I drank it anyway. It felt, correctly, like punishment.

I listened to my own heartbeat, and I hated it. It sounded exactly like the clock. It sounded like something counting — steady, patient, professional — counting away the tour, the year, the work I would never write. Upstairs my sister turned over in her sleep. She had lost her mother that week, and I sat below her, grieving for my timetable.

I have done worse things than that. I have done very few that shame me more.

## 10. The Cage

Grief, I discovered that summer, is not one feeling. It is a schedule.

It wakes you at six. It stands you in front of a cold stove. It walks you to the shops with a basket and a list, past neighbours who lower their voices kindly as you pass. Then it sits you at a table you cannot leave, in a house you cannot leave, in a life that — I could see no way around this — was now yours.

Every morning I lit the stove and burned the porridge. This is not a figure of speech. I could turn a teapot into a tortoise, and I could not cook oats in water. Aberforth ate my failures without comment, which was his form of mercy, and Ariana ate almost nothing, which was her form of everything.

Then the goats. They were Aberforth's, and he did the true work of them, but there were mornings he stayed with Ariana, and then the goats fell to me. I, Albus Dumbledore, winner of the Barnabus Finkley Prize for Exceptional Spell-Casting, stood in a shed at dawn being pushed about by animals who were entirely unimpressed by me. They were the most honest audience I ever had.

Then the money. I counted it twice a week, and then counted it again, as if it might breed when left alone in the dark. It never did. I invented small economies and was proud of them for whole minutes. I mended my own robes with a charm I had once used, for a prize, on a tapestry in front of three hundred people. Nobody applauded the robes. The robes and I bore it together.

I want to describe one bad day properly, because the words "bad day" are doing too much quiet work in this chapter.

It was a Tuesday in the second week. The silence started at breakfast. By noon the whole house had drawn in its breath. Aberforth moved everything breakable off the dresser, softly, one piece at a time, the way you defuse a spell. I stood in the doorway being useless in three languages.

It came at four o'clock: the kitchen shelf, the good jug, and the window over the sink, all at once, with a sound like the world clapping its hands. Then my sister on the floor with her hands over her ears, crying, saying sorry — to the jug, to us, to our mother, I have never known — and Aberforth already on his knees beside her, talking about goats. Talking nonsense about goats, slow and warm, until her hands came down.

I repaired the window and the jug. Eleven seconds, both of them. It is the only repair I was ever able to make in that house, and I have never in my life felt more completely beside the point.

And through all of it, around all of it, there was Ariana.

She had good days. On a good day she came down early, fed the goats at dawn while the lane was empty, and hummed to herself over her bowl. On a good day she liked to sit near a window with the curtain open one hand's width, watching the light move. She hummed the same three or four little tunes. I never learned where she had found them. I would give a great deal now to know.

And she had the other days. Those began with silence — a particular, pressing silence, like the air before a storm. We learned to read it. Voices went low; doors were closed softly; the kettle was moved off its iron stand, because iron sings when it shakes. Sometimes the storm passed. Sometimes it did not, and then there would be a burst — a shattered jug, a cracked window, once the whole kitchen shelf — and through the house that faint, sweet, wrong smell of burnt sugar.

Afterwards she always cried, because she was fourteen and gentle, and something lived inside her that was neither.

Meanwhile the owls kept coming. The world, which did not know I had left it, wrote to me all summer.

An invitation to speak at a conference in Cairo, where I had won a gold medal the year before. A letter from a learned journal, asking — very flattering — when they might expect my paper on transfiguration across species. A note from Nicolas Flamel himself, wondering when I would come to Paris. I answered that family matters kept me in England for the present. The sentence took me four drafts, because I wanted it to sound light.

I filed the letters in a drawer. On bad nights I took them out and read them, which I recommend to nobody.

The paper for the journal deserves its own sentence. I began it four times that summer, at the kitchen table, after the others were asleep. Four beginnings, each one brilliant, each one dead by the second page — because a paper is a long argument, and a long argument needs unbroken hours, and my hours broke on a schedule that was not mine. I still have the fourth beginning somewhere. It is very good. It is the cleverest gravestone I ever carved.

Doge wrote too, faithfully, from the tour that should have been ours. His postcards arrived sunburnt and happy: Greece, then the boat to Egypt, alchemists, temples, a warning about dragons in Romania that he was clearly delighted by. I read each one twice and kept them in the same drawer, which turned the drawer into a small museum of my unlived life. I answered cheerfully. I have always written a very good cheerful.

Here is what happens to a mind like mine when it is left in a small house with no work. It does not go quiet. It eats itself.

I argued with dead authors in the margins of their books. I invented improvements to spells I had no reason to cast, and theories I could tell no one. I gave lectures to the goats. I lay awake building, stone by stone, the brilliant year I was not having — the conference, the paper, the introductions, the applause — and then took it down again, every night, like a man building and burning the same house.

And under the grief, and under the love, there was another feeling. I was ashamed of it then. I will name it now, because naming things late is my one great talent.

I am wasted here.

That was the feeling, black and simple. Not: my sister is suffering. Not: my brother carries too much. Those thoughts came too, and I mean them no dishonour. But the feeling underneath, the low constant note under the whole summer, was mine and only mine. I am wasted here. Nobody sees me. My one life is being spent on porridge.

I was ashamed of every feeling I had that summer, and I had all of them anyway. That is how feelings work, as I would explain to the boy I was, if he would listen. He would not. He had never once listened to anybody.

One evening I want to keep here, before what comes next.

It was late in June, warm, the window open its one hand's width. Aberforth was out with the goats. Ariana sat on the floor by my chair, braiding a long ribbon our mother had kept — braiding it, unbraiding it, braiding it again, humming one of her little tunes. She liked to hear reading aloud, so I read to her. A travel book, of all things: mountains, rivers, cities with white walls.

She was calm. The house was quiet in the good way. The evening light came in gold and lay along the floor, and for a while — I want this written down — for a while it was almost peace, and I was almost in it.

Then I caught myself glancing at the clock.

Not once. Twice in ten minutes, a small secret movement of the eyes, the look of a man checking whether his life had started yet. My sister sat at my feet, humming, safe, nearly happy. And I sat above her, reading about rivers I believed I would never see, checking the time.

I hated myself with a completeness that frightened me. Then I turned the page and kept reading, because the reading, at least, I could do well.

We would all have gone under that summer, I think, if not for Bathilda.

She came over most days, without ceremony, through the gap in the garden fence. She brought bread, or a pie, or half a ham, always with some excuse — she had baked too much, the shop had made a mistake, it would only spoil. It was years before I understood how carefully all that excess was planned.

She sat with Ariana, too. Not many people could. Bathilda's gift was that she talked without needing answers — a warm, endless river of talk about her garden and her neighbours and above all her book, the famous *A History of Magic*, and the letters she received from important people, and the errors of other historians, who were all fools. Ariana would sit and listen to the river with her ribbon in her hands. It asked nothing of her. It was company with no eyes in it.

Sometimes, listening from the next room, I closed my own eyes and let the river run over me as well. An old woman talking nonsense about goblin treaties. It was the kindest sound in England.

June ended. July began, hotter. My eighteenth birthday waited at the far end of August, and when it came nobody would notice it, least of all I. I settled into the routine like a man settling into cold water. Cage is the word I have been avoiding for three pages. Very well: it was a cage. I had built none of it, and chosen all of it, and I walked its edges every day like something at a zoo, counting my steps.

And then, on an afternoon at the start of July, Bathilda knocked at our front door. This itself was unusual — she always came through the fence. She stood on the step in her good hat, bright-eyed, holding a letter, so full of news she was almost dancing.

Her great-nephew was coming to stay. From the continent. A difficult boy, she said, with the deep satisfaction old women reserve for difficult boys. He had been expelled — expelled! — from Durmstrang itself, and his mother needed him elsewhere for a while, and Bathilda had offered.

"He is terribly clever, Albus," she said, on her way back down the path. "They say he is quite brilliant. Even you might find him interesting."

I remember that I smiled politely.

I remember that I doubted it very much.

## 11. The Boy Next Door

Bathilda's great-nephew arrived at the beginning of July, and I met him the day after, over the garden wall.

I was in our garden with a basket of washing — the washing is important; I want it on record that I was holding somebody's socks — when a young man came over Bathilda's wall. Not through the gate, which stood open three steps away. Over the wall, one hand on the stone, landing in the lane like a cat, laughing at his own landing.

Golden hair to his shoulders. My age, or near it. He looked as if he had walked out of a painting, and knew it, and found the painting rather slow.

"You are the clever one," he said, in place of good morning. His English was quick, lightly accented. "My aunt talks about you the way priests talk about weather. Albus Dumbledore. I have a question about Adalbert Waffling, and everyone I have ever asked has given me a stupid answer. You have one minute to do better."

I should have found him unbearable. Half of me did. The other half had already put down the basket.

He asked his question. It was a good question — a genuinely good question, about the third law, the sort of question I had been asking into empty air my whole life. I began my answer carefully, the way I always did, laying the simple stones first, waiting for the familiar moment when the other person's eyes glazed over.

Let me explain something about that moment, because my whole youth lived inside it.

All my life I had been the cleverest person in the room. I say this without pride; it is simply the fact around which everything else arranged itself. And being the cleverest person in the room means this: every conversation is a staircase, and at some step — sooner, later — the other person stops climbing. You feel it happen. You learn to feel it coming. And you learn to stop two steps early and stand there smiling, so that nobody feels small.

I had done it with teachers. I had done it, gently, with poor Elphias for seven years. I was doing it now, in our lane, with the washing at my feet — slowing early, preparing to stop.

The boy from over the wall did not stop climbing.

He took the stairs two at a time. He interrupted — rudely, wonderfully — finished my sentence, turned it over, found the weak joint in it, and offered me a better sentence, all before the washing line had stopped swinging. Then he stood there, head on one side, waiting to see what I would do about it.

Nobody had ever waited to see what I would do about it.

We stood in that lane for two hours. Bathilda brought us tea at some point; I have no memory of drinking it. I remember arguing about Waffling, and then about wand-lore, and then about the goblin wars, on which he was shockingly well-read and shockingly unjust, and I told him so, and he laughed like a boy given a present.

At some point, out of old habit, I mentioned Cairo. I did it the way I always did it — carelessly, on purpose, a gold medal dropped into the conversation like small change. All my life that trick had worked. Teachers glowed. Students went quiet. Aunts told other aunts.

Gellert looked at me with genuine pity.

"Prizes," he said, "are what schools pay you for staying in the cage. They give the fastest bird the shiniest bars. Tell me something you have done that nobody asked you to do."

I opened my mouth. I closed it again. Seventeen years of medals, essays, and applause, and I stood in the lane with my mouth closed, because the honest answer was: nothing. Everything I had ever done magnificently, somebody had assigned.

No one had ever beaten me in an exam. This was better. I had been found out, and being found out, by the right person, feels shockingly like being praised.

His name was Gellert Grindelwald.

Even now — a lifetime and one black hand later — my quill slows when I write it.

He was seventeen. He had indeed been expelled from Durmstrang, and he wore the expulsion the way other young men wear a medal. When I asked, that first afternoon, what he had done, he waved a hand.

"Experiments," he said. "They have small ideas there, and I did not fit in them. Schools are cages, Albus, for people who cannot fly. The teachers could not forgive me for having wings. Very well — I have saved us both a year of my time."

I want to be honest about this sentence, because it matters. I heard the arrogance in it. I heard something colder under the arrogance — *experiments*, and no detail, and that small wave of the hand. I heard it, noted it, and filed it away under Brilliance, Cost Of. I was the greatest scholar of my age, and I had just discovered a text I did not want to read critically.

And there was this, too, which is not an excuse but is the truth. A boy calls schools cages, and you have spent one month locked in a house with a clock and a drawer full of unlived letters. The word does not land on your judgement. It lands lower, in the place where you live.

I asked him — politely, over the tea I do not remember — why Godric's Hollow, of all sleepy villages in England.

His face changed. The mockery went out of it, and something else came in, and I did not yet have a name for the something else.

"Do you know," he said, "who is buried in your churchyard?"

I named a few old families. He shook his head at each one, letting me fail, enjoying it.

"Ignotus Peverell," he said at last, quietly, the way you lay down a winning card.

The name meant little to me then — an old family, long ended, a worn stone in the yard I had walked past all my life. I said so. Gellert smiled and looked at me over the wall, and drew, with one finger on the stone between us, a small figure: a line, inside a circle, inside a triangle.

"There is a story," he said. "Most people tell it to children. A few people take it seriously. I am one of the few." And then he changed the subject — deliberately, I later understood; he was a master of the well-placed silence — and would say no more about graves that day.

A spark, dropped into dry grass, and then the hand withdrawn. I spent half that night wondering about it, which was, of course, the point.

Here is what I said to myself as the afternoon went on, and I record it because this book exists to record such things.

I had been lonely my whole life. I did not call it loneliness; I called it standing two steps down on every staircase. Doge loved me, and I loved him, but Elphias admired me, and admiration is a wall with a window in it: you can wave through it, but you cannot touch. My teachers were proud of me. My examiners were amazed by me. My mother, God rest her, was grateful for me and worried by me. Not one of them, not once, had run beside me.

That afternoon, for the first time in my life, somebody ran beside me. Somebody ran, at moments, ahead.

It felt like being found.

I have chosen that word with care, and I will defend it. Not impressed — I had been impressing people since I was five, and it is lonely work; the impressed go home, and you stay where you were. Not understood, even, though he understood me faster than anyone before or since. Found. As if I had been standing my whole life in a large empty station, politely, with my luggage, and a train had finally stopped.

The danger of being found, at seventeen, by exactly one person, is a sum that a clever boy should be able to do. If only one person in the world can reach you, then that person can take you anywhere. I did not do the sum. I was too busy being happy, for the first time in my adult life, and happiness — I say this as a warning, not an excuse — happiness is the one subject on which I have never been clever.

We talked until the light went. Bathilda called him in to supper and was ignored; called again, was charmed instead; gave up, delighted, and brought out bread and cheese to the garden wall. The stars came out over the lane. I remember Gellert lying back on the wall like a bench, pointing out the constellations his grandmother had taught him wrong names for, inventing worse ones, making me laugh — really laugh, out loud, in the open air.

At some point the church clock struck eleven, and I realised three things together, standing in the dark lane with cold washing in the basket at my feet.

That I had missed supper entirely, and nobody in my house had come to find me.

That for six hours — six whole hours, the longest stretch since the black-edged letter — I had not once thought about the locked house, the money, the goats, the clock, or the weight of my own name.

And that I wanted, more than I had ever wanted the tour, or the medal, or the applause of the Wizengamot, to climb that wall again tomorrow.

Of the three, it was the second that should have frightened me. Six hours of forgetting, handed to me free, on a summer evening, by a golden stranger. Nothing is free. Forgetting least of all. I had just had my first taste of a very fine drug, and like every first taste it felt like health.

I went in. The kitchen was dark. On the table, under a plate, Aberforth had left me bread and cold meat — my brother fed everything that wandered into his yard, on principle, even me. I ate it standing up, at midnight, smiling at nothing.

Upstairs, my sister was asleep. Across the lane, a light burned in Bathilda's attic window, directly opposite mine.

I noticed that. I noticed it the way you notice a door.

## 12. Two Windows

That July, the lane between our houses became the shortest road in England, and the busiest.

Two houses, side by side, and at the top of each an attic window, and the two windows faced each other across the lane like an unfinished sentence. Mine was mine. The other was Gellert's — Bathilda had given him the attic room, because he wanted somewhere to work, and Bathilda would have given him the roof, the garden, and the deed to the house if he had smiled while asking.

We were together every day. That is a plain fact and it hides everything, so let me try again.

We were together every day, and it was not enough, and so we invented the nights.

It began in the first week. We had argued all afternoon about the Statute of Secrecy — I will come to the Statute — and had been separated at last by supper, by Bathilda, by the ordinary machinery of the world. An hour later, one hour, there was a soft knock of a beak at my window, and there was Bathilda's little owl, looking harassed, with a note tied on much too tightly.

I still know it by heart. It said: *You conceded too early on the third point. You saw something and hid it. Write it down and send it back, or I will not sleep, and my aunt says I am unbearable when I have not slept.*

I wrote four pages. His answer came back before two in the morning, and it was six.

That poor owl. Within a fortnight we had nearly worn her out — she began to hide when either of us came near the window with paper in hand — and so we spared her, some nights, and used sparks instead: a small charm of coloured light, flashed from attic to attic, a code we built in an evening. Green for *yes, you are right, curse you*. Red for *no, and I can prove it*. Gold — we used gold too much — gold for *come to the wall, this cannot wait until morning*.

It could always have waited until morning. Nothing we said that summer could not have waited fifty years, and in the end, most of it did. But I was seventeen, and the flash of gold light on the glass at midnight was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I used to sit in the dark and wait for it, with my heart going like a spellstruck clock.

What did we talk about? Everything. I had spent my whole life storing things to say, against the day somebody could hear them. The dam had broken, and a summer is barely long enough to empty such a dam.

Magic theory, first and always: transfiguration, wand-lore, the deep laws under the spellwork, where he was quick and I was quicker and both of us knew both things without saying them. History, where he was better read than I was and far more certain — Gellert read history the way a general reads maps, looking for the passes. Alchemy. Dragons' blood. The nature of power, treated theoretically, as young men treat everything dangerous.

And then, more and more, crowding out the rest of it: the Statute of Secrecy, and what was wrong with the world.

Understand what the Statute was to us. It was the law under all our lives: wizards hide. Hide the magic, hide the schools, hide yourself, forever, from the Muggles around you. To break the silence was the deepest crime we had.

Gellert's argument ran like this — and I ask you to feel its pull, because if you feel nothing, you will learn nothing from my story. By what right, he asked, does the greater hide from the lesser? We hide, he said, like criminals who have committed no crime. And what has hiding bought us? Read your history — read my aunt's own book. Burnings. Drownings. Fear on both sides of the curtain. Muggles kill what frightens them, he said, and everything unknown frightens them, and we have chosen, on purpose, to stay unknown.

He would walk up and down Bathilda's garden while he talked, and the sun would move on his hair, and he did not talk like a student. He talked like a man reading aloud from the future.

And I? I did not merely listen. Here is the part I would leave out if this book permitted leaving things out. I improved it.

Where his argument was loud, I made it quiet, which made it stronger. Where he said *rule*, I said *guide* — the same knife in a kinder sheath. He was brilliant in speech, but on paper he rushed; I have never rushed on paper in my life. So the finished arguments, the ones polished until no ordinary conscience could find a handhold on them — those were in my hand. He said openly that I was the better writer. He was generous with any praise that cost him nothing. And I — starving, since childhood, for one listener of my own size — I lent my gift gladly. Gladly. I would have lent him anything that summer, and he had a great talent for accepting.

The days went through the garden, and the nights went through the two windows, and the summer caught fire, and it seemed to me that I had never been so awake in my life. That house across the lane held more life than the whole world I had planned to tour.

You will notice that some people have gone missing from this chapter.

I notice it too. I have written the word *golden* many times now, and the word *Ariana* not once, and I promise you the proportion is honest. That is how that July was lived. My sister and my brother did not leave the story. I left them, while standing in the same small house, which is the only way of leaving that fools nobody but yourself.

Ariana had a bad week in the middle of that month. I know it happened. I could not tell you, then or now, which days it was, or what broke, or how long she cried, because I was elsewhere in every way that matters — my body at her table, my mind across the lane. Somewhere in there the burnt-sugar smell stood in the kitchen again, and I noted it the way one notes weather on a day out of doors. Aberforth managed her. Aberforth, that summer, managed everything I had been sent home to manage.

And Aberforth watched.

He did not argue with me — not yet; his argument was coming, and it would arrive all at once, terribly, at the end of August. That July he only watched. He would be mending the goat shed, or milking, or carrying water, always with his sleeves rolled and his hands full of the actual world, and when Gellert's laugh came over the fence from Bathilda's garden, my brother would straighten up slowly and look.

There was nothing hot in the look. That was what made it hard to meet. It was flat, patient, and entirely unimpressed — the look the goats gave me, if I am truthful — the look of someone watching a neighbour's fine new fire and calculating, quietly, the distance to his own roof.

I met that look perhaps a dozen times across a fence that July. I never once went over to ask what it meant. I have written whole books' worth of letters in my life, to ministers and heroes and scholars, and the conversation I most needed was fifteen steps away, by the goat shed, free, and I did not go. Clever is not wise. If this book teaches one thing, let it teach that.

One evening at the end of July, we sat late on Bathilda's garden wall. The light was doing what it does in England only ten evenings a year, and every leaf in the garden had its own gold edge. We had talked ourselves, for once, into silence — a rare, full silence, the kind that only comes when nothing more is needed.

Then Gellert put his hand on my shoulder.

He said something ordinary. That is the truth, and I refuse to improve it: after almost a century I cannot even swear to the words. *Until tomorrow, then* — it was no more than that. An ordinary sentence, an ordinary hand on an ordinary shoulder, between friends, at the end of a summer day.

And the world changed colour. I do not know how else to report it. The garden, the lane, the grey church tower, the first star over the hill — everything stood suddenly in its full colour, as if all my life until that evening I had been reading the world in a good translation, and someone had just opened the original.

He went in. I sat on the wall alone for a long time, being, quietly, someone new.

I was in love. Let the sentence stand alone, in the plainest words I have, because it was the plainest thing about me and I dressed it up for decades as friendship, as partnership, as the meeting of two minds. It was those things too. Under them all, first and last, I was a boy in love, sitting on a wall in the dark.

Did he know? He never said, and I never said, and between us that summer stood the whole grammar of things unsaid. But — yes. I believe he knew. Gellert missed nothing that could be used, and knew everything useful, and what a boy in love will write, and carry, and forgive, and lend — that is among the most useful knowledge in the world.

There. It has taken me a hundred years, one dead hand, and eleven chapters to write four words: I was in love. My biographers may divide the fault among themselves.

Across the lane, as I finally went up to bed, the gold light flashed twice in the attic window.

*Come to the wall. This cannot wait until morning.*

It could have waited until morning. I went down anyway, in my nightshirt, laughing, seventeen, on fire.

## 13. The Greater Good

Every dangerous idea is beautiful when it is young. I want you to understand this, because otherwise you will understand nothing else in this book. If our plan had looked evil, I would have run from it. It did not look evil. It looked like mercy. It looked like the end of every locked door in the world.

We built it through July and August, mostly in Bathilda's garden. There was a bench under her apple tree. The light fell through the leaves in gold coins, and the ideas came faster than we could write them down. Gellert talked with his hands. By August, so did I. We filled three notebooks. We gave the chapters names. We were not playing; that is the frightening part. We were drawing a real map of a real future, and the drawing was very good.

I am going to build the vision for you now, brick by brick, the way we built it then. I will make it as warm as it was. Be careful as you read. That is the purpose of this chapter: I want you to feel how easy it is to walk in.

Start with a fact that nobody argues with. Wizards hide. For two hundred years our world has lived behind a law called the Statute of Secrecy. We may not show Muggles what we are. We close our curtains. We teach our children to lie to their neighbors before we teach them to fly. We make ourselves small, and we call it safety.

Why do we hide? Because Muggles fear us. And fear makes people cruel. Muggles burned witches once — real fires, real screams — and mostly they burned the wrong people, which tells you everything about fear. Fear does not aim. It only strikes.

Now the second brick, and this one was mine. I knew what hiding costs. I had watched the bill arrive at my own front door.

Three boys saw my sister through a hedge. They saw a small girl making light, and nobody had ever told them that such a thing could be good. So they were afraid, and then they were cruel, and my family fell like a tower, one floor at a time. Father. Sister. Mother. If wizards had not been hiding — this was my argument, and I remember the fire in my chest when I made it — those boys would have grown up knowing what magic was. They would have clapped. Nothing makes people cruel like a secret.

Do you feel it? The plan is not for us. The plan is for her. Every hedge in the world torn down, so that no girl, anywhere, ever again pays for being magical in front of the wrong children. Tell me that is not a beautiful thing to want. I cannot tell you that, even now.

Third brick. If wizards simply step out of the shadows, what happens? Panic. Mobs. War. You cannot open a door that has been locked for two hundred years and expect the light to be welcome; the light itself becomes an attack. So the change must be led. Managed. Guided, slowly and wisely, by the people who see the whole board.

And there it is. The little word slides in like a knife between two ribs. Led. Someone must lead. Someone must rule — gently, of course; wisely, of course; only for a time, of course. Rule the way a parent rules a child's bedtime: not for the parent's pleasure, but for the child's protection.

And who should rule? Well. Who was sitting on that bench?

Gellert said it plainly. He said wizards were kings living as beggars. I did not like the word kings — it was too honest — so I found better clothes for the same body. That was my work all summer, though I could not see it at the time. He supplied the hunger; I supplied the dress. I made his appetite sound like duty, and I was proud of how well it fitted.

We even had an answer for the Muggle question. We had read their newspapers. We had seen the weapons they were building, the wars gathering in their century like storm clouds. Wizard rule would be for the Muggles' own good too, we said. We would stop their wars the way a parent takes matches from a child.

Notice one thing about parents and matches. Nobody votes for the parent. Nobody asks the child. That is the whole arrangement, and we called it kindness.

Bathilda would bring us tea and hear a phrase or two before she wandered off. She thought it was wonderful that young people cared about politics. I record this without blame. Tyranny in its first weeks sounds exactly like two polite boys discussing a better world under an apple tree. That is the sound it makes. There is no alarm bell.

One night that August I could not sleep. We had argued that evening — our only kind of argument, fast and joyful, like a duel with no wounds — about force. When may the new order strike? Who decides what is necessary? Gellert thought the question hardly worth asking. I thought it was the whole question, and I sat down at my desk at three in the morning to settle it, the candle bending in the draught from the window. I can still see my own hand moving across the page. It was a young hand then. I wrote:

> Gellert —
> You ask whether we have the right. I have been awake all night with your question, and I believe it is the wrong question. Right is not the word. The word is responsibility.
> If we take power, we take it for the ones who have none. For the Muggles, who cannot save themselves from their own wars. For wizard children who learn to be ashamed before they learn to spell. We do not rule because we are better. We rule because we see further, and seeing further is a duty, not a prize.
> Force only where force is necessary, and not one spell more. And every act, every law, every strike must pass a single test: is it for the greater good?
> Hold to that test, and we cannot become monsters. Power in kind hands is not tyranny. It is medicine.
> Yours, at three in the morning, and entirely certain — A.

There. Now you have seen it born. Four small words, written by candlelight, by a boy who believed every one of them, in a house where his sister lay sleeping two doors away.

I sent it across the lane by owl, though I could have walked it over in my slippers. An answer came back within the hour; he had been awake too. He had underlined the four words twice, and written beneath them one line: *This is our flag.* From that night, the phrase belonged to both of us. We said it as a greeting. We said it instead of goodnight. It closed our letters the way other people write their names. A phrase used that often stops being an idea and becomes a wall in the house you live in; you no longer look at it at all. That is what a motto is for, I have since decided. It is a thought you have agreed to stop examining.

I have gone back to that letter many times in my memory, the way you press a bruise to check that it still hurts. Let me take it apart for you, since taking things apart is my one gift that has never failed.

"We take power for the ones who have none." Notice that nobody had asked them. The Muggles had not written to us. The wizard children had not voted. The ones who have none are wonderfully convenient to serve, because they are never consulted, and so they never say no, and so they never say stop. I have looked for the difference between that arrangement and simple theft, and after nearly a century I can report the difference is the quality of the prose.

"We see further." Perhaps we did. But a man who sees further is exactly the man who stops seeing what is near. I could describe the future of Europe in detail. I could not see a girl on the other side of a wall in my own house.

"Force only where necessary." Necessary is the softest word in any language, and it opens every door it touches. No tyrant in history has ever called his cruelty unnecessary. Every terrible thing done by governments was called necessary by somebody with clean hands and a good vocabulary. I had the best vocabulary of my generation.

"Power in kind hands is not tyranny." At three in the morning I believed this. Here is what I know now: there are no kind hands. There are only hands, and power, and time. Kindness is not a metal. It does not keep its shape under weight.

And under all of it, the deepest fault, the one it took me a lifetime to name. Show me a man with a plan to save the whole world, and I will show you a broken thing at home that he cannot bear to look at. I planned to heal every hedge on earth because I could not sit for one hour with the girl behind my own. The world is a wonderful thing to love. It is far away, it is grateful, and it never asks you to come home by supper.

Notice, too, what our beautiful plan required of me. Travel. Followers. The continent. Great deeds in great places. Every road on our shining map led away from Godric's Hollow. A plan drawn by my conscience would have led up the stairs. The plan was an escape dressed in moral clothes, and the sewing, as I have said, was my own.

The most dangerous lies are the ones that are half true. Hiding does hurt us — true. The hedge was real, the fires were real, and the Muggle wars came exactly as we promised — all true. A whole lie dies quickly in daylight. A half-truth can live for fifty years, because you keep feeding the true half and tell yourself the whole animal is healthy.

I have seen those four small words again since that summer. I saw them carved in stone over the gate of a prison, in letters taller than a man, and people walked under them into cells and did not come out. The world believes Grindelwald chose those words. Let the record, at last, be correct. They were carved first in my own handwriting, by candlelight, at a desk in my mother's house, at three o'clock in the morning, for the greater good.

## 14. The Three Brothers

There is an old story that wizard children hear before bed. Muggle children have their own versions of it, because it is really a story about death, and death belongs to everyone. I will tell it quickly, in my own plain words, because that summer it stopped being a story in our hands and became a plan.

Three brothers came to a river too deep and too fast to cross. Being wizards, they made a bridge out of air and walked over. Halfway across, Death was waiting for them. Death felt cheated — the river was his — but he hid his anger and praised their cleverness, and offered each brother a gift.

The eldest asked for a wand that could never lose a duel. Death cut him one from an elder tree by the bank. The second brother asked for the power to call back the dead, and Death gave him a stone from the river. The third brother, the youngest and the least trusting, asked for a way to leave that place without Death following him. Death, very unwillingly, handed over his own Cloak of invisibility.

You can guess the rest. The eldest boasted about his wand, and someone cut his throat in the night for it. The second turned the stone three times and called back the girl he had loved; she came, but she was cold and sad and did not truly belong to the living world, and in the end he went to join her instead. The youngest wore the Cloak all his life, and Death never found him until he was old, and then he greeted Death like an old friend and went with him gladly, as equals.

The Elder Wand. The Resurrection Stone. The Cloak of Invisibility. Together they are called the Deathly Hallows, and the tale says that the one who unites all three becomes the master of Death.

Most wizards think it is only a bedtime story. A few think the objects are real, scattered somewhere in the world, passing from hand to hand down the centuries. Gellert did not think. Gellert knew. I have never met certainty like his, before or since; it did not argue, it simply stood there, like weather.

He had a mark for them. A vertical line for the Wand, a circle for the Stone, a triangle around both for the Cloak. He drew it everywhere. In the margins of my books. In the dust on Bathilda's piano. In spilled tea on the garden table, with one long finger, while he talked about something else entirely. At the bottom of his letters, where other people put their names. I began to see that mark in my sleep. I suppose that was the intention. He understood, long before the word existed, how a symbol works on a mind: not as an argument but as a drip of water on stone.

And here is the uncomfortable truth about his certainty: it was not madness. We did the work. We spent whole days in Bathilda's library — she owned half the serious history written in our language, and she was happy to see it used. We followed the Wand through the centuries the way you follow a wolf by its kills. It has had many names — the Deathstick, the Wand of Destiny — and its path through history is written in exactly the ink you would expect. This famous duelist, unbeatable for twenty years, found dead in a cellar, his wand gone. That warlord, suddenly invincible, then murdered in his sleep by the student who carried a new wand the next morning. A line of blood, running down the centuries, too straight to be chance. Legends do not usually leave bodies. This one did. I sat in a sunny room with lists of the dead in front of me, and what I felt — write it down, Albus — was excitement.

This was the true reason he had come to Godric's Hollow. Not Bathilda, and not rest, and certainly not me — I came later, an unexpected bonus, as he once said with that smile that made insult feel like a gift. He came because of a grave. Ignotus Peverell, the third brother of the tale — the youngest, the one with the Cloak — is buried in the churchyard at Godric's Hollow. That much is not legend; the stone is there, very old, the name still readable if the light falls sideways.

We went there one evening in late July. The churchyard was empty. The grass stood long between the graves, and the light was that heavy gold that August borrows from honey. Gellert found the stone at once — he had clearly been there many times — and knelt in front of it, and traced the old mark with his finger, slowly, the way you touch the face of someone asleep.

I stood behind him and watched. His head was bent. The low sun made a small fire of his hair. He was silent for a long time, which for Gellert was an event with witnesses.

He laughed at everything, you understand. At schools, at ministries, at professors, at holy books, at me when I deserved it and often when I did not. Mockery was the air he breathed. But not there. In front of that worn stone with a triangle on it, he was quiet and careful and almost afraid, and I thought: so he does have a church. It was the only thing I ever saw him treat with reverence. I should have asked myself, standing in that lovely light, what it means when the single sacred thing in a man's world is an object of unbeatable power.

He spoke, at last, without turning around. "They are real, Albus," he said. "He lay in this ground with one of them folded under his head. The other two are out there, in the world, in somebody's careless hands. Objects like that do not allow themselves to be lost. They wait." Then he stood, and brushed the grass from his knees, and was ordinary again — laughing at the church weathercock, planning supper. But I had seen the other face, the still one, and I am ashamed to say it did not warn me. It moved me. Everyone else worshipped comfortable things. He knelt only to power, and I mistook that for a kind of honesty.

I did not ask what a man like him would do on the day the waiting ended. You will notice, by now, a pattern in what I did not ask.

We divided the Hallows between us without ever quite saying so. It happened the way everything happened that summer: quickly, wordlessly, in the space between two sentences.

Gellert wanted the Wand. He was completely open about it, and I will give him this: he was never a hypocrite; that post was filled. A revolution, he said, needs a spine. Build your new world with armies, and you pay for it in blood and gold and years. But a wand that cannot lose — that is an argument nobody answers. With the Elder Wand, we would never need to make war. We would only need to never lose. Peace through one unbeatable stick. He could make even that sound like simple counting, and I nodded along, because the sums were correct if you agreed never to look underneath them.

The Cloak interested us least, which is its own small joke. Hiding was the thing we had come to abolish. We were seventeen, both of us; we wanted to be seen. Neither of us understood yet that the quietest of the three gifts was the wisest one, and that the brother who asked for it was the only brother who died in his bed.

And I? I told myself I was above wanting. I was the philosopher of our partnership, the conscience, the boy who wrote about responsibility at three in the morning. I did not want power, I said. I wanted justice, knowledge, the healing of the world.

Underneath, I wanted the Stone.

I wanted it the way you want water. Turn it three times in the hand. My mother at the kitchen table, pouring tea. My father with his wand behind his ear, free, laughing, the Azkaban years unhappened. I would say sorry. They would see what I had become — the prizes, the medals, the famous letters — no. Even that is a costume. Strip it further, Albus; this book has no purpose if you stop stripping.

Here is the floor under the floor. With my parents returned, I would be free. Ariana would have her mother again, her father again — and I would have the door. The Stone would do quietly, magically, blamelessly, what I could not decently do myself: it would take my family off my hands. I dressed this wish as grief, and grief made it respectable. The Stone was an escape key wearing mourning clothes.

It took me most of a lifetime to say that sentence. Even now, an old man in a quiet office, I write it and my black hand lies on the page like a dead crow, and I look at it, and it looks back. There is a reason I watch that hand while I write this chapter of all chapters. I will tell you the reason near the end of this book. It has the same shape as everything else I am confessing; by then you will recognize it at a glance.

We swore, one midnight in the garden, to find the Hallows together. It was a real oath — hands clasped, the stars out, both of us solemn for once. Wand, Stone, Cloak. Master of Death. We would begin the search after.

After. We said the word often, and we never once opened it to see what was inside. After the trip began. After the movement had followers. After the world was ready. It was such a small, clean word, and it carried everything we refused to look at. My sister lived inside that word. She ate there, slept there, hummed there, waited there. I never looked in. Of all the magic I have ever studied, none of it frightens me like the magic of the word after: say it, and whatever you cannot face simply disappears.

## 15. What I Did Not See

People say love is blind. Mine was not blind. It was something worse, something with a will of its own: it saw everything, and then it chose. I did not miss the signs that summer. I collected them, examined them, and filed them away where they could not vote. This chapter is the catalogue.

Sign the first. Durmstrang expelled him. Sit with that fact for a moment, as I did not. Durmstrang is not a gentle school. It has a long, comfortable friendship with the Dark Arts; things are taught there that Hogwarts will not even name. And that school — that school — looked at sixteen-year-old Gellert Grindelwald and decided he had gone too far.

I asked him about it once, early on. He laughed, and made a gesture like a man releasing a bird. "Experiments," he said. "They lacked the nerve to follow them." And then a joke about the headmaster's beard, and we were somewhere else, and I was laughing too. Notice the machinery: I asked a serious question, received no answer, got a joke in exchange, and felt that I had profited. I turned his expulsion into a compliment — too large for small men, schools are cages for people who can fly — and the translation was so flattering to both of us that I never checked the original text. What experiments? On what? On whom? A century, and I still do not know. I am no longer sure I want the answer, which tells you the question was always real.

Sign the second, smaller and worse. Little flashes, quick as fish. A village boy's pet rabbit that afterwards would not stop shaking, and Gellert's face as we walked away, interested, the way one is interested in a result. The children of Godric's Hollow, who went quiet when he passed, the way birds go quiet. Once, a wasp on the garden table, held in the air by his idle wand for a very long minute while he finished a sentence about liberty. Each moment small enough to explain. I explained each one. I was an excellent explainer. It was, you might say, my area of genius: I could take any fact and return it improved.

Sign the third. His theory of necessary force. I have shown you my letter; now I must show you what I lent it to. Gellert never used ugly words for ugly things. He never said hurt, or break, or make afraid. He said cost. He said price. He said history's bill. Cruelty came out of his mouth wearing a clean collar, and I — who knew better, who had the finest ear in England for a false note — I listened to the music and corrected the grammar. My letters that summer made his ideas sound nobler than he could make them sound himself. He told me so, delighted. I have received many honors in a long life. That compliment is the one I cannot get off my skin.

Sign the fourth, and now we come to the one that shames me most, so I will slow down.

I told him about Ariana. Everything. I, who had spent seven years at school guarding that story like a wound under a shirt — who had never told Doge, dear loyal Doge, more than a tenth of it — I gave it all to Gellert within two weeks. The hedge. The boys. My father's revenge and his silence in court. The explosions, the burnt-sugar smell, my mother's death. He listened beautifully. Nobody has ever listened to me like that, before or since.

But here is what I noticed, and filed, and hid from myself. He did not listen with pity. He listened with interest. His eyes were bright, the way they were bright over the Peverell grave. Somewhere in the middle of my story he stopped hearing a girl and started hearing an argument. "You see?" he said softly, when I finished. "You see what hiding does to us? Your sister is what the Statute costs. Say that to any wizard with a heart, and he is ours."

He filed my sister away as evidence for the cause. And I — this is the confession — I was grateful. You must understand what those words did to me. For eight years, Ariana's suffering had been a dead weight: useless, shapeless, unspeakable. Pity had done nothing for her. Secrecy had done nothing. And here was a beautiful voice saying her pain could buy a better world — that it meant something, that it could be spent. Nobody had ever made her suffering useful before. Gratitude, I have since learned, is the most dangerous feeling I know. Fear at least keeps its eyes open. Gratitude closes them, in a warm room, and calls it trust.

There is a fifth sign, small and far away, and I add it for completeness, because it shows the sickness had spread past one friendship. Doge was writing to me all summer from his tour — long, happy, terribly spelled letters about monsters out of old Greek stories and a boat that nearly sank, each one ending with the same loyal line: he would cut the trip short in a moment if I needed company. Those letters had been my lifeline in June. By August I was leaving them unopened for days. Kind, plain, honest Elphias — he had become slow reading, you understand. I had richer conversation across the lane. When I finally answered, my letters were short and gracious and empty, the exact letters I had once resented my famous correspondents for sending me. A man's decline is measured best by what begins to bore him.

All that August, one person in Godric's Hollow saw clearly, and he was fifteen years old and smelled of goats.

Aberforth spent that summer being useful in all the ways I found small. He mended the goat shed, roof and rails. He milked, he cooked what I burnt, he sat with Ariana through the bad nights, singing the silly songs that worked when nothing of mine worked. And he watched Gellert over the fence, every day, with an expression I chose to read as jealousy because that reading required nothing of me.

He tried words exactly once. He came to my room one evening, planted himself in the doorway — he never in his life leaned on anything — and said it straight, as he said everything. "He's using you. You're meant to be the clever one. How can you not see it?"

I want to write down what I answered, and I find my hand slows even now. I looked at my brother over the top of a book, and I told him, with a small smile I had learned from Gellert, that I did not expect him to understand, that there were minds whose reach he could not measure, and that the goats, no doubt, were missing him. Then I went back to my page as though he had already left.

The contempt of a brilliant man is an unfair weapon. It cannot be answered, because it does not argue; it simply reseats everyone at a lower table. I had been hit by that weapon myself — by whispers at school, son of the Muggle-hater — and I had sworn never to be on the handle end of it. So much for oaths. My brother stood in my doorway carrying the plain truth, and I made him feel small for the shape of the parcel it came in.

He stood there for a moment longer. I did not look up, but I heard him not leaving, and then I heard him leave. He never tried words with me again — not that summer, and, on this subject, not in all the years since. He went back to the shed and the milking and the watching, and I told myself the matter was settled because the room was quiet. I had yet to learn the most important fact about my brother: he is not a man who argues twice. He files his verdict and waits for the evidence, and the evidence, that August, was already on its way.

Here is what I could not see, and it has taken me nearly a century to phrase: Aberforth could not follow my mind. He never pretended to. But he was not reading minds that summer. He was reading eyes. He had spent his whole childhood learning one skill while I collected prizes — the skill of watching a face for the weather coming. He looked at Gellert and saw the storm. I looked at Gellert and saw the sun. We were both describing the same sky, and only one of us had ever been rained on without an umbrella of words.

One more scene, and then I will let this chapter end, because I have been avoiding the scene since I began it.

A warm evening, the middle of August. Ariana was calm that day — the good kind of calm, the real kind. She had braided her hair herself, badly and proudly. She had been humming in the kitchen; I remember the tune and will not write it. When I passed through with my notes under my arm, she caught my sleeve. "Sit with me, Albus," she said. "Like before. You read, I'll listen. You do the voices."

I was expected at Bathilda's. We were revising the third notebook that evening; the chapter, if you want the full weight of it, was on the protection of the vulnerable under wizard rule. I kissed the top of my sister's head, and I said, "Tomorrow."

She nodded. She was used to tomorrow. It was the word I had been giving her all summer, and she took it without complaint, the way she took everything, and went back to her humming.

There were eleven days left. I have counted them many times since — there are old men who count fortunes, and I count these. Eleven evenings, and every one of them had a tomorrow in it.

I did not sit with her.

## 16. The Argument

By the last week of August, the trip had a date. This is the detail people miss when they tell my story, and it matters: none of it was dreaming anymore. There were maps on Bathilda's table with a route inked in — the coast, the boat, the continent. There was a list of names Gellert meant us to visit, wizards who thought as we did, scattered from France to the Urals like dry wood waiting for a match. There was even a leather trunk, half packed, in my own room. In my own room. I ask you to remember that trunk. Everything I claimed not to have decided was folded inside it.

I had even begun, many times and only in my head, the arrangements for my sister — begun them the way you begin a letter you know cannot be written. Every attempt died at the same sentence, the one where I had to name who would look after her, and for how long, and why that was acceptable. A plan you cannot write down in plain words to a stranger is not a plan. It is a hope wearing a plan's clothes. I knew this. I packed anyway.

Aberforth was due back at Hogwarts within the week. His own trunk stood empty. Instead of packing it, on a bright, ordinary morning, he walked into the sitting room where I was writing and forced the only conversation that mattered.

I can see the room with terrible clarity. My mother's two good chairs. The clock on the mantel with its slow, wooden heartbeat. Sunlight in a long stripe across the floor. Ariana was upstairs, asleep at last; she had had a bad night, full of noises, and the house was holding its breath the way it always did after. That detail matters too. Everything in this chapter matters. I have gone over this morning more times than any morning of my life.

Aberforth did not sit. He stood in the light with his feet apart, like a man expecting the floor to move, and he said, "Tell me the plan for Ariana."

I began to answer and discovered — in the middle of my own sentence, like a man stepping on a stair that is not there — that I did not have one. I had phrases. I said the world we were building would be safe for people like her. I said arrangements could be made, care could be found; I believe I said the word temporary. I had led the Wizengamot's youth delegation. I had debated professors into applause. And my brother, who never finished a book in his life, took my beautiful sentences apart with his bare hands, one by one, because he was not listening to the music. He was checking the sums.

"She can't ride a crowded train," he said. "She can't stand loud voices. Strange rooms, strange people — you know what happens, you've seen what happens. You're going to drag her across Europe? On your crusade?" And then, when I offered more phrases: "Or you'll leave her. With who? Bathilda? For how long? Say it plainly, Albus. Your plan has no place in it for her. It never did."

He was younger than both of us, and he was the only adult in the room. His voice was getting louder now, and I remember glancing up at the ceiling — at her — and even that small glance was accounting, was managing, was anything but answering him.

Then the door opened, and Gellert came in.

He did not knock. He had stopped knocking weeks before, and I had liked it — proof of how completely our two houses had grown into one. He came in with a map roll under his arm, took in the scene, and smiled, and stayed. This was a family matter, in a family room, between brothers. He was not family. I should have sent him out; one sentence would have done it. I let him stay. My brother looked from him to me, and I watched him understand exactly what the new order of things was, and file it where he filed everything, in that hard, slow, permanent memory of his.

What followed had the shape of an avalanche: slow at first, then all at once.

Aberforth repeated his questions for the new audience, louder. Gellert answered them, and his answers were worse than mine — smoother, higher, colder. He spoke about history, and destiny, and the courage to think in centuries. My brother said he didn't need a century, he needed to know who was going to sit with Ariana on Thursday. I want that exchange preserved exactly, because the whole summer is inside it. One of them was speaking about mankind. The other was speaking about Thursday. It has taken me a lifetime to understand that Thursday was the larger subject.

Then the greater good came out. When it did, my brother laughed — one short, ugly bark — and said the greater good could not milk a goat, could not calm a frightened girl at two in the morning, could not do one single useful thing in the real world where his actual sister actually lived. He was magnificent. It has taken me my whole life to say so.

And Gellert's voice began to drop.

You must understand about his voice. Loud, he was harmless — loudness was theatre, and we both enjoyed it. The tell was quietness. When Gellert's voice went soft and slow and pleasant, something in the room was about to be broken. I knew that tell the way a sailor knows a green sky; I had catalogued it in the same drawer as the rabbit and the wasp, the drawer marked things I have decided not to know.

The voice went soft. He looked at my brother with those bright, interested eyes, and he was clever about it — he was always clever about it — a sentence with a smile in its mouth and a razor in its sleeve. He called Aberforth a small mind, in prettier words. He suggested that a boy so fond of the animals might stay behind with them, where he was suited. And then he said something about Ariana.

I will not write the sentence down. Understand me: not because I have forgotten it. Because I remember it exactly, every word in its place, the way you remember the shape of the stone you tripped on at the cliff edge. It was brief. It was elegant. It reclassified my sister from a person into a problem of logistics, and it did this with a small, light smile, and the voice never rose at all.

Aberforth went for his wand.

He was quick. He was always quicker than I gave him credit for. But Gellert had been a duelist in a hard school since he was eleven, and Gellert was already smiling, and Gellert's wand was already out. He said the curse the way you say pass the salt.

The Cruciatus Curse. I had read about it — I had read about everything; that was my contribution to the world that morning, that I was extremely well read. My brother folded onto our mother's floor into the stripe of sunlight and screamed. I had never heard a human being make that sound. I have heard it since, in other rooms, in a war, and every single time it has been August again, and the clock, and the light on the floor.

And here is the sentence this whole book has been walking toward, so let me stand still and say it properly.

I drew my wand on Gellert Grindelwald at last. Not when he mocked my brother. Not when he sneered at my sister — I stood through the sneer; I heard the sentence I will not write, and my wand stayed where it was. I drew it when he tortured my brother in front of me, on my mother's floor, and not one second before. That was where my line was, after two months of him. I will let you judge how far away that is from where a line should be.

What happened next has no order in my memory, and I distrust any account of a duel that has one. It was not fencing. It was a burst pipe. Three wands in a small room — because Aberforth was up again almost at once, shaking, on one knee and then on both feet; he was never as breakable as I told myself he was — three wands, and every schoolboy restraint gone in the first exchange.

I remember light with colors I have no names for. I remember my mother's chairs bursting like shot birds, the clock leaving the mantel, the window turning to white sugar and leaving the frame. I remember the noise — that is the thing the songs and the storybooks leave out of duels, the sheer noise of it, curse and counter-curse and wood and glass, a house being unmade around three shouting people. I remember that some part of my mind was cold and clear and thrilled, and was measuring angles, and was, God help me, enjoying being extended at last — and that it was the trained, prized, applauded part, the best part of me by every measure the world had ever used.

People have asked me — carefully, sideways, over the years — what I was casting in that room. Here is the truest answer I can give. I do not remember choosing curses. I remember casting to win. My brother, I believe, was casting to protect. Gellert — I have never let myself finish the sentence about what Gellert was casting to do. But I remember my own state, and I will not pretty it: I was not defending anyone by then. I was in the fight completely, all my skill unlocked at last, and the fight had stopped being about Ariana within the first three seconds. It had become about the two of us, as everything that summer had become about the two of us.

We were three of the most dangerous boys in Europe, in a room the size of a boat's cabin, and not one of us was thinking about the noise.

Noise always hurt her.

## 17. Ariana

The noise woke her. Noise always hurt her. It had hurt her since she was six years old.

She came down the stairs. Nobody saw her. Nobody heard her. We were three wands and a burning room. She was small, and barefoot, and fourteen.

Maybe she came to make it stop. Maybe she came to find Aberforth. He was the one she looked for when the world got loud. I will never know why she came. Add it to the list.

A light crossed the room.

I will not tell you its color. I do not know its caster. Three wands were casting. Any one of us could have thrown it. Nobody ever knew whose it was. Nobody ever will.

She fell without a sound. After all those years of explosions, she left the world quietly. She was on her back on our mother's floor, in the sunlight, and she was not moving.

The duel died in the air. Everything stopped at once, like a clock hit by a hammer.

The silence after is the loudest thing I have ever heard. I have stood in it for almost a hundred years. I am standing in it now, as I write this line in a quiet office at night. Some rooms you never leave.

Aberforth went to her. His legs would not carry him — the curse was still in them — so he crawled. My brother crawled across the broken glass to our sister. He said her name. He said it softly, the way he called her in from the garden for warm milk. She did not answer. He said it again. She did not answer.

I looked at Gellert.

One look. That is all I got, and I have turned it over for nearly a century like a coin in the dark. What was in his face? Fear — of what he had done, or of what it would cost him? Calculation — the map already redrawing itself, the plan already walking away from the wreckage? Grief? Was any part of it grief?

I have never decided. That is the honest answer. I have built him a hundred faces over the years, kind ones and monstrous ones, and I cannot swear to any of them. I saw one look, across a ruined room, over my sister's body. Then he was gone through the door.

By morning he had left Bathilda's house. By the next night he was on the continent. He did not come back. He did not write. Not that week.

He left no note. I looked for one. I am telling you everything in this book, so I will tell you that too: while my sister lay dead in the next room, I searched the morning post. There was nothing.

But that came later. First there was the floor.

I knelt on my mother's floor and held her. I held her for a long time. She grew cold slowly, the way a kettle does, and I could not find the moment when it happened, and I have looked for that moment ever since in everything.

And even then — I want this written down, because it is the truest thing I know about myself — even then, my mind was working. The mind that never stops. The prize-winning mind. It was already measuring the room. Which curse. Whose angle. Whose wand. It laid out the whole problem for me, neatly, while my sister's hair was against my mouth. It has always been a superb machine. It has never once known when to stop.

I refused the answer. I closed the problem unworked — the first problem I ever refused in my life — and I have kept it closed through everything since, at prices you will learn later in this book.

She was fourteen. She liked the goats, and humming, and my worst small charms. She braided her own hair, badly and proudly. She asked me to sit with her, and I said tomorrow.

We killed her — one of the three of us. And I have spent my life not knowing which, because not knowing was the only mercy I could arrange for myself.

Bathilda came that evening. She stood in the broken doorway. She looked at the room, and at us. She asked no questions. She was a historian; she knew a battlefield when she saw one. She made tea that nobody drank, and she stayed until dark, and I have loved her for it ever since.

Aberforth and I did not speak that day. Or the next. There was nothing safe to say. Every sentence in the house had her name in it. So we moved around each other like two men carrying glass.

I do not remember that week in order. I remember pieces. The clock, face down, still trying to tick. My mother's chair, burnt down one side. And the trunk in my room, half packed for the continent. I unpacked it. That is the one act of the week I remember from start to finish. I folded everything. I put the maps at the bottom of a drawer. I closed it quietly, the way you close a door on someone sleeping.

Outside, that morning, the goats were crying to be milked. The world does not stop. That is the worst thing I know about it, and also, I have come to think, the kindest.

## Part Three: The Long Silence

*There is a kind of silence that is not peace. It is a held breath, and mine lasted forty-six years. This part is the story of that breath — the school, the mirror, the letters I did not open, the good I did with small things while a great evil grew. Notice, as you read, how comfortable silence can be. That was always its danger.*

## 18. The Funeral

We buried Ariana on a bright morning at the start of September. The weather was wrong. It was the kind of day she liked best — soft sun, a little wind, the smell of cut grass. A day for feeding the goats early, before the village woke. The sky had no right to be so blue, and I remember being angry at it, because being angry at the sky was easier than the other thing.

The churchyard at Godric's Hollow is very old. Wizards and Muggles lie in it side by side, quiet at last about their differences. Our mother had been buried there in June. Now the ground beside her was open again. The same old man with the same spade stood at a polite distance, waiting for our grief to finish so that his work could begin.

From where I stood I could see the grave of Ignotus Peverell. It is one of the oldest stones in the yard, and cut deep into it is the mark: the triangle, the circle, the straight line. The sign of the Deathly Hallows. Six weeks earlier, Gellert had knelt at that stone with his golden head bent, like a man at prayer.

I noted the mark that morning the way one notes a stain on a wall. It was there. My sister's coffin was also there. The two facts stood together in one small field, and I stood between them, and I understood the arithmetic perfectly. I have never needed help with arithmetic.

Few people came. That was the price of the locked doors: you cannot hide a girl for twelve years and then expect the village to weep for her. The neighbours who came, came mostly for the story. I heard the whispers even at the graveside. *The sister — the one who was ill. The one nobody ever saw. And the mother, only this summer. Unlucky family.* Unlucky. There are worse words, and I was waiting for them.

Bathilda was there, in old-fashioned black, crying without any shame at all. She had lost something too, though she did not yet understand what her house had been used for that summer. She pressed my hand and called me a poor dear boy. I let her. It was not the moment to explain that the poor dear boy was part of the reason we were all standing there.

And Doge came. I want to write this slowly, because it deserves slowness.

Elphias Doge was in Greece when the news reached him — the grand tour, the one I should have been on, the boat tickets, the dragons still ahead of him. He read the letter, packed his terrible luggage, and came home. He cut short the journey of his life for a funeral of a girl he had never once met.

He did not say anything clever. He was not able to say anything clever; it was the great gift of his nature. He said, "I'm here, Albus," and then he stood next to me, slightly too close, the way a plain loyal dog stands, and he stayed there for the whole of it.

I had spent seven years at school being admired by clever people. In that churchyard I learned the exact worth of a plain one. Cleverness, I have found, always wants something back. Doge just stood there. If you have such a friend, reader, and you are young, you may not yet know what you have. You will.

Aberforth stood across the grave from me. Not with me — across, with the width of our sister between us. He was sixteen. He wore a black coat that had been our father's and did not fit him, and he had not spoken one word to me since the sitting room. His eyes were fixed on the coffin, and his face was the face of our mother: straight, proud, closed.

The service began. An old wizard said the usual things in the usual gentle voice — rest, and peace, and the mercy of the next world. I did not hear most of it. My mind, which never once in my life has had the decency to stop, was working even then. It kept starting to measure — the angles of the room, the order of the spells, whose wand had been where. Each time, I shut the door on it. I had already decided not to know. It was the one decision of that whole year I have never gone back on.

They began to lower her. And that was when my brother moved.

He came around the open grave, not quickly, not in rage — that was the terrible part, how calm he was — and he stood in front of me, and he broke my nose with his fist.

I went down. There was blood on my mouth and on my collar, and the sky I had been angry at was suddenly above me, very blue and very far away. Bathilda gasped. Somewhere a woman gave a little scream. Doge stepped forward — plain, loyal Doge, ready to defend me from the one attack in my life I had fully earned.

I caught his sleeve from the ground and stopped him. It is one of the few actions of that year I am proud of.

Nobody else moved. The old wizard stood with his book open. Aberforth looked down at me for a moment, and then he walked back around the grave to his place, and the service went on, and I got to my feet with my nose bleeding into a borrowed handkerchief, and we buried our sister.

I must try to explain what I felt, lying on that grass, because it was not what the gasping neighbours supposed. It was relief. Pure, warm, enormous relief — the first honest thing anyone had done to me in a week. All week people had been kind. Kindness, to a guilty man, is a slow poison; every gentle word says *you poor thing*, and the guilty man knows better. My brother's fist was the only sentence spoken at that funeral that I believed.

He did not say, "It is your fault." He had said that already, in the sitting room, over her body, before. At the grave he only hit me. My brother has always been economical. He makes a thing once and does not repeat it: a judgment, a blow, a silence. I have accepted many compliments in my life with less agreement than I accepted that punch.

The nose was never set quite straight. Healers offered, over the years; there are simple spells. I always declined, politely, giving no reason. You have seen portraits of me, perhaps. The crooked nose is not an accident that was never mended. It is the one sentence of the verdict I could carry on my face.

I chose the words for the stone myself — my mother and my sister share it. They are old words about treasure, and about where the heart lies. I believed them when I chose them. I believe them now. I have simply never managed, in more than ninety years, to live by them for longer than an evening at a time.

Two days later, Aberforth went back to Hogwarts. Term had begun; there was nothing else to be done with a boy of sixteen. I walked him to the edge of the village — that is, I walked several steps behind him to the edge of the village, because he did not slow down and I did not dare to catch up. He got into the cart. He did not look at me. The cart went, and the long silence between my brother and me began, and it has lasted, with a few small breaks, to the night on which I write this.

Then I closed the house.

I understood, doing it, what my mother's life had been. Room by room, shutter by shutter. The kitchen wall Ariana broke and Mother mended. The scorch mark on the stairs. The curtains that were always drawn, which I now drew again out of pure habit, though there was no longer anyone inside to hide. When I turned the key in the front door I stood on the step for some time with the key in my hand. Locked doors had been the great shame of my childhood. Here I was, locking one, and there was no one left behind it. I had, in a way, achieved my dearest wish. I was free. I have never in my life felt anything less like freedom.

I took a room in the village for the last few weeks of that September. I ate at Bathilda's table when she insisted. At night there was silence — real silence, the kind I had claimed for years to be starving for. No explosions. No goats. No humming. I sat in that silence every evening and could not read a page.

And then, near the end of the month, the letter came.

A foreign owl, large and tired, with the dust of the continent on its feathers. One envelope. My name on it in handwriting I knew as well as my own — better, perhaps, for I had studied it all summer by candlelight, the impatient G, the words leaning forward as if running downhill.

Gellert's letter. The only one he ever sent me after that morning, or the only one that ever arrived.

I held it for a long time. I want to be exact here, because it matters: I was not calm. My hands shook. Everything in me that had loved him — and it had not stopped, grief does not cancel love, whatever the poets pretend — everything in me wanted to open it. It might contain sorrow. It might contain an explanation. It might contain the one fact I had decided to live without: whose curse it was. It might contain three words that would have unmade me entirely.

I lit a candle, and I burned it unopened, and I sat watching until the last corner went black.

People to whom I have half-told this story — there are one or two — always ask the same question: how could I bear not knowing what was inside? They misunderstand the act. I did not burn the letter although I longed to know what was in it. I burned it because I longed to know. I have wondered ever since what was in it. That was the point of burning it. The wondering was the punishment I could arrange; the knowing might have been either better or worse, and I had lost the right to anything better.

The ash went into Bathilda's garden with the rest. Somewhere under her roses, I suppose, it is still there.

## 19. The Empty House

Autumn came, and the offers came with it.

Fame has a short memory for grief and a long one for prizes. The world had heard, vaguely, that young Dumbledore had suffered some family misfortune — sad business, unlucky family — and the world waited what it considered a decent interval, which was about five weeks, and then the owls began to arrive.

The Ministry wrote first. There was a place for me, the letter said, a fast road: a junior post shaped exactly like the first step of a great career, with the second and third steps politely visible behind it. An alchemist in Paris — a very great one, whose letters I had once shown off to half of Hogwarts — invited me to work beside him. There was a chair being prepared at a foreign academy. There was a research position in Cairo, where they remembered my gold medal.

I read each letter at Bathilda's kitchen table, and I felt the old machine wake up and begin, quite by itself, to plan. That is what frightened me. Grief had not broken the machine. My mother was dead and my sister was dead and my brother would not look at me, and the machine still purred at the word *career* like a cat at the sound of a tin.

So I refused them all, and I wrote instead to Hogwarts, and I asked whether they needed a teacher.

They did — Transfiguration, as it happened, the subject I loved best. The salary was small. The title was nothing. When it became known, people who admired me were openly disappointed, and said so, in that special voice reserved for wasted promise. The cleverest student of his age, burying himself in a schoolroom, marking the essays of eleven-year-olds. A waste, they said. Even Doge, the loyalest man alive, allowed himself one puzzled question, and accepted my answer, and never asked again.

I gave everyone the same answer, and it was even true, as my best lies always were: that teaching was honourable work, and that I had had enough of ambition for a while. But since this book exists to say the things under the things, let me open the answer up. There were three reasons, and I understood only one of them at the time.

The first reason I did understand, and it was this: a school is the place where clever children arrive before they have decided what their cleverness is for. I had been such a child. One summer, one golden-haired friend, one beautiful idea at the wrong moment — and I had become something that still made me sick to remember. Nobody had caught me. The teachers had given me prizes; not one of them had looked past the prizes and seen the hunger underneath, or asked what it might do when it left the castle. I wanted to be the one who stood at that door. I wanted to catch the boy I had been, next time he came through — and he would come through; his sort always does. It took thirty-nine years, but he came.

The second reason I only half-understood. A school is made of walls, terms, bells, and lists. Everything in it repeats. September answers to September; the timetable is a spell against surprise. I told myself I found this restful. What I actually found it was *safe* — a cage, if you like, but a cage I had chosen and fitted to my own measurements. My father of a family had been a cage, and I had beaten against it so hard that a girl died. Very well. I had learned what I do with freedom. I would not be trusted with it again. Hogwarts had bars enough, and the bars were painted to look like towers, and I have gripped them gratefully for nearly a hundred years.

The third reason I could not have said aloud then at all: the castle was the only place left that had ever felt like home. The house in Godric's Hollow stood locked and empty. The kitchen at Mould-on-the-Wold existed only in my memory, where I visited it far too often. But Hogwarts — Hogwarts had lamplight, and noise at breakfast, and staircases with opinions. When I walked back through its gates that autumn with one small trunk, something in my chest unclenched. I was ashamed of it even as it happened. The reader of this book will notice that I am ashamed of most things that have ever comforted me. I offer no cure for this; I never found one.

The house in Godric's Hollow I neither sold nor opened again. I paid a village man a little each year to keep the roof sound and the garden from becoming a scandal, and I told myself this was practical — in case Aberforth should ever want the place. Aberforth never wanted the place. Twice in those first years business took me through the village, and both times I found my feet arranging, without instruction, to pass along a different lane. An empty house belongs to a family the way a name does: you may leave it, but no one else may have it. It stands there still, mine and not mine, curtains drawn on rooms where nothing happens. I have owned it for ninety-seven years, and since that September I have not slept in it one night.

Here is a thing nobody tells you: a brilliant mind is a bad teacher. Not a good one waiting to happen — a bad one, to begin with, and I was.

I had never in my life failed to understand anything on the first attempt, and so I had no idea what not-understanding felt like from the inside. I would explain a transformation clearly — beautifully, even — and a boy would blink at me, lost, and I would explain it again in the same words, only slower and colder, as if his difficulty were a discipline problem. I was never cruel in the open. I was worse: I was patient in a way that let a child feel exactly how much patience he was costing me. A girl in my second year of teaching cried over a matchstick, and I understood, with horror, that she was crying because of my voice.

I had wanted to catch clever children before they became me. It had not occurred to me that I would have to begin by catching me.

That girl taught me more than Cairo ever had. I asked her, the next lesson, to stay behind — she stayed with the face of one awaiting execution — and I put the matches away and asked her instead about her cat, which she had mentioned once in passing. We talked about the cat. Then about what whiskers are for, and how a thing may keep its nature while changing its shape. She transformed her match on the following Tuesday. It was the ugliest needle I have ever seen — bent, and still half wood at one end — and I have been handed medals that moved me less.

So I learned. Slowly, the way the slow learn, which was itself the lesson. I learned that a mind that cannot follow yours is not a wall but a door with a different key, and that finding the key is the entire craft. And I discovered something that the boy of 1899, planner of continental revolutions, would never have believed: there is a particular feeling when a confused child suddenly understands — when the match becomes a needle at last, after forty failures, and the child looks up at you with the whole morning in his face. It is a very small feeling. It repairs nothing and changes no laws and will never be printed in any newspaper.

I had spent a summer drunk on the greater good. This was the good at hand — one child, one hour, one match. I built my life out of it the way my brother, one village and a world away from me, was building his out of goats and warm milk. It took me most of a lifetime to notice we had made the same choice, and that his version was the honester one.

For two more years, my brother and I lived in the same castle. He sat at the long tables; I sat at the staff one. We passed in corridors. A teacher and a student who happen to be brothers must exchange a certain number of words, and we exchanged exactly that number and none over. He left school without saying goodbye. I heard from Bathilda — from Bathilda, mark that — that he had gone to work with animals, and later that he had taken over a public house in Hogsmeade. One village away from mine. We are neither of us, it seems, able to leave; we have simply arranged never to arrive at the same place.

The winters at Hogwarts are white and long. I found I liked the snow: it makes silence visible, and I had a great deal of silence to look at. In the evenings there was marking, and the staffroom fire, and the papers from London a day late.

And one evening in my early years there — I could find the exact date, I have never wanted to — I sat in the staffroom with tea going cold, reading the foreign pages of the newspaper, and found a small item at the bottom of a column. Disturbances in the east of Europe, it said. Villages emptied of Ministry men; documents missing; a small circle of young wizards around a leader whose name the paper had, in the way of papers, misspelled.

But not so badly that I could pretend. It was him.

I sat quite still and attended to my own body, as a healer attends to a patient. My breathing: even. My face, in the dark window opposite: mildly interested, nothing more. My hands on the newspaper: steady. Perfectly steady. I noted the item, and I noted my steadiness, and I chose — the word is *chose* — to be reassured by it, when I should have been ashamed of it, because a steady hand at such news is not calm. It is refusal, wearing calm's clothes.

Then I turned the page. There was a report on cauldron regulations. I read every word of it.

Turning the page took me forty-six years.

## 20. The Mirror

There is an object I must now tell you about, and I would rather not. You will understand my reluctance by the end of the chapter. It has followed me, that object, or I have followed it; after ninety years I am no longer sure which of us is the ghost.

In my early years as a teacher — the exact year does not matter, and I have taken care to forget it — I came across a mirror in a part of the castle where no mirror needed to be. Hogwarts collects things. Rooms wander; cupboards acquire contents; objects arrive without histories, like cats. This was a tall mirror in a gold frame, standing on clawed feet, facing the door as if it had been waiting for it to open.

Across the top of the frame ran an inscription, carved in letters that read backwards, so that only a careful eye — or a mirror — could make sense of them. I had a careful eye. I read the warning, and I knew at once what the thing was, for I had read of it: the Mirror of Erised, which shows neither your face nor the room behind you, but the deepest desire of your heart.

I want it recorded that I knew. Not suspected — knew. I knew what it was, I knew what it did, and I knew what such devices do to the people who consult them. I stood in the doorway with my hand on the frame of the door, in full possession of more theory than any wizard of my age in Europe.

Then I stepped inside and looked. Of course I looked. Knowledge has never yet stopped a man at a door; it only means he cannot claim afterwards that he wandered through it by accident. This entire book, you may have noticed, is the confession of a man who always knew better and went in anyway.

What I saw was a kitchen.

The kitchen at Mould-on-the-Wold, which by then had not existed for years — other people lived in that house and had surely repainted everything. But there it stood in the glass, complete, down to the burn on the table edge. My mother was pouring tea. She was laughing — my mother, who laughed perhaps four times in the years I knew her — laughing at something my father had said, and my father sat with his boots on the fender and his wand behind his ear, exactly as the Ministry had taken him from us, only easy, only unafraid, a man with nothing in the world to hunt.

Aberforth was there, mid-laugh himself, his head thrown back. His nose was unbroken. So, in the glass, was mine.

And Ariana. The mirror did not show me the girl of fourteen. It showed her grown — a young woman by the window with our mother's dark hair, saying something over her shoulder, entirely well. Not radiant, you understand. Not an angel, nothing the village would ever have turned to look at twice. Ordinary. Marvelously, immortally ordinary: a woman with a teacup, in a kitchen, safe, with her whole boring beautiful life in front of her. Of everything the mirror ever showed me, it is her ordinariness I cannot forgive it.

And at the edge of the frame, sometimes, not always, there was golden hair. I have now told you, and we will move on, which is more mercy than the mirror ever showed me.

I went back the next night. I imagine you guessed.

I went back the next night, and the night after, and I discovered what the warning on the frame had been for. The mirror does not trap you with anything so crude as magic. It simply shows you the one thing you cannot have, and lets your own heart do the walking. I began to mark essays quickly after supper — I, who preached care to my students — to free the later hours. I took corridors on my evening rounds that had no reason in them except that they ended at a certain door. Within a month I had, without once admitting it, rebuilt my entire evening around a piece of furniture. The cleverest man in the castle, scheduling his life by a glass, as a drunkard schedules his by a bottle.

And the man in the glass was always the same age, but the man in front of it was ageing, one night at a time.

I kept, at first, to a quarter of an hour, measured by my watch. Then the watch began to lie to me — or I began to lie to it, which is how these things truly go. I would set myself fifteen minutes and surface after ninety, cold to the bone, with no memory of the time between, the way drinkers speak of lost evenings. The scenes were not fixed, either. Some nights the kitchen; some nights the garden at Mould-on-the-Wold, before the hedge meant anything; and once, unbearably, the funeral morning itself — but mended. Aberforth stood at my side instead of across the grave, his hand on my shoulder, both of us older, as if the glass wished me to know that it could do futures too — futures of the past, the might-have-beens, which are the only futures a heart entirely believes in.

On the worst night of that winter I heard the breakfast bell ring while I still stood there, and understood that I had been in front of the mirror from supper until morning, and had felt it as an hour. I taught that day, I am told, quite normally. I remember none of it.

I recognized it, in the end. That was my single advantage over the others who have stood there: I had felt this exact hunger before, and I knew its name. This was the Stone — the Resurrection Stone, the second Hallow, the one I had told myself I wanted for noble reasons in a golden garden in 1899. The wanting had not died with Ariana. It had gone quiet, and waited, and now it had found a new door with my measurements written on it. The dead in the glass, like the dead the Stone promised, asked nothing of me. That is the whole secret of their charm. The living are expensive; the dead in the mirror are free, and they are always at home, and they are always glad to see you.

Men have wasted away in front of that mirror. They have sat down before it and forgotten to eat. I was young, unmarried, guilty, and lonely; I had buried everyone I loved or driven them away; I was, in short, as well qualified to waste away before it as any man alive.

Whether what I did next was strength or only fear, I have never settled. I stopped. I made myself a rule of the kind I understand — absolute, exact, no exceptions — and then, not trusting the rule, I arranged for the mirror to be moved. I did not ask where it was taken. You will have noticed by now that this is a habit of mine: I arrange not to know things. Not knowing where the letter's words went, not knowing whose curse it was, not knowing which room now held my family. A man who cannot trust himself with a door learns to lose the address.

I will not pretend the stopping was one clean act. Nothing true is. There was a last visit, which I permitted myself the way a drunkard permits himself a last glass, and I stood before them all for a long while — the tea, the boots on the fender, the dark-haired woman by the window — and said goodbye to each in turn, silently, like a man walking through the portrait gallery of a house he has sold. None of them noticed. That is the mirror's final unkindness, and its most instructive one: you may break your heart taking leave of the dead in the glass, and the dead in the glass are not taking leave of you. They are not doing anything. There is no one there.

For weeks afterwards I caught my feet turning toward the old corridor in the evenings, and had to turn them back, the way one turns back a dog.

In later years I learned to watch for the mirror's other victims, for I could not be the only one the castle had ever introduced it to. Once you have had the sickness you know its face: a student missing from meals, a tiredness that sleep does not explain, eyes that in the middle of a conversation go elsewhere — to a room only they can see. Over the decades I found three. I did not lecture them. I asked each what the glass showed, and listened, and then told them, this being the only medicine I know, that the mirror gives neither knowledge nor truth, and that it eats the hours it is given and gives nothing back. I never told them how I knew. Physician, they say, heal thyself. I had at least learned to recognize the disease.

It was on one of those walks — the castle black and freezing, snow at every window, my feet aching with cold in the long stone dark — that I first built the lie. I reasoned that a man who looks as I was beginning to look, a man collecting a reputation, would one day be asked directly: *what do you see in the mirror of your heart?* And the truth was not sayable and never would be. So the lie should be prepared in advance, like a spell; and it should be small, and warm, and a little absurd, so that the asker would smile and stop asking. That night, walking on frozen feet past white windows, I chose it. Socks. Thick woolen socks. A man can always use another pair; no one ever thinks to give them; there is no grief in them anywhere. I put the lie away in its drawer, and it waited there, warm and folded, for the better part of a century.

There is no mirror in my office. Visitors assume it is modesty. Even now, writing this at night, I sit with the window at my back.

Ninety years after those winter evenings, in front of that same glass, a boy of eleven looked up and asked me the exact question I had prepared for. But he deserves his own chapter, and he will get it.

## 21. The Minister's Owl

The first time the Ministry of Magic offered to make me Minister, I had chalk on my sleeve and a second-year's essay on my desk with the word *Transfiguration* spelled three different ways in one page, none of them correct.

I will not fix the year precisely; it does not matter, and precision would only feed the historians, who have been wrong about this episode in print so many times that I have come to enjoy it. It is enough to say that the times were nervous, the previous Minister was leaving under a cloud, and somebody in London remembered that the greatest talent of his generation was inexplicably folding paper birds in a Scottish classroom.

I knew it was coming a little before it came. Doge, who sat on two committees and heard everything, had warned me over dinner with the delight of a man delivering a birthday present a week early. And the Prophet had begun printing my name in that speculative way papers have, seated next to the word *leadership* like a couple placed together at a wedding to see what develops. So when the owl came down to the staff table at breakfast, in front of the whole school, the Ministry seal shining like a coin — they meant it to be seen; that too was part of the letter — I was able to open it with a face I had prepared in advance.

The owl was magnificent — the Ministry is not mean with owls when it wants something — and the letter was long, and I reproduce its heart from memory, which for my sins is exact:

> The times, as you know better than most, are dark, and grow darker. The Wizengamot is divided; the public is afraid; abroad, matters stand worse still. What is wanted is not a politician but a mind — and, forgive us, a name. No one else in Britain commands such respect. No one else could unite us. We are aware of your modesty, but there are hours when modesty itself becomes a luxury, and we believe this is one of them. Your country asks.

It was a good letter. Whoever wrote it knew his business: every sentence held a hook, and every hook was baited with duty rather than glory, because they had guessed — correctly — that I was the kind of vain man who is only caught by humility. I read it twice. Then I did something I have never told anyone, and which it is the whole purpose of this book to tell.

I did not write a refusal. Not at first. I took a clean sheet of parchment — I remember the candle, the red ink I marked essays with, the quiet — and at the top of it I wrote, in a neat hand, *What I would do.*

I told myself it was an exercise. A man should refuse with a full understanding of what he refuses; that was the theory, and it lasted perhaps four lines. What happened after that was not an exercise. The list began to write itself, and I began to enjoy it, and the machine — the old machine, the summer machine — woke from its long sleep with a stretch and a purr and said, *at last.*

And such measures, reader. Such wise, clean, reasonable measures. Reform of the Wizengamot — I knew its weaknesses to the man. Protections for Muggle-born wizards; I drafted the law in six lines and it was better than the one we have now. A rebuilt treaty with the goblins, schools inspected honestly, the Prophet held to account for its lies, quiet pressure here, a firm hand there — only where necessary, of course, only for a time, of course — and each measure summoned the next, and my quill went faster, and the candle burned down, and I was no longer in a schoolmaster's office at all. I was seventeen, in a bedroom in Godric's Hollow at three in the morning, writing to a golden-haired boy across the lane about how we two would arrange the world for its own good.

The hand was the same hand. The ink might as well have been the same ink.

I read my beautiful list back from the top, and I heard it. Under every line of it, like a watermark, the old letter: *for the greater good.* Not one measure on the page was wicked. That is the point; that is the whole point; wickedness would have saved me and it was nowhere on the page. It was a list of good deeds that all required one instrument, and the instrument was me, enlarged. Power does not arrive asking for cruelty. It arrives asking for efficiency, and it finds you in your own handwriting.

I burned the list in the candle I had written it by. You will notice that when I am frightened of a paper, I burn it; my biography could be written in my fireplaces. Then, on a fresh sheet, I wrote to the Ministry six lines of gratitude and refusal, pleading my unfitness for high office, and posted it before I slept, and the magnificent owl carried it away into the dark.

They concluded, of course, that I was modest.

That was the version that got about, and it has been about ever since: Dumbledore, the great refuser, too gentle for politics, too wise to want power — as if wanting power were a childhood illness that clever men grow out of. Let this page correct the record. Other men want power and should not have it. I wanted it — wanted it the way a reformed drunkard wants the bottle in the cupboard he cannot stop pricing — and I knew, from evidence, from a floor with a dead girl on it, exactly what I am when I hold it. I am not humble. I am correctly labeled. These are different things, and the difference cost me one afternoon and a candle to learn, and the world has admired me for it for a century under the wrong name.

They asked again, years later, when the next crisis wanted a name to hide behind. That time they sent a delegation instead of an owl: three officials in my office, and one of them, cleverer than his colleagues, tried the opposite of flattery and told me bluntly that my refusal would cost lives. He believed it. He may even have been right. I said no in under a minute, before any pause could open, and he saw something in my face as I said it that made him stop arguing, and I never asked what he saw. They asked once more after that — three times in all, each asking shorter than the last, because by then my refusals had entered my legend and the ceremony was half complete before the letter was sealed.

The later refusals took me minutes, and not because refusing had become easy. Because I no longer trusted myself with the pause. I never again allowed myself the clean sheet of parchment. A man who has once written *What I would do* knows better than to sit down with that particular blank page twice.

Through all those decades there was Doge.

We dined — in Hogsmeade, or in London when the Wizengamot sat, or latterly in my office, two old men and a bottle of oak-matured mead. He talked about his travels, his committee work, his bad leg. I talked about the school. He watched every public turn of my career with an approval so complete it should have been ridiculous, and somehow never was. And in more than a hundred years of friendship, this man — who had stood beside me at the grave, who had seen my brother's fist arrive and my hand come up to stop him from intervening, who knew, therefore, that there was a story, and knew that he stood next to it every time we met — in a hundred years he asked me nothing. Not one question. Not once, gently, late in the evening, over the last of the mead: *Albus, what happened that summer?*

Clever men could not have resisted. I could not have; curiosity is the vice I have never even tried to give up. But Doge understood, in that wordless way of his, that a door a friend keeps closed is closed for a reason. It is the great tact of unclever men, and I have eaten dinner inside its shelter all these years, and it is worth more than any conversation I have ever had with a genius.

Once — once in a hundred years — he came close. It was late, the fire was low, and he said, into his glass, "You would have been a great Minister, Albus." And I said, "No, Elphias. I would have been a remarkable one." He laughed, because he took it for modesty making a joke. It was the truest sentence I ever told him, and it is the nearest we ever came. When I am gone he will write something loyal and wrong about me, and I find, sitting here, that I would not have one word of it corrected.

You may ask what checking the knots looks like, for a leash of this kind. It looks like nothing at all. It looks like a man declining a committee chairmanship with thanks; like a headmaster who could end a Ministry argument with one public sentence and writes a private note instead; like letting fools hold high office and confining oneself to catching what they drop. Every year there is an hour — a crisis, a headline, a frightened delegation in my office — when the old voice observes, quietly and reasonably, that everything would go better if I simply took matters in hand. The voice has never once been wrong about the *better*, in the short term. That is precisely what makes it the old voice. Saying no to it is not a decision I made once, long ago, at a desk. It is a decision I make the way one shaves: daily, briefly, without ceremony.

So the history books have it that Albus Dumbledore, from modesty, three times declined to rule, and remained a teacher, and the world has agreed to find this beautiful — an eccentricity, like the sherbet lemons, a wise man's charming refusal of the obvious. Let them. But you, reader, have come this far with me, so you may as well have it straight.

It was not modesty. It was not wisdom, either — wisdom would not have needed the candle. It was a leash. I made it myself, alone, at a desk, on the night I read my own list back and heard whose voice it was written in. I made it myself, and I have checked the knots every morning of my life.

## 22. News from Europe

In the spring of 1917 a pamphlet reached my desk — the first of them. Bathilda sent it. She folded it inside one of her long, warm letters, between a recipe and some Ministry gossip, with a note in the margin: "Thought this might interest you, Albus. They say these are everywhere on the continent now."

It was four pages, cheaply printed, the ink already turning grey. People were passing it from hand to hand, she said, in Vienna and Prague and a hundred villages whose names I would never learn.

I read it standing in the staffroom, with a pile of essays under my arm. I read it twice. Then I sat down, because my legs asked me to.

It spoke of the Muggle war. This was 1917. The Muggle war was in its third year then, and even we, behind our curtain of secrecy, knew what it was. The trenches. The gas. Boys of eighteen drowning in mud, a hundred thousand at a time, for reasons that nobody on either side could clearly explain.

Look at them, said the pamphlet. Look what they do to themselves when no one wiser holds their hand. And we hide. We obey a Statute written by frightened old men. We watch children die in the mud, and we call our hiding a virtue. We could end this. We have the power to end this. What do you call people who can stop a horror and choose to look away?

It was well written. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I have left. It was clear and warm and reasonable, and it moved from pity to duty, and from duty to action, so smoothly that you did not feel the steps under your feet. I knew the style the way you know a voice in the dark.

And at the bottom of the last page, in small type, like a signature: FOR THE GREATER GOOD.

I had seen those words in one place before. In my own handwriting. In a letter composed at three in the morning, in a summer garden, eighteen years earlier, by a boy who believed he was writing about love.

Now they stood in a stranger's typeface. Strangers were reading them by candlelight in countries I had never visited. And some of those strangers, folding the cheap paper carefully to pass it on, were nodding.

There is a special horror in meeting your own argument in the mouth of the world. You cannot call it stupid, because you built it, and you did not build stupid things. You cannot call it evil, or not quickly, because you remember the evening it was born, and there was no evil in that garden that you were willing to see. You can only sit very still in a staffroom, holding essays about the transfiguration of teacups, while the past lays its hand on the back of your neck.

I told no one. Of course I told no one. Silence was already my oldest habit, and I wore it like a warm coat.

The movement grew slowly, and then it grew fast. His followers called themselves the faithful. That word was chosen with care — he chose all his words with care, and the ones he did not choose, he had borrowed. Not an army, not at first. He was far too clever for armies at the beginning. A family, they said. A light. A future.

He gathered the hurt first. Wizards who had lost someone to Muggle fear, in the old bad centuries or in some private disaster like mine. He gave their pain a shape and a direction, and they were grateful, because pain without direction is the heaviest kind. I understood the trick precisely. I had watched him learn it, one summer, on my own sister's story, and I had been grateful too.

Then he gathered the proud, who needed no reason beyond a promise that they mattered. And then he gathered the frightened, who will always join whoever shouts last.

The small news items began in the twenties. A Hungarian wizard wrote sharply against him in a journal, and then wrote nothing ever again. A witch in the German Ministry spoke his name in an open session, without respect. She did not come home from her work one evening in March. The newspapers used the word disappearance. It is a curtain of a word. It is hung up so that we do not have to look at what stands behind it.

Every Muggle horror of those years became his evidence. That was the true genius of it, and I say genius exactly. The war ended and left Europe grey and hungry, and he pointed at the hunger. Look, he said. This is what their freedom builds. We let this happen — we, who could have governed them gently, as a parent governs a child. Every argument I had polished in Bathilda's garden came back to me improved, sharpened, and armed. I had written the music. He had built the orchestra.

Then the letters began arriving. To me.

The first came in about 1926, from a wizard I had known by letter since the Cairo conference, a decent, careful man who studied the properties of dragon blood. His handwriting shook a little. You knew him, he wrote. They say you knew him when you were both young. You are perhaps the only man alive he might still hear.

I sat with that letter for a long time, wondering one thing only: how did they know?

The answer, when I found it, was almost funny. Bathilda. Dear, kind, talkative Bathilda, who had never in her life kept an interesting fact to herself for longer than a week. Over the years, over a thousand cups of tea with visiting scholars, she had mentioned it — innocently, proudly, the way one mentions a family photograph. My great-nephew, you know. Yes, that Gellert. He stayed with me one summer, the same summer young Dumbledore lived next door. Two of the cleverest boys in Europe, and thick as thieves, the pair of them. Inseparable.

She meant no harm at all. Secrets leak through kindness as easily as through betrayal. More easily, in truth, because kindness is never on its guard.

More letters came after that first one, year upon year. From Ministries. From widows. From a schoolmaster in Bavaria who had lost four students to the faithful and wrote to me in Latin, because grief had made him formal. You are the only one he might listen to, they said. Or, later, when listening was over: you are the only one who could match him.

I answered every letter. I am a courteous man; it is one of my cheapest qualities. I answered kindly, thoughtfully, at length — and vaguely. I shared their concern; I watched developments closely; I hoped, and I trusted, and I remained, with deep respect, theirs sincerely. I became a master of the sentence that sounds like a promise and weighs nothing at all. I have written many such sentences in my life. If you gathered them together they would make a book far longer than this one, and it would say nothing whatever, beautifully. Not one of my correspondents ever wrote back to call me a coward. They were too polite, or too hopeful, or they simply concluded that great men have great reasons. Great men count on that conclusion. It is one of the ways we stay great.

What did I tell myself? Here is the full list. I have had a lifetime to compile it, and I will strip each item as I set it down.

He is Europe's problem, I said. As if a border were a wall. A border is a curtain, nothing more, and evil does not knock before it enters. I knew this even as I said it, because I had read history — I had lived next door to the woman who wrote it.

He can still be turned, I said. Turned from what? He was not lost on some road; he was walking, with excellent boots, exactly where he had always meant to go. And turned by whom? By the friend whose finest skill had been agreeing with him?

Hogwarts needs me, I said. This one was the most shameful, because it was dressed as love. There were real children in my care, and I really did care for them, and I held them up between myself and Europe like a screen. Children make excellent shields. They are light to carry and no one dares shoot at them.

And beneath all three excuses, in the cellar under the house of my reasons, sat the true reason, in the dark, saying nothing. I will bring it up into the light later in this book. I have promised myself that. Not yet.

Near the end of those years, a report reached my desk through Ministry friends. I remember the evening exactly. It was January; snow was falling past my window without hurry; my instruments ticked their small mechanical comfort around the walls.

The report said that Grindelwald had built a fortress-prison in the mountains, for his enemies — a place to put the people who disagreed with him, now that there were too many to disappear one by one. The report gave its name: Nurmengard. And the report noted, in a dry official voice, one detail about the entrance.

Over the gate, cut deep into the black stone, an inscription: FOR THE GREATER GOOD.

He had carved my words over a prison door. Words I had written to him about mercy, about responsibility, about the beautiful world we would build — words from a love letter, if I am finally honest about what those letters were. Men and women would look up at my sentence, my own three-in-the-morning sentence, as the door closed behind them for the rest of their lives.

My hand lies flat on this page as I write, black and dead, like something left behind by a fire. Some nights it seems to me the most honest part of my body. It, at least, looks like what I have done.

I read the report twice, as I had once read a pamphlet twice. Then I filed it carefully, and went down to dinner, and gave out house points. I did not go to Europe that year, either.

It snowed all that January, I remember. Snow is very good at covering things. It was the only thing in Britain working harder at that than I was.

## 23. The Hog's Head

Half an hour's walk from my office door, down the road into Hogsmeade and along its least respectable street, there is a public house called the Hog's Head. It is dark inside, and the floor is covered with sawdust that remembers better days, and the windows are so dirty that the daylight gives up halfway through them. It smells, powerfully, of goats.

My brother has kept that bar for most of his life.

When Aberforth finished school he did not take a Ministry job, or an apprenticeship, or any of the sensible doors that open for a young wizard. He bought the Hog's Head, and he moved in above it, and he has stood behind its counter ever since, polishing glasses that will never come clean, serving strangers who prefer not to give their names.

People found it strange, I am told, that the great Dumbledore's brother poured drinks in a village bar. People found many things strange that were, in fact, the simplest arrangements in the world. He wanted a small life, with animals at the back door and no conversation he did not choose. He built exactly that. It took him one year. I have never built anything I wanted so cleanly, and I have had a century.

So we have lived, my brother and I, for decades, one village apart. I could see the roofs of Hogsmeade from the castle. On clear mornings, if I stood at a certain window, I could see his chimney smoking.

We did not speak. I do not mean we quarrelled. A quarrel is a living thing; it moves, it feeds, it can be settled. What lay between us did not move at all. Our record, if I have counted honestly, was six years without a single word — six years in which we passed in the village street perhaps a dozen times. He would nod. I would nod. That nod was our entire correspondence, and it said everything either of us was prepared to say.

The silence had begun at the graveside, of course, with his fist, and it settled into its final shape within the year. The house in Godric's Hollow was closed up; there was nothing in it either of us could live with. He finished school because I had made him, took his examinations without distinction and without caring, and vanished into his own life with the efficiency of a man who has already wasted enough time on other people's plans. When I learned, some years later, that he had bought a bar within sight of my windows, I did not know what to make of it, and I do not know today. Perhaps Hogsmeade simply had a bar for sale. Perhaps he wanted to be near the only family he had left, on the strict condition that nearness never once be mentioned. Both of these are exactly like him.

For most of a century, the students of Hogwarts have walked past the Hog's Head on their village weekends, and hardly one in a hundred has known that the sour old barman and their headmaster share a name. We have never corrected anybody. It is the one project, I believe, on which my brother and I have worked together in perfect harmony.

People imagine hatred. They are wrong, and the truth is worse. Hatred still watches you; hatred still cares where you are. What my brother holds is a verdict. It was reached long ago, in a sitting room and then at a graveside, after full consideration of the evidence, and it is correct, and it is final. He is not waiting for my apology. This is the part visitors to my life have never understood. There is nothing an apology would return to him. You cannot apologise a girl back onto her feet.

Above the fireplace in his bar, over the mantel, hangs a portrait of our sister.

I do not know when he had it painted, or by whom, or from what — some photograph of Kendra's, I suppose, saved from the house. I learned of it the way I learn most things about my brother, sideways, from someone else's chatter. A colleague mentioned the picture of a girl at the Hog's Head, and asked, entirely innocent, whether the barman had once had a daughter.

I went down to see it exactly once.

I chose a quiet afternoon in winter. I sat at the end of the counter and ordered a drink I did not want, and Aberforth served it without a word, as if I were any stranger — which was, I believe, precisely the message. And I sat there, under my sister's painted eyes, and I made myself look up.

She is laughing in the portrait. That is the thing I was not ready for. Whoever painted her caught something true: the particular way her laughter arrived all at once, like weather. She hangs there in the smoke and the dark of the least respectable bar in Scotland, laughing, fourteen forever, while her brother stands beneath her and pours drinks for people who are hiding from something. I understood, sitting there, that he had not hung her portrait as a wound or as an accusation. He hung it because he wanted her in the room. It was that simple, and I could not bear it.

I could not finish the glass. I left too much gold on the counter — far too much, a clumsy, ashamed amount, the old mistake in miniature: gold instead of myself — and he swept it into the till without looking at me, and I walked back up to the castle in the snow. Halfway up the hill I stopped and looked back at his lit windows, and behind one of them, I knew, my sister was laughing at the whole room, as she had once laughed at my worst small charms. I did not go in again for years.

Once — once in all those decades — I attempted the conversation.

I prepared for it. There is the confession inside the confession: I prepared. I chose a date, an hour when the bar would be empty. I rehearsed sentences while I marked essays, revising them as I have revised speeches for the Wizengamot. I walked down the hill with my opening line ready, and my fallback line behind it, and something honest and broken in reserve behind that, in case honesty was required.

He was alone, wiping the counter. I sat. I said his name. I said, "I have been thinking a great deal about that summer. There are things I have never said to you, and I should like—"

"You practice being sorry," my brother said, "the way you practiced everything. Top marks, Albus."

And he turned away and took up a glass and went on polishing it, and that was the end of the conversation, because there was nothing whatever to say back. I have faced examiners, ministers, and the finest duellist of the age. I have never in my life been so completely answered.

Because he was right — that is the point. That is why I set his sentence down here in full, the one judgement in my life I accept without a single footnote. My sorrow was excellent. It was studied, structured, examined from every side; it had, so to speak, read all the relevant literature. He saw in four seconds what I have needed a lifetime to see: that its excellence was exactly what was wrong with it. Grief had become one more subject I was determined to be best at. Even at the bottom of my shame, I was reaching for the top of the class.

The goats deserve a paragraph. He keeps them behind the bar in a shed he built himself, and their smell is in the sawdust, in the curtains, in the beer, and their smell, I confess, I find almost unbearable — and entirely right. Warm milk was what our sister needed on her worst nights, warm milk and silly songs, and he knew it at thirteen while I was in Cairo collecting medals. He has spent his whole life on the things I once called small. Animals that trust him. A fire kept in. A room where odd, frightened people can sit and drink and not be asked questions — he is rude to every customer and has never once turned one out into the cold. My brother built himself a life out of everything I called small, and it has held, which is more than may be said for a single one of my grand things.

I will note one more thing, because it belongs to the record of those years. Through all the decades when Europe darkened and the letters begged me to act and the newspapers asked their question, my brother said nothing to me on the subject. Not one word, not one look across the street sharpened into meaning. He, who had more right than any living person to demand that I face Gellert Grindelwald, never demanded it. I used to wonder at that. I no longer wonder. He knew better than the newspapers what stood between me and the continent; he had been in the sitting room. His silence was not mercy and it was not indifference. It was accuracy. Some things cannot be asked of a man by anyone who truly knows why the man has not done them.

I have collected titles the way his goats collect burrs. Grand Sorcerer, Supreme Mugwump — I could fill the page. He has a bar, a shed, and a picture of a laughing girl. There has never been any real question, between the two of us, about which one is the rich man.

He is alive as I write this, one village away. His chimney will be smoking now; it is past midnight, but he keeps late hours. He does not know about my hand. He does not know that his brother has a year, more or less, and is spending it filling a notebook with the truth. I have not told him, and I sit here explaining to a page what I cannot walk half an hour to say — and there, you see. A hundred years, and I have learned nothing at all.

## 24. The Boy in the Orphanage

In the summer of 1938 I put on my best attempt at a Muggle suit and took a train to London. I have been assured, by people who love me, that my idea of a Muggle suit convinces nobody. It was plum-coloured. I thought it very quiet.

Every year a few of our letters go out to children raised entirely among Muggles, children who have no idea what they are. Somebody from the school must visit each one, sit down with the family, and explain the unexplainable. That year, one such letter was addressed to an orphanage in London, to a boy named Tom Riddle. The visit fell to me. I have wondered since what would have grown differently if it had fallen to anyone else, and I have concluded: nothing. But I have wondered.

Muggle London that summer was a city pretending very hard to be calm. There were newspapers on every corner, and men reading them in that particular way — standing quite still, too long, in the middle of moving crowds. I understood them completely. Their continent and mine were sickening from the same weather, and only one of us knew it was the same, and I walked through their streets in my ridiculous suit carrying my half of the secret.

The orphanage was a grey, square building, scrubbed clean and poor to the bone, smelling of soap and boiled cabbage. It was decent. I want that recorded: nobody was cruel to him there. The matron who received me was a tired woman with quick eyes, and after a glass of something to steady her nerves she talked more freely than she had perhaps intended.

She was worried about the boy. No — I will be precise, because precision matters here. She was frightened of the boy, and ashamed of being frightened, and glad almost to tears that somebody had come to take an interest. Odd things happened around him, she told me. Things she could not prove and could not forget. Another child's pet had died, strangely. Two children had gone on a summer outing with him and come back changed — silent, the both of them, and no punishment or kindness ever got out of either one what had happened. "He was never anywhere near the trouble," she said. "That's the thing, sir. He's never anywhere near it." She had given me, without knowing it, as exact a description of a certain kind of wizard as I have ever heard.

He was upstairs, in a small clean room, sitting on a grey blanket. Eleven years old. Dark-haired, pale, tall for his age, and still — still in a way children never are. Children move; it is practically their definition. This one sat like a closed box and watched me come in, and his eyes measured me before I had said a word.

He took me for a doctor at first — the sort of doctor that takes children away — and for a moment I saw the fear under everything, the bottom card of the whole deck. Then the fear turned to anger, and the anger, when I explained what I was and what he was, turned into something I can only call arrival. He was not surprised to learn that magic existed. He told me he had always known he was different. He said the word special. He had known, he said, that there had to be a reason.

Then, quite calmly, he showed me his cards. He listed the things he could do, in a level voice, the way another boy might recite his examination results. He could move objects without touching them. He could make animals do what he wanted, without training them. He could hurt people who were unkind to him — he watched my face as he said that one, with great attention, to see what I would do with it. He was not confessing. He was demonstrating. He wanted to know the exchange rate of his powers in this new world I had brought him.

And when he understood that I had something he wanted — a school, a place, a category to belong to — the charm switched on. I use those words deliberately: it switched on, like a lamp, all at once and from nowhere, and it was good charm, warm and modest and grave. It had been practiced on the matron and the visitors and everyone else worth practicing on. The room got friendlier without anything in it moving. I have only ever met two people who could do that at will. He was the second.

There was a cupboard in the corner, and I asked him to open it. Inside was a small box, and inside the box a handful of little things, worthless things — a thimble, a mouth-organ, small stolen things — one from this child, one from that. Trophies. Not stolen for wanting; stolen for winning. Each one said: I did this and was never caught. I told him to return every item to its owner, with apologies. He said, "Yes, sir," at once — too much at once — and looked at me with those level eyes, and filed me, I believe, in the small drawer he kept for future enemies.

Before I left I told him plainly that the school had rules, that the rules would apply to him, and that in our world thieves and bullies answer for what they do. He listened with an attention no eleven-year-old gives to rules — he was not receiving guidance, he was mapping walls. Then he asked his questions, and every one was about power: what could be learned, how fast, what was forbidden and what merely difficult. Not one question about whether there were others like him. I noticed that, and understood it fully only years later. He did not want there to be others like him.

I must now tell you what was happening inside me during that half hour, because it is the whole reason this chapter is in the book.

I was back in a summer garden.

The weather in that boy's eyes — I knew it. The hunger that has already counted the cost. The certainty, bone-deep and unexamined, that power is the answer to every question and that the price is always payable, since others will be paying it. The charm worn like a coat over the cold. I had met all of it before, aged seventeen, over a garden wall in Godric's Hollow, and I had loved it with my whole heart. And I had met it, if we are being thorough — and in this book we are being thorough — once more after that, in a mirror.

The difference was the speed. Gellert took me an entire summer and a death to see; I was inside the storm, and from inside, a storm looks like weather at last, like the sky finally doing something interesting. Tom Riddle took me one afternoon. It is easy to recognise a storm when you have already drowned in one.

They were not the same, the two of them; let me be fair to the differences. Gellert was warm — theatrically, genuinely, both at once; he loved an audience, loved argument, loved, I still believe, at least the reflection of me that he found useful. The boy in the orphanage was cold and complete. He wanted no friend, no partner, no mirror. He was already a country of one, with borders and an army. But the core was the same metal in both. Only the temperature was different.

So — what did I do, with all this hard-won recognition? Here is the accounting.

I gave him his place at the school, because it was his by right and by law, and because a boy like that untaught was more dangerous than a boy like that watched. I watched him, all seven years — courteously, at a distance, missing, I would later learn, a very great deal. He was, naturally, a model student. Prefect, Head Boy, prizes; the teachers competed to praise him; the pattern of his school career was so nearly a copy of mine that reading his reports sometimes gave me a feeling like touching cold glass. I warned no one loudly; loud warnings would have taught him only to hide better, and he needed no teaching. And I did not do the one thing everyone at Hogwarts expected the friendly old Transfiguration master to do with the most brilliant student in a century. I did not open my mind, or my library, or my heart. I kept my gifts in my pocket, and I watched him notice that I kept them there. He was too clever not to notice. Of all the adults in his life, the one whose admiration he could not switch on was the one who had seen the trick performed before.

Gellert had my whole mind by the second afternoon. Tom Riddle got my caution from the first hour. He never forgave me for it, I think — and he was right to be surprised. It was out of character. It was, if I am honest, the first fully intelligent thing I ever did about a brilliant boy, and I was fifty-seven years old, and it had cost a war and my sister to learn.

And yet — here is the coda, and I owe it to honesty even though it undoes some of the credit I have just given myself. Caution is also a way of failing a child. I recognised him instantly, and so I never once tried to reach him. Was there a door into that boy? If somebody had loved him at three, at five — plainly, practically, the warm-milk kind of love my brother understood and I did not — would the metal have cooled? I do not know. I want to write no, for comfort. I want to write yes, for punishment. The truth is: I do not know, and no one will ever know, because nobody tried, and by eleven, when I met him, I judged the question already closed. Perhaps I judged it closed because trying would have been expensive. My judgements have a history of arriving exactly where my convenience was standing.

I rode the train back north through the summer evening, and somewhere past York I understood the shape of my life from that day forward. It has held two clocks ever since. One ticked in Europe, where the continent was going dark under a man I had helped to arm with arguments — under my oldest mistake. The other had just begun ticking, very quietly, in a scrubbed grey building in London, under my watchfulness. I sat between the two of them with my tea going cold, the cleverest man on the train, listening.

## 25. What the Mirror Knew

The stories arrived before the war did. They came with the refugees.

By the late thirties, wizarding families from the continent were reaching Britain in twos and threes — quiet, well-dressed, over-polite people carrying too little luggage. Some had British cousins. Some had nothing but an address on a scrap of paper. A family from Austria ate supper in our Great Hall one October evening while the Ministry's parchment-work found them somewhere to live; the father praised everything twice, and his hands shook when the doors banged. I remember their daughter best. She flinched every time the owls came in. In her town, she explained to a Gryffindor girl who asked — politely, factually, passing the salt — the owls had come first, and then the disappearances. She was nine.

The two wars fed each other by then; that is what people who were not alive find hardest to understand. The Muggles were arming and marching toward their catastrophe, and through the same streets, invisibly, ran ours. Grindelwald had wanted it so — chaos was his weather, and their madness was his best pamphlet. Every Muggle drum that beat made his old argument for him: see what they are. Two fires in one forest, sharing wind.

Then came the morning I found a name I knew on a list.

The Prophet had begun printing them by then — small type, back pages, wizards missing abroad. Missing. It is a gentler word than the truth, and its gentleness is the cruel part: it leaves a door open for hope to stand in, out of the rain, for years. I read those lists the way one presses on a wound, and one morning, between two names I did not know, there he was. A former student of mine. He had been afraid of my classroom for the whole of his first year; vanishing spells defeated him utterly, and he laughed at himself, which is rarer than talent. He had gone to Vienna to study healing. Years before, he had sent me a jar of honey from his family's bees, with a note in which he had misspelled "transfiguration" — after seven years of my teaching — and I had kept the note, because it made me laugh, and I have it still. He was on the list from Vienna under the word missing.

I put the paper down and looked at my hands. You will hear about my hands again before this chapter ends.

A list of strangers is only numbers; one known name is a death. That is not wisdom, it is only how human beings are built, and I record it because it matters to what follows: for twenty years I had read the news from Europe as history, terrible and large and abstract, and on that morning it became personal, and I discovered that even this was not enough to move me. I had believed, in some unexamined cellar of myself, that when the war finally touched someone I had taught, the touch would break whatever held me in Britain. The war touched him. Nothing broke. I went to my morning class and taught it well. A man can learn a great deal about the true size of his own obstacle by watching what fails to knock it over.

The pressure on me was public now; that was the new thing. For years the letters had come privately, and privately a man can manage his shame — file it, answer it kindly, feed it excuses. Now the newspapers asked in headlines. The Prophet ran my photograph — they always chose one in which I looked wise, which I found harder to bear than mockery — above the question the whole country was asking at its breakfast tables: what is the greatest wizard of the age waiting for?

A fair question. I never wrote to the editor. What could I have sent? The true answer was four words long, and I could not have written those four words then for any prize the world offers. I cannot quite write them yet, and I have been circling them for three chapters.

The Howler came in the spring of 1939, at breakfast, in front of the whole school.

I knew what it was the moment the owl dropped it — the red envelope was already smoking at the corners. A colleague reached for a wand to silence it; such things were not to be endured at the staff table. I stopped the wand with a look. Then I stood up, because a man should stand for what was coming, and I let it speak.

It was a widow. Her husband had gone to the continent two years before, with other volunteers, to do what wizards could — and I alone, said the voice, cracking and rising through that enormous silence while four hundred children stared, I alone had the power to have gone where he went and to have ended what took him, and I had stayed, and taught spells to children in a warm castle, and eaten pudding. She did not curse me. I would have preferred curses; I know what to do with hatred. She only asked me the question from the headlines, over and over, four times, five, each time with his name in it, until her voice failed. Then the envelope burst into ash, as such letters do, and the flakes of it came down over my plate like black snow.

I thanked the air where it had been. I sat down. I buttered a piece of toast with perfect care, and did not eat it, and all around me the hall began, slowly and embarrassedly, to breathe again.

And I still did not go.

I want the reader to sit with that sentence as long as I have had to. A woman stood in my Great Hall, in the form of a burning envelope, and asked me why her husband was dead, and the question had a correct answer, and the answer was standing at the staff table buttering toast. Everyone present believed they had watched Dumbledore endure something that morning. They had watched him be paid. It was the first bill that had ever been presented to me in public, and some part of me — I noticed it with horror even then — was relieved that the price was finally being said aloud.

Here — now — the page and I must have it out, because I have reached the place where every account I have ever given of those years runs aground.

I could write here that Hogwarts could not spare me; that a school full of children in wartime needed its Transfiguration master more than Europe needed one more duellist. I have said it to ministers, and it is even partly true, and — no. I have already shown you, two chapters ago, what children were to me in that argument. Shields. Cross it out.

I could write that the moment was not yet ripe. That going too soon, alone, would have meant a splendid useless death, and the cause needed me whole. Strategy, timing, patience — I can still make that paragraph sing, sixty years on. I know its music too well to trust it. Cross it out.

I could write that I was afraid of his power — the Elder Wand, the armies, the terrible momentum of him. Many believed it. It flattered him and excused me, a rare bargain. But it is false, and it is the one falsehood I refuse to leave standing anywhere in this book: I was never afraid of Gellert Grindelwald's power. Between the two of us, and I say this without a gram of pleasure, I was always slightly the better wizard. Cross it out.

There was another reason. There was only ever one reason.

I know its name. I have known its name since before Nurmengard had a gate to carve. It is four words long, it weighs more than the castle I live in, and I am not going to write it on this page. Not yet — not from cowardice this time, or not only; a confession also has its craft, and some sentences kill the book they are dropped into too early. I will say it. I have promised the page, and the page has a way of collecting. But even now, even here, an old man alone at night with a dead hand and one year left and nothing whatever remaining to lose — even now I find I need a running start.

The Muggle war began that September. Their wireless spoke of nothing else; even our instruments seemed to tick more carefully, the way people lower their voices in a sickroom. In the halls my students' faces turned older by years in a season — the Muggle-born children especially, who had two worlds to lose and knew it.

On the third of September, in the afternoon, I taught first-years. The lesson was the one I have taught every autumn since 1899: a match into a needle. It is a good first lesson. It is small, it is honest, and it teaches the only spell that matters, which is that things can change.

A girl in the second row got it before anyone — the wood going suddenly silver, the point, the little gasp. She held it up in the light, shining, and looked from it to me with her whole face asking: did you see? I saw. I said what a teacher says. And as I bent over her desk I noticed, with the detachment of a man reading someone else's diagnosis, that my hands were perfectly steady. The steadiest hands in the school. The steadiest hands, very possibly, in Europe — where cities I had walked in as a young man were being marked on maps for burning.

They did not shake at all, those hands, and I looked at them with something I can only, at this distance, call contempt. It seemed to me that decent hands would tremble. Mine had learned better. They had been not-going for twenty years by then; they had the calm of long practice; steadiness, too, can be a habit of guilt — the body keeping the secret the mouth will not say.

I straightened up. I awarded ten points to a delighted child in her first week of magic. Outside the windows the first cold was coming down off the mountains, and in my office, far above us, the instruments went on ticking, measuring — patiently, all through that terrible autumn — exactly how long I was taking.

## Part Four: The Duel

*Every duel begins long before the first spell. Mine began in a summer garden in 1899, and it waited for me, patiently, for forty-six years. This is the part of the story where the world paid for my waiting. It is also the part where, at last, I walked toward the one door I feared.*

## 26. Names

The war reached me first as paper. Not fire, not screams, not broken walls — paper. Every morning the *Daily Prophet* lay folded beside my breakfast, and every morning it carried names.

In the beginning the lists were short. Foreign names, far places, small print. The paper put them at the bottom of page four, between the Quidditch results and an advertisement for self-stirring cauldrons. *Missing*, the lists said at first. Then *taken*. Then, when even the papers grew tired of being gentle, simply: *dead*.

I read every name. I read them slowly, from the first to the last, every morning, without exception. It was the least I could do — and I want you to look closely at the shape of that sentence. The least I could do. For twenty years, the least was almost all I did.

By 1940 the lists had moved to the front page, and the print was no longer small. By 1941 there were British names on them: wizards with family on the continent, traders, travelers, one former student of mine, a witch who had married a French healer and had written to me, two years earlier, a happy letter about her work. I still have the letter. I did not keep the newspaper.

That year I bought a map of Europe. A Muggle map, large and beautiful, the kind schoolboys dream over. I kept it in a locked drawer of my desk, and in the evenings I would take it out and mark it. A dark line here, where his people now controlled the roads. A circle there, where a wizarding village had gone silent. Shading, spreading slowly westward, like ink dropped into water.

You will notice what I was doing. I had not held a map since 1899, when Elphias Doge and I planned a grand tour across those same countries — Greece, Egypt, the alchemists, the dragons. The old hunger for planning had woken up in me and found new work. Once I had drawn routes toward everything I wanted. Now I drew borders around everything I had allowed to happen. The hand enjoys a map. I am ashamed to say that even that map, my hand enjoyed.

Let me tell you how he worked, because it matters, and because the world has largely forgotten it. It remembers him as a monster, and monsters are simple. He was never simple.

He did not begin with terror. He began with arguments — beautiful ones, the pamphlets I have already described. They spread now year by year into towns whose names I would never learn, better printed, warmer in tone, my four words standing quietly at the bottom of every last page.

The Muggle war served him as a stage serves an actor. Their cities burned, their young men died in numbers no wizard could imagine, and every photograph became a page in his argument. *This is what they do when we leave them to themselves*, the pamphlets said. *We are not their enemies. We are their unclaimed responsibility.* I want to record, because the record should be honest, that this was a clever argument, and that many kind people believed it, and that unkind arguments do not conquer continents — kind-sounding ones do.

I knew those arguments the way you know your own handwriting. Some of them, in their first young form, had been mine. I had built them at seventeen, in Bathilda Bagshot's garden, and polished them at three in the morning, and given them to him freely, gladly, with love. Now they came back to me printed in a stranger's typeface, grown up, armed, standing on a continent. Reading them was like hearing my own voice shouting in another man's mouth.

His faithful — I have told you about that word. He arrived in a district with words before wands. Towns welcomed him — I must be honest, many truly welcomed him — because he offered pride to people who had spent centuries hiding. Those who argued were visited politely. Those who kept arguing were visited again, less politely. Those who organized against him were, in the quiet language of the lists, *taken for questioning*. Seduction first; fear only for those the seduction failed. It is a worse method than terror, not a better one. Terror locks the door. Seduction persuades you to open it yourself.

In Britain, meanwhile, we drank our tea. "He will not cross the Channel," people said at dinner parties, as if ideas could drown. "It is a European matter," said the Ministry, for years, as if a border were anything more than a curtain. I have already stripped those excuses once in this book, when they were mine. It was strange and unpleasant to hear a whole country repeating them with the same comfortable face I had once worn at my own mirror.

Then the Ministry stopped repeating them, because the lists grew too long, and turned — of all directions — to me.

I must ask you to appreciate the irony, because I could not enjoy it at the time. The institution whose highest chair I had refused three times now wrote to me monthly. Would Professor Dumbledore lead a delegation to the European ministries? I declined, politely. Would Professor Dumbledore consider — this letter was marked secret, and its handwriting shook a little — leading a strike against one of his strongholds? I declined, politely. I was extremely good at declining politely. I had been practicing on smaller invitations for twenty years, and this, you might say, was my Nurmengard of refusals: the great work my whole practice had prepared.

My reasons, in those replies, were careful and wise and entirely believable. I gave a different one each time, which should have told them something. A man with one true reason gives it twice.

Only one person ever asked me the real question. Elphias came to dinner at the castle two or three times a year all through the war, and we talked of dragons and old friends and his terrible packing lists, and never once of the map in my drawer. But one evening — it was 1942, I think, the candles low — he set down his glass and looked at me and said, quite gently: "Is it because you knew him?"

I said nothing. I remember looking at my own hands.

And Elphias — this is the whole of Elphias Doge, and why I have loved him for a century — nodded slowly, as though I had answered, and refilled my glass, and began a long story about a Romanian dragon-keeper we had both met in 1902. The great tact of unclever men. I have used that phrase before in these pages, and I use it again without apology. Some things deserve saying twice.

And the private letters kept coming too, as they had for twenty years — from Vienna, from Prague, from old colleagues and strangers, in shaking hands and careful ones. *You knew him once*, they wrote — how they knew, I have already told you: Bathilda, tea, kindness. *You are the only one he might listen to. You are the only one who could match him.* I answered every letter. I answered kindly, and at length, and never once with yes. There is a special corner of my shame reserved for how well written those refusals were. A man should not be eloquent while declining to save people.

So the years passed. I taught. That must be said plainly, because it is absurd: through all of it, I taught. While Europe went dark region by region, I stood in a warm classroom and showed eleven-year-olds how to turn a match into a needle. Some mornings a student would be missing from my class, and I would learn that a brother or a cousin had been on the morning's list, and I would set the class an easy exercise and walk between the desks and put my hand briefly on a shoulder, and feel like a forger signing someone else's name. Safety, when you have not earned it, sits on the skin like borrowed clothes.

And then, one morning in the autumn of 1943, the lists reached me. Not Britain. Me.

Halfway down the front page, among forty names, I found one I knew: an Egyptian alchemist called Rashid, whom I knew by letter from the Cairo conference days, when I was sixteen and he was thirty, and who had later gone to teach in Paris. He had laughed for a full minute at my schoolboy Latin, and then corrected it so kindly that I did not mind. Every New Year for a decade he had sent me candied dates and one impossible question about the nature of metals. He had six grandchildren, and claimed in every letter that each of them, individually, was his favorite.

*Taken for questioning*, the list said. *Whereabouts unknown.* By 1943, everyone knew what those words were for. They were a soft cloth laid over the word everyone understood.

I put the paper down. I did not finish my tea. I went up to my office, past the ticking instruments, and I opened the locked drawer. But this time I did not take out the map of Europe with its spreading ink. I took out a second map, a smaller one, which I had owned for a year and never yet unfolded: a map of the eastern mountains where he was believed to be.

I unfolded it, and smoothed it, and weighted its corners with an inkwell and three books, the way a serious man prepares a serious document. I found the region. I found, or guessed, the valley. I sat looking at it for a long time — an old man, the papers' "greatest wizard of the age," staring at a fold of printed mountains as though they might speak first.

I did not go. Not yet; there were two years of cowardice still in me, and I will account for them. But that evening, for the first time in forty-four years, I let myself look at the road.

## 27. Letters I Did Not Send

There is a drawer in my desk — a different drawer, deeper than the one with the maps — that I opened perhaps ten times in forty years, and always at night, and always alone. It held no dark instrument, no weapon, no secret of state. It held drafts.

Letters to Gellert Grindelwald: begun and abandoned, finished and never sent, folded, unfolded, crumpled and smoothed flat again. The oldest was written in 1922. The newest, before the end, in 1944. How many were there altogether? I never counted them. Counting them would have meant admitting that they were a collection, and a man who keeps a collection is keeping something alive.

He wrote to me once. One letter, in September 1899, a few weeks after the funeral, with a foreign stamp. I burned it unopened; I have told you this already, and told you why. That was the whole of our correspondence for four and a half decades. Two men who had once worn out a patient owl's wings in a single August night, letters crossing in the dark between two attic windows, faster than either could answer — and then nothing. The arithmetic of ruin is very simple. Everything, then nothing.

But I learned something in those years that I offer you now, free of charge, from a man who tested it thoroughly. Silence is not the absence of letters. Silence is the writing of letters that go nowhere.

The writing had its ritual, and rituals should be confessed along with their sins. It always happened at night, always after some fresh news of him — a pamphlet forwarded by a worried colleague, a report, a name I recognized on a list. I would tell myself I was going to my desk to prepare lessons. I would prepare them, too; I have never in my life skipped the honest work that stood between me and the dishonest kind. And then, lessons done, castle quiet, I would take a clean sheet, and write his name at the top of it, and sit looking at the name as though it might finish the letter itself. Sometimes it did. Those were the bad nights.

Here is the first draft I remember, from 1922, when his name was newly loud in Europe and mine was newly asked for. I reproduce it as exactly as memory allows:

> To Gellert Grindelwald, at present in central Europe.
> It has come to the attention of the writer that your movement has adopted methods which even your own arguments can no longer cover.
> The writer proposes terms. You will dissolve your organization. You will release those held against their will. You will present yourself to the International Confederation for judgment.
> In exchange, the writer undertakes to speak on your behalf concerning —

It stops there. Look at it. *The writer.* I could not even hold the word *I* steady in his direction; I hid inside cold official language like a clerk behind a counter. I wrote coldness the way a mason builds a wall — to have something standing between us — and when I read it back, the coldness rang so false that I was embarrassed alone in an empty room. Into the drawer it went. Terms of surrender, unsigned, from a man who would not for another twenty years so much as cross the Channel.

The second kind of draft was worse, because it was better. This one is from the middle thirties:

> You say that wizards hide like criminals although we have committed no crime. It was always a good sentence. I should know; I helped you polish it. But follow it honestly, the way you taught me to follow an idea past the point of comfort. If we have committed no crime, what do you call the village of Grindelwald's faithful at Radomir? What do you call the lists? You will answer that force is the tool of the transition, temporary, regrettable, necessary. You always had a fine wardrobe for that word. But transition to what? A world ruled by men who found force convenient will be ruled by force forever, because convenience does not resign. Consider, since you like foundations, what your new world stands on: it stands on —

Abandoned mid-sentence. Not because I had lost the argument — I was winning it, and that was precisely the trouble. Twenty minutes in, I noticed my own state as one notices a fever: my heart was quick, my quill was flying, I had made three points he would be forced to admire and I was already savoring his counter-attack. I was not writing to an enemy of civilization. I was seventeen, and it was August, and the owl was waiting on the sill, and the finest mind of my life was across the lane composing its answer. I caught myself *enjoying* it — the old duet, the old fire — and the enjoyment frightened me more than his army ever did. A man may fight his enemy. What does he do when his enemy is also the only conversation he has ever wanted?

Into the drawer.

The third draft has no date, because I wrote it many times across many years, and it never changed, because it could not be improved. It was two lines long:

> Did you see which curse it was?
> One line. You owe me one line.

That was my masterpiece. Forty years of letters to statesmen and scholars, and the truest thing I ever addressed to anyone was eleven words to a man I was refusing to meet. I sat with that draft entire nights, candle down to the pool, the envelope actually made out once — I got as far as the second line of the address. It stayed unsent, every time, for a reason I will give you fully in the next chapter, because it deserves a chapter and I have promised myself no more flinching. But you may already have guessed. You have met me by now. You know what I do with doors: I study them, I admire their construction, and I do not open them.

And then there is the fourth draft, and I would rather show you any other page of my life than this one, which is exactly why it must go in. It was written late — 1943, perhaps 1944, the war at its worst, the lists at their longest. It begins like this:

> Gellert —
> It is late here, and the castle is asleep, and I find that tonight I do not want to argue with you. I read that the mountains where you have made your fortress are cold. You were always ridiculous about the cold; you wore a scarf in July and claimed it was for style. I remember —

There is no more. There is no third line in existence. I do not know what I remembered, because I did not permit myself to write it down; I sat with the quill lifted and understood that the sentence, wherever it went, could not be allowed to arrive, and I put the quill down with the care of a man setting down a blade. A letter that begins too warmly to a man burying a continent — there is a word for what such a letter is, and the word is *unforgivable*, and I mean the letter and I mean myself. Of all the drafts, that is the one I am most ashamed of. It is also, and I say this with my eyes open, the only one I regret burning.

I should record, for completeness, that the drawer also held things too small to call drafts. Single lines. Openings with nothing after them. One sheet, I remember, carried only the words *Dear Gellert* crossed out, then *Grindelwald* crossed out, then nothing — a man spending an entire evening on the problem of what to call the person he had once needed no name for at all, because who else would the letter be to? The first line — the name you choose to write — is the deepest part of a letter. Everything after it is merely information.

Why did none of them go? I have given you a different small reason for each, and each is true, and all of them together are the polite cover over the single real one. Every letter is a door. That is its entire nature: paper pretending to be wood, a rectangle you knock on. And behind every door I could construct — the cold one, the clever one, the eleven-word one, the warm one — behind every single door, without exception, was the same room. The sitting room. The overturned chairs, the scorched wall, my brother on the floor, and a girl in a nightdress who came down because of the noise. All my letters, whatever their first line, ended in that room. So they stayed in a drawer, which is a door that opens only inward.

And why keep them? I told myself for decades that I kept them as evidence of restraint — see how often I almost, see how firmly I refrained. That was the label on the drawer. The truth is simpler and smaller. One keeps drafts the way one keeps photographs. I had no photograph of him. I had his side of a conversation that never happened, in my own handwriting, which — he said it once, in the garden, laughing — was the better handwriting of the two.

In the last week of 1944, on a night I will come to soon, I took the whole bundle out of the drawer and read every page once, in order, oldest first, standing up, like a sentry. Then I put them in the office grate and burned them.

You have watched me burn things throughout this book — his one letter in 1899, my list of wise measures in the Minister chapter, and now this. Fire has done a great deal of my filing. It is the one clerk I have always trusted, because it keeps nothing and tells nothing and does not offer its opinion.

The drafts went up quickly, being dry and old and, like their author, mostly air by then. Twenty-two years of unsaid things made perhaps four minutes of ordinary yellow flame, which felt, on balance, like a fair exchange rate. I stirred the ash flat. Fawkes watched from his perch and, for once, I was glad that phoenixes cannot read.

The next thing I wrote to him was not a letter.

## 28. The True Reason

Several chapters ago, standing at the edge of this page, I admitted that I was not yet able to write it, and asked for a running start. I have had my running start. It was three chapters long and forty-six years wide, and there is no more road left in front of me. So.

Let me first clear the ground of the false reasons, one final time, so that nothing is left standing here but the truth.

Was I afraid of his power? No. I have said it once already in these pages. I will say it a last time, plainly, without pleasure, and with full knowledge of how it sounds: between the two of us, I was always slightly the better wizard. Not greatly. Slightly — the way one runner is slightly faster, invisible to the crowd, perfectly known to both runners. He knew it too. I believe it was the only fact in the world he ever hated. So when the papers wrote that Dumbledore hesitated to face Grindelwald's terrible strength, they were printing a picture of a fear I did not have. I let them print it. A false fear makes excellent cover for a true one, and I have never once corrected the record until tonight.

Was it that Hogwarts needed me, that the children needed me, that Britain needed defending? I stripped those excuses in an earlier chapter, where I said that children make excellent shields. I will not dress them up again only to undress them.

Was it hope — the hope that he might still be turned, that the boy in the garden might climb back out of the man in the fortress? There was a thread of that, in the early years. Threads do not hold a man in place for two decades. Something stronger held me.

Here it is.

I was afraid to meet him. Not to fight him — to *meet* him. I was afraid of standing in front of Gellert Grindelwald because of what Gellert Grindelwald might know.

Go back with me to the sitting room — briefly; we will not stay. End of August, 1899. Three wizards casting in a small room, fast, furious, half-blind with anger: Gellert, my brother, myself. Spells crossing in the air like weather. And in the doorway, drawn down by the noise that always hurt her, a girl of fourteen. One curse struck her. She fell, and everything I have ever done since begins at the point where she touched the floor.

Whose curse? Nobody saw. I have told you that, and it is true. But *nobody saw* is not the end of the account; it is only the beginning of it. Three people were in that room, and each carried away a different angle, a different memory of the timing, a different map of those few seconds. Aberforth had just been under the Cruciatus Curse; he was on the floor, in pain past thinking; of the three of us, he likely knew least, and I have never asked him, and he has never said. I was casting and moving and shouting; my memory of those seconds is a corridor of light with no doors in it — believe me, I have walked it thousands of nights, looking for a door, and there is none. But Gellert — Gellert stood where he stood, facing the room, with the fastest eye for spellwork I have ever known before or since.

Gellert might know whose curse it was.

Now do the small, cold arithmetic with me. There are three possible answers to the question I never asked. If the curse was Gellert's — that would have been a grief with a place to put it. A place, and a face, and a name that was not mine; hatred is a heavy load, but it is at least designed for carrying. If it was Aberforth's — that would have been terrible in a different key, a thing to protect him from, a secret to hold over my own brother like an umbrella for the rest of our lives; terrible, and bearable. Of the three possible answers, only one was unbearable. It was not "Gellert's." It was not "Aberforth's."

I could face his army. I had, by any honest measure, the means to face the man. I could not face three words. *It was you.* Three words, in that voice, in that precise and musical English he learned partly from me — and everything I had built out of the wreckage, the teacher, the refuser of ministries, the careful good man with his careful good deeds, would have come down like a stage set, because underneath every plank of it was the one uncertainty I lived in. Not knowing was not a gap in my life. Not knowing was the *house*. I told you, when I described her death, that not knowing was the only mercy I could arrange for myself. I ask you now to understand what that mercy cost to maintain, and who paid the running expenses. While Gellert and I never stood in the same room, the sentence could never be said. Every year of distance was another year in which it remained unsaid. That was the machinery of my famous patience. That was all it ever was: an ocean, kept between me and a possible sentence, at the price of a continent.

Fear does not announce itself. That is its first and best disguise. Fear hires wisdom to speak on its behalf, and wisdom works cheap and sounds wonderful. "Dumbledore waits. Dumbledore sees further than other men. Dumbledore will act when the hour is right." I read such sentences about myself for twenty years, in respectable papers, written by serious people. I saw no further than the door of a sitting room in Godric's Hollow. The hour had been right since 1926. The man the hour required was hiding in a school, reading lists of the dead at breakfast, checking that his one question was still safely unasked.

Let me be exact about the cost, since exactness is the only coin I have left to pay with. From the first letters begging me to act, around the middle twenties, to the day I finally crossed the Channel in 1945, runs roughly twenty years. In those years he took territory, and minds, and lives. How many lives are on my side of the ledger — how many of the names on those lists would still be alive if the better wizard had gone east in 1930? I do not know. I have never tried to count. Mark that: the man who reads every name will not add them up. I am a coward in my bookkeeping too, and at least, by now, a documented one.

Twice, before 1945, I came close. I record this because it is evidence of the machinery at work, and machinery is best observed in motion. In 1932 I accepted an invitation to a conference in Paris, knowing — everyone knew — that his people watched such gatherings, half-hoping the nearness alone would force the matter. I withdrew a week beforehand; the reason I gave was school business, and I even invented some. In 1938, after the orphanage, after Tom Riddle's eyes had shown me the price of watching storms form, I went so far as to book passage across the Channel. I stood in my office with the ticket on the desk, and I looked at it for the better part of an hour, and then I filed it — filed it, like a receipt — in the drawer with the drafts, where it belonged. Fear is not always a fever. Sometimes it is a clerk with perfect handwriting.

People assume it was guilt that kept me from him — shame, the unpayable debt, the sight of the one witness to my worst summer. It is a reasonable theory and it is wrong. Guilt I could carry; I had carried it since the funeral, the way you carry a stone in the chest: it is heavy, it is always there, and it asks nothing except to be carried. Guilt looks backward. Fear is a different animal entirely. Fear stands guard at a particular door, facing outward, armed. My guilt lived in 1899. My fear lived in every future room that might contain him, and it kept me out of every one.

And there was a second fear. It is smaller than the first, and it is worse, and I will say it quickly, because it does not deserve a paragraph of preparation and I do not deserve the shelter of one.

I was afraid that if I stood in front of him — grey, armed, at the head of his faithful, the most hated man in Europe — it would not feel like meeting an enemy. I was fifty years old, and sixty, and part of me had remained seventeen with the fidelity of a portrait, and I could not be certain which of us would step forward when he said my name. Love does not expire merely because it has been sentenced. I loved him. "Loved" — past tense — is a grammar; I have never been fully sure it is a fact. The Mirror of Erised had already told me as much, years before, in its quiet corner: the family restored, and at the edge of the glass, sometimes, gold. I chose to treat that as a flaw in the furniture. One does not argue with a mirror; one drapes it.

So there is the true reason, whole, in daylight: I did not go because meeting him could cost me my not-knowing, and my not-knowing was where I lived; and beneath that, I did not go because I did not trust what I would feel when I got there. Fear of a sentence. Fear of my own unfinished heart. Dress it in any robes you like — I did, for decades, and I had the finest wardrobe of justifications in Europe. Undressed, it is this.

I do not ask you to forgive it. I have not asked for forgiveness once in this book, and I am not going to begin in its darkest chapter. I ask only that you name it accurately, because accurate names are the entire project of these pages. Not patience. Not strategy. Not wisdom. Fear. The greatest wizard of the age — their phrase — was afraid of three words, and the world buried its dead while he perfected the art of not hearing them.

It is very late now, and the candle is down to its last inch, and Fawkes is awake — he has been watching me for the past page and a half, head tilted, saying nothing. Phoenixes do not judge. It is why I keep one, probably.

## 29. The Tipping Point

People imagine that great decisions arrive like thunder. In my experience they arrive like weather. Slowly, from several directions at once, and by the time you notice, the sky has already changed.

What moved me, in the end, was not one thing. It was weight. A scale does not tip because the last stone is heavy. It tips because the stone is last. But since a confession owes its reader particulars, let me set out the stones I can still identify — there were three — in the order they fell.

The first came in March of 1944, off the morning lists. In 1919 I had taught a girl called Honoria Pyke, a cheerful, unhurried student who never once managed the vanishing spell and laughed at herself so warmly each time she failed that the whole class laughed with her, not at her — a rare gift, rarer than the vanishing spell. She married a Dutch wizard and moved to Utrecht, and sent me a kind, misspelled letter when I was made Head of Transfiguration. In March of 1944, halfway down the second column, I found her son. Nineteen years old. Killed, said the paper, in the defense of his town.

I sat very still for a long time with my hand flat on the newsprint. The arithmetic that broke me was not the number of the dead — numbers, to my shame, I had learned to survive. It was the generation. The war had reached the children of my pupils. I had delayed so long that the delay itself had grown up, come of age, and begun to bury the sons of the girls I had taught. A man can tell himself he is waiting for the right hour. He cannot tell himself that for two generations. There is no clock with a face that long.

The second stone was a document. In the autumn of 1944 the Ministry, despairing of me, sent — I think as a rebuke, or a last try at shame — the full plan of what they would do instead: a combined assault on the fortress by British and allied forces, some two hundred wands, in the spring. They asked me to review it, since I would not join it. I reviewed it. It took me forty minutes to be certain of what I had suspected in ten: the plan was doomed. Nurmengard's approaches were built against exactly this — against armies, against numbers, against courage in ranks. I could name, from the map, the three points where the attack would break, and estimate, from the lists of names attached so neatly at the back, roughly who would die at each of them. Two hundred wands would fail where one wizard might not — because a fortress designed to stop an army has no particular defense against a guest, and because I knew, with a certainty I could not put in the margins of a Ministry document, that its master would open the door to one visitor in the world.

I returned the plan with my objections, and the objections were strong enough that the assault was postponed. Postponed, mark you — not cancelled. It sat in a drawer in London the way my drafts had sat in a drawer at Hogwarts, waiting for spring. From that day forward, the deaths on those back pages were on my clock. If I did not go alone, they would go together, and the list of names would write itself.

The third stone weighed nothing at all, which is how I knew the scale was finished. It was December, and I was walking down from the castle to Hogsmeade through fresh snow — bright cold, blue shadows — and behind the post office a group of village children were playing a game. One small boy stood behind a wall with his cloak pulled over his head, and the others crept toward him, daring each other, shrieking and scattering whenever he moved. And they were singing, to the tune every child knows and no adult remembers learning, a skipping-rhyme: *Don't look east and don't be told, or you'll be took by Grindelwald.*

The rhyme was old, I learned later; children had sung it for years; I had simply never happened to hear it. I stood in the middle of the lane in the snow, an old man in a violet cloak, and listened to children sing the name of the boy who had climbed over Bathilda Bagshot's garden wall rather than trouble the gate, laughing, golden, seventeen, with a book under his arm that he wanted to show me. My private history had become the world's monster under the bed. They had made a bogeyman out of the best conversation of my life, and — this is the part that finished me — they were *right*. The children's version was the true one. Mine was the fable.

Something in me completed its motion that afternoon in the snow. I felt it move, the way you feel a key turn in a lock two rooms away.

The decision itself, when it finally showed itself, was so undramatic that I nearly missed it. It was a Tuesday morning in the early spring of 1945. I was at my desk marking first-year essays — the annual harvest of blotted parchment on the theory of the match and the needle — and I finished one, and dipped my quill, and reached for the next, and found the decision lying there already made, quietly, like a coin discovered in an old coat. I did not make it. I *found* it. I believe now that it had been assembling itself for months out of the three stones and twenty years, and simply waited for a morning when my hands were busy and my guard was down. Great decisions are made the way ice melts: nothing, nothing, nothing — water.

I finished marking the essays. That is true, and I set it down because it is the most honest sentence in this chapter: the world tipped, and I finished marking the essays, because they were first-years, and first-years check the margins for comments. Then I began to put my affairs in order.

It took eleven days. I wrote out plans for my classes a term ahead. I arranged my will with the goblins, who approved of my punctuality if nothing else. I wrote letters — sendable ones, this time; my hand was almost surprised. One went to Aberforth. It was three lines long:

> Aberforth —
> I am going to Europe to stop him. I thought you should hear it from me and not from the *Prophet*.
> If I do not come back, everything is arranged; you need do nothing. — A.

No apology. Do not mistake that for coldness; it was the closest I could come to respect. He would have burned an apology, and he would have been right to, and we both knew the postage was not worth it. He did not reply. I learned, much later and from someone else, that on the evening the letter came he closed the Hog's Head early. From my brother, that is a letter.

Elphias I told in person, over dinner, plainly, between the soup and the fish, because he deserved not to read it in a newspaper and because I discovered I could not begin the sentence twice. He did not argue. He did not ask a single question — not one, not even *when*. He nodded, and talked of other things with only a little more care than usual, and at the door he shook my hand too long. Perhaps four seconds too long. Fifty years of friendship were in the extra seconds. We have never needed more vocabulary than that, he and I.

I packed lightly: travelling clothes, a little gold, no books — for the first time in my life, no books. And my wand. I mention it because of what it did that week, or what I did to it: the wand I had carried daily for half a century, which sat in my pocket with no more presence than a pen, began to weigh. I would walk the corridors and feel it against my ribs like a bar of iron. It knew, you will say, what it was being taken to do. Wands know nothing; the knowing was mine; but a man who has spent his life declining to be a weapon feels the exact hour he consents to become one, and the feeling settles, for some reason, in the pocket.

I left on a grey morning in March, alone. The Ministry had offered escorts, Aurors, an honor guard of the same wands I had saved from their own assault; I refused them all, politely, one last exercise of my one perfected skill. I crossed the Channel — the Channel that a whole country had assured itself he would never cross, and which I had never crossed toward him — and went east, by the slow means the war allowed, through a continent I had last planned to visit with Elphias Doge and two trunks and a packing list, in another century, as another person.

I will not make literature out of the journey. Burnt villages do not need my prose. I will say only that for five years I had marked a map going dark in the privacy of a locked drawer, and now I walked into the marks, and found that my ink had been accurate. That is the particular sickness of the well-informed: nothing surprised me, and I was ashamed that nothing surprised me. Stations without names. Fields without fences. People who did not meet my eyes, in case I was somebody's question.

On the ninth day I came up a valley I had memorized in an office a thousand miles away, between mountains whose names I had traced with a dry pen, and saw it above me on its crag, black against the snowfields: the fortress where he kept his war.

There were no guards on the road. Understand what that means: a fortress at war, the most wanted man in the world inside it, and the mountain road stood open like a garden path. No wards challenged me. No spell so much as tugged at my cloak. I stopped at the threshold of the outer court, in the wind, with my wand weighing in my pocket like the whole of the year 1899, and understood.

He knew I was coming. He had always known everything six weeks early. The gates were open.

## 30. The Duel

They call it the greatest duel ever fought between wizards. I have read the accounts. There are songs about it, and paintings, and a chapter near the end of every history of our century. In the paintings I am tall and terrible, wrapped in white light, and he is a shadow with burning eyes. The songs give us speeches. One of them gives us a storm.

The people who made these things were not there. Nobody was there. That is the first true fact about the greatest duel in history: it had no witnesses. Two men who had once been friends met in an empty place in the mountains, and only one of them walked back down. Everything else — the light, the storm, the speeches — was added later, by people who needed the story to be larger than the men in it.

I understand the need. A legend asks nothing of you. The truth is smaller, and worse, and it asks everything. I will tell you the truth, because that is the business of this book, and because I am the only person left who can.

His fortress stood high in the mountains, an old ruin he had taken and made into the seat of his war. Not Nurmengard — that he kept for his enemies. This was a colder place, grey stone and broken towers, with one courtyard still whole at its heart. I climbed the road alone, on foot, in the last snow of spring. I had told no one the day. I had left my affairs in order and my letters written, and the mountain was very quiet, and my breath made small clouds in front of me, and I remember thinking that the clouds were the most alive thing on that road.

I want to record what I thought about on that road, because it surprised me then and it instructs me now. I did not think about strategy. I did not rehearse spells; there is no rehearsing against a man who taught you half of what you know. I thought about breakfast at Mould-on-the-Wold, and about Doge's terrible packing lists, and about a girl feeding goats at dawn so that no one would see her. The mind, facing the largest hour of its life, went home and sat in the kitchen. I let it. We climbed together, the boy in the kitchen and the old man in the snow, and I could not have told you which of us the duel belonged to.

The gates were open. I said this before, and I say it again because it was the whole message of the man: he had always known everything six weeks early, and he wanted me to know that he knew. There were no guards. There were no traps — I checked; I am careful, whatever else I am. There was only the courtyard, and the mountain light, and a man standing in the middle of it with his hands empty at his sides.

Forty-six years do strange work on a face. They had thinned him. The golden hair — I had carried it golden through half a century — was grey-white now, cut short, an old soldier's hair. The bright quickness of the boy had hardened into something still and watchful, the way fast water hardens into ice. I stood in the gateway and looked at him, and I felt the years land on me all at once, all forty-six of them, like snow sliding from a roof.

Then he spoke, and that was the terrible part. The face had aged. The voice had not.

"Albus," he said, as if we had parted the week before, at the garden wall, with an owl waiting.

Nothing in all my preparations had frightened me as much as how easy it was to answer.

"Gellert."

"You are late," he said. "I expected you twenty years ago. I had arguments ready. Good ones." A small movement at the corner of his mouth — not quite the old smile, but its grave. "They have gone stale. It is a pity. You would have enjoyed them."

"I have read them," I said. "You published most of them."

"Not the best ones. The best ones were for you."

That was all we said, or nearly all, and I will not decorate it. There were no speeches. There was no storm. Two old men stood in a cold courtyard and spoke perhaps forty words, and the largest thing between them was never said at all.

Because here is the second true fact, the one that matters more to me than the outcome: I did not ask him the question. I had come up that mountain still not knowing whose curse killed my sister, and I had understood, somewhere on the road, that I would go down it not knowing too. For half a century that question had stood between me and this courtyard like a locked door. I had let a continent burn rather than knock on it. And now I stood three steps from the only man who might have the answer, and I found that I was not going to ask, and that I could live — could fight, could win, could fail — without asking.

Understand me: this was not strength arriving. It was only weight. The names on the lists had grown heavier than my fear, that is all — one name at a time, year by year, until the scale finally tipped. But when I walked through those open gates with the question still shut inside me, the worst of him lost its power over me. Whatever he said or did not say, whatever he knew, I was already carrying the heaviest version of the truth. A man who has accepted the worst sentence cannot be threatened with it.

That was my victory. It happened before the first spell, and no song will ever mention it.

He drew first. I want that written down plainly, not as an excuse — I had come to fight and would have drawn in another heartbeat — but because it was so exactly him: he could not bear a silence he did not own.

Of the duel itself I can give you only pieces, because that is how I hold it. Speed, first of all — a speed I had not met in forty-six years, because no one else alive had it. Light of every colour and no colour. The courtyard stone rising at his word into shapes with claws, and falling back to stone at mine. Fire that bent around my shield like water around a rock, looking for me, patient as a living thing. Cold that cracked the air. At some point the snow above us on the peaks came loose and roared down somewhere out of sight, and neither of us glanced at it.

And under all of it, the strangest thing, the thing I have never told anyone: it felt like conversation. We had learned magic from the same summer, from the same garden, from each other's sentences. Every attack he made, I had half-thought of first; every defence I raised, he finished reading before it formed. Fighting him was like dueling my own reflection with better weather. Twice I caught myself admiring him in the middle of a spell that was meant to kill me, and once — I am ashamed of this, and I am too old to leave it out — once, matching him turn for turn with the whole sky burning, I felt something that was horribly close to joy. No one had run beside my mind for forty-six years. He was trying to end my life, and part of me was simply glad of his company.

One picture stays sharper than the rest. Somewhere in the middle of it, both of us behind shields, both of us for one breath doing nothing, we looked at each other across the wrecked courtyard — simply looked, the way you look across a lane at a lit window. I do not know what my face held. His held something I had seen once before, on a summer evening, over a chessboard, when I had beaten him in eleven moves: pure delighted attention. Then the breath ended, and we went back to trying to kill each other, and I understood that for him, too, this was the only conversation left, and he did not want it to stop either.

The songs say we fought for three hours. Perhaps. Time was not keeping count, and neither was I.

He had the Elder Wand, and the Elder Wand is not a story. Every spell he cast arrived larger than it left him; every shield of his weighed more than mine. It was like playing chess against a man who moves twice for your once. That advantage should have decided everything, and against any other wizard on earth it would have. It very nearly did. I was slower by the second hour, and burned along one arm, and I understood with perfect clearness that I could not beat the wand.

So I stopped trying to beat the wand, and set about beating the man.

Here is what I knew, that no history book knows: Gellert Grindelwald could not be patient. He had never needed to be. All his life, doors had opened at his first touch — schools, minds, countries, me. He did not want to win our duel; he wanted to win it brilliantly, in one grand shape, a masterpiece — because I was the audience he had been performing for since he was seventeen, and this was the last performance. He needed the beautiful ending.

I only needed the ending.

So I gave him a door. I let my shield thin on the left side — slowly, over many exchanges, the way real exhaustion thins it, because he would smell a trick that came too fast. I let his curse through. It cost me: a wound along the ribs, deep enough to be honest. I do not recommend the method. But he saw me stagger, and he saw the finish, the grand shape, the masterpiece one stroke away — and he reached for it with everything, at last fully open, the way you are only open when you are certain.

Patience is not a glorious spell. There is no incantation. It is only the oldest magic I know: the willingness to be hurt now for the sake of later. He had never learned it. There had always been someone else to be hurt on his behalf.

My counter-spell took him wand and all.

The finish of the greatest duel ever fought looked like this: an old man sitting down hard on the stones of a cold courtyard, and another old man standing over him, bleeding, holding two wands. The mountain was very quiet again. My breath made its small clouds. Somewhere below, unbelievably, a bird was singing, because birds are not informed of history.

He looked up at me and began, softly, to laugh. Not madness — I know that laugh in the histories, and it is wrong. It was the real one, the summer one, tired now.

"Still the better wizard, Albus," he said. "I did wonder, all these years. It is almost a relief."

I said nothing. I was watching his hands.

"They will put me in Nurmengard," he said, as if remarking on weather. "There is a justice in it that I would have admired, once, in someone else's life." Then the laughter went out of his face, and something older came into it, and he said, quite gently: "About your sister —"

"No," I said.

One word. I had climbed a mountain and crossed a war to stand within reach of the answer, and when it opened its mouth I closed the door myself. Do not call it strength. Call it what I call it: the one mercy left that I could still arrange, and I was no longer sure, by then, which of us I was arranging it for.

He studied me a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and did not finish the sentence — not then, not ever. He understood mercy, I think, the way a man born deaf understands music: from the outside, by its shape. But he understood it.

I bent and picked up the Elder Wand from the stones. The Wand of Destiny. The Deathstick. The unbeatable weapon two boys once planned a summer around, the first Hallow, the spine of the revolution, the most dangerous object in the world.

It felt like a stick.

That is all. A length of elder wood, light in the hand, still warm from his grip. No song in it, no thunder, no voice. I stood in that ruined courtyard holding the thing we had been willing to remake the world for, and it weighed nothing at all, and my sister still weighed everything, and I thought: so this is how it ends — not with a storm, but with an inventory.

My black hand lies on the page as I write this, and even it remembers: the wand was never the heavy thing. Everything true is an anticlimax. The legends are how we forgive the truth for being so small.

## 31. Nurmengard

The world found out within a week, and the world went mad with joy, and I watched it the way you watch rain through a window: close enough to touch, and not touching.

There were parades. I am told there was dancing in the Ministry atrium, on the desks, and that a very senior official sang. Church bells rang in wizarding villages from Scotland to Sicily — our people and the Muggles were celebrating in the same months, their war ending as ours did, and in a few places the two joys ran together in the streets and nobody asked questions. The Daily Prophet printed my face across the whole front page under one word, and the word was not a word I would have chosen, and I will not repeat it here.

The letters came in sacks. For twenty years, letters had come asking why I did nothing; now the same hands wrote to thank me for everything, and some of them, I am certain, were the very same hands. A widow from Bruges, whose Howler I had once listened to standing and all the way through, sent three lines of thanks and a pressed flower. I have kept the flower. I answered every letter that had a living person behind it, kindly and briefly, and each answer cost me something I could not name at the time. I can name it now: every "thank you" was addressed to the man who came in 1945, and I signed for him, the way you sign for a neighbour's parcel. The letters for the man who should have come in 1925 do not get written. The dead do not have his address.

They gave me the Order of Merlin, First Class. There was a ceremony. I stood on a platform while a man I hardly knew listed my virtues to a hall full of strangers, and the list was long, and every item on it was technically true, and the sum of it was a person who did not exist. I remember looking at my own hands during the applause and thinking that they had held my dying sister and the Elder Wand and this medal, in that order, and that of the three, the medal was the only one that had cost me nothing, and it was the one they cheered.

Soon afterwards a confectionery company wrote to ask whether my likeness might appear on the cards they put in their chocolate frogs. I said yes. People find this modest and charming of me. The truth is simpler: by then I had understood that the hero of 1945 was a picture, and a picture may as well be printed on something children enjoy. It was the most honest use anyone proposed for him.

Through all of it — the parades, the platform, the songs — I felt as though I were watching my own celebration through thick glass. On the far side, a great man had defeated a monster he had never met. On my side, an old man had disarmed his oldest friend in an empty courtyard and then sat with him a while in the cold, because the escort could not arrive before morning, and neither of us had anything left to say, and we did not say it together, in silence, all night, like two travellers waiting for different trains.

Now let me write down what was actually done, after, because no history records it and I was there.

Gellert Grindelwald was taken to Nurmengard. There was no trial worth the name — what court was large enough for a continent's grief? — and there was, to the Ministry's disappointment, no cage-wagon, no chains for the photographers. I insisted on that, my one use of the new power nobody would have refused me. He walked. I walked with him, with the escort behind us, two days through the mountains to the prison he had built, and we spoke about nothing — the weather, the road, once, briefly and seriously, about goats, because I mentioned my brother kept them and he asked, to my astonishment, whether Aberforth had ever forgiven me, and I said no, and he said, "Good. One of us should hold the line." I have never known whether that was cruelty or the kindest thing he ever said to me. With him the two were never far apart.

Nurmengard stands on rock, higher than its own weather, a black tower you smell before you see — cold iron and old fear. He had filled it, over the years, with everyone who told him no. In the weeks before we arrived, the cells had been opened, and his enemies — the ones still living — had walked out through the gate as free people. Some of them were on the road as we climbed it, going down while we went up: thin men and women with new coats over old bones, stopping to stare at the prisoner. Nobody shouted at him. I had expected shouting. They only looked, the long look of people checking that a thing is real, and then walked on down toward the world. He met every pair of eyes. Give him that; it is on the record now: he met every pair of eyes.

So the tower that had held hundreds was to hold a single prisoner: its architect. The world found the justice of this delicious. The world was welcome to it.

Over the gate, cut deep into the stone by some careful, obedient chisel, were the words. You know the words. FOR THE GREATER GOOD. I had written them first when I was seventeen, at three in the morning, in a letter that smelled of candle smoke, thinking myself the most generous soul in England. Now I walked under them, into a prison, escorting the man I had written them to. I am perhaps the only person who has ever entered a prison beneath an epigraph he himself composed. I looked up at my own handwriting made stone — he had kept even my capital letters — and I felt the sentence complete itself around me like a lock.

There was no last conversation. I want that plainly on the record, because storytellers cannot resist giving us one — the tower cell, the final words, the tear. No. We climbed the stairs. The cell at the top was small and clean, with one window looking at nothing but sky, and I thought, he chose this cell for his worst enemy, and here he is. He stepped in. He looked around it with the eye of a man checking the work of builders. Then he looked at me, and said nothing, and I said nothing, and I closed the door, and that was the end of the summer of 1899, forty-six years and two dead parents and one dead sister after it began.

I never visited. Not once, in all the years after, though the journey is nothing to a wizard and the permission was mine for the asking. People have wondered at it. Those who think me kind imagine it would have pained me; those who think me cold imagine I had simply finished with him. Both are wrong, and since this book exists to say the things I never said: I did not go because there was one room left in the world where the sentence could still be spoken to me — *it was you* — and he lived in it. He had built the room, and I had written the words over its gate, and between the two of us we had made the one place on earth I could not enter twice. I stopped being afraid of armies in 1945. The sentence I was never brave enough to stand in front of, I simply arranged never to hear. You will notice the method. It was my life's one great talent: the elegant arrangement of not-knowing.

Years later — many years — a report crossed my desk. Reports on Nurmengard came rarely by then; the world was forgetting him at the usual speed of the world. This one noted, among inspection details, in a single dry line, that the prisoner in the top cell appeared, in the guards' judgement, to feel remorse for his crimes. He had said so. He had said it, apparently, to no one in particular, facing the window.

I read that line in the evening, alone in this office, and I sat still for a long time — even now, writing the word *remorse*, my quill slows — and I have examined many times what it was I felt, because it was not what it should have been. It should have been hope, or healing, or some quiet gladness for him. What I felt was envy. I am not proud of it, but I am done being selective. I envied him, because he had somewhere to send his remorse. His crimes had names, numbers, dates, gravestones; a man can kneel at that. Mine has no address. Whose curse killed Ariana? Not knowing was the mercy I chose, and I chose it twice, and the price of that mercy is that my remorse has wandered for a hundred years like an owl with a letter and no window to deliver it to. He, in his tower, facing his sky, was at least writing to somebody. I have only ever written to *perhaps*.

And still — I will allow the old man in the cell this much, on the record: they say remorse is the hardest magic. Harder than the duel. I believe it. Of the two of us, it is possible he attempted the harder thing first.

Two more small facts belong to that year, and then I am done with it.

Elphias Doge was waiting for me at the castle gates when I returned. Nobody had told him the day; he had simply come every afternoon, he admitted later, for most of a month, and stood a while, and gone home. When I appeared on the road he did not wave. He walked to meet me, shook my hand too long — the same handshake he had given me when I left, as if the two were one handshake with a war in the middle — and said, "There's supper kept." Not one question. In fifty years, never one question. The great tact of unclever men, I called it once, and I let the word "unclever" stand in this book so that I can put on record, here, what my cleverness was worth next to it.

And I wrote to Aberforth — three lines again, informing again: it was finished; I was back; she had been mentioned by no one. No reply came. But that September, walking through Hogsmeade at dusk, I passed the Hog's Head, and my brother was in the doorway with a crate in his arms, and he looked at me, and nodded — one short nod, the kind you give a man whose cart has finally, after long delay, delivered — and went inside. We have built cathedrals in this family out of less. I walked back up to the castle warmer than the medal had ever made me.

That autumn — the autumn of 1945 — I went home. Not to a parade; term was starting. I came back to Hogwarts with a healed rib, a medal I never wore, and the most dangerous object in the world in my coat pocket, and on the first of September I stood in front of a class of first-years, eleven years old, still smelling of the train, and taught them to turn a match into a needle.

It is the oldest first lesson we have, and I had taught it for four decades, but I had never heard it properly until that morning. Take something small and ordinary, the lesson says, and change it carefully, by attention, one property at a time — and when you make a mistake, it is a match and a needle, and nothing burns, and nobody falls. At the back of the room a boy got his needle last of everyone, near the end of the hour, and scowled at it, and when the bell rang he looked almost sorry to stop. The Elder Wand lay along my ribs like a splinter of everything opposite, and I thought: this is the whole choice, and I have finally made it. The unbeatable wand in my pocket would spend the rest of its working life producing matchsticks, needles, and the occasional firework at the end-of-year feast. I can think of no better revenge on it.

So the great year closed. I had gone up a mountain and come down a hero, and I knew what I actually was: a widower of something that has no name — not a friendship, not only a love, not quite a war; the single person on earth who had run level with my mind was in a tower captioned in my handwriting, and I would never see him again, and I had chosen that, and the choice was right, and it cost what right choices cost. There is no word for what I lost in winning. I have had fifty years to find one and I offer you instead the facts.

I gave out house points. I confiscated a biting teacup. I ate dinner at the long table under the floating candles, between colleagues who were shy of me for a month or two and then, being teachers, mercifully forgot to be. And the years, which had held their breath since June of 1899, quietly began again.

## Part Five: The Stone

*Every story I have told you so far happened to a young man, and I could hide behind the years between us. This last part happened to an old man, and there is nowhere left to hide. It is about a stone, a lie about socks, and a hand that turned black. It is about how little a person changes, and what that little costs.*

## 32. The Headmaster

There is not much to tell about the next forty-six years, and that was the point of them.

I came back from the duel a hero, and I did what heroes are not supposed to do. I gave out house points. I marked essays. I grew old on purpose. The world wanted me to be a great man, but I had seen where great men end — in mountain prisons, with their best sentences carved over the gate. So I chose to be a useful man instead. It is less beautiful. It lasts longer.

For some years I went on teaching Transfiguration, the art of changing one thing into another. I have always found it a comfortable subject, because it is honest. Nothing in Transfiguration pretends that change comes free. The spell must balance, weight for weight, this for that. I spent my life wishing that the rest of magic — and the rest of me — worked the same way.

Then, in time, the governors offered me the school itself. Headmaster of Hogwarts. I remember reading the letter twice and waiting to feel the old hunger wake up and reach for it. It did wake. But a school is a small kingdom, and its subjects leave every seven years, and its king spends his evenings signing permission forms. If a man must be given power, let it be power with homework. I said yes.

So I moved into the round office at the top of the tower, where the portraits of dead headmasters pretend to sleep and listen to everything. I filled the room with small silver instruments that tick and turn and puff smoke. Visitors think the instruments watch dark wizards. Some do. Most of them simply tick. I like a room that sounds like time passing. A man with my history should never be allowed to forget that it does.

Fame arrived properly in those years, the heavy kind that does not wash off. Order of Merlin, First Class. Grand Sorcerer. Supreme this, Chief that. And one honor larger than all of them: my face on a chocolate frog card. I am told that children mostly know me as a face that vanishes from a chocolate frog card. Of all my titles, it is the most accurate. You open the packet, an old man smiles at you, and when you look again he is gone, and what you keep is the sweet.

The Ministry never asked for me again after the third time; three refusals, it seems, is the dose that cures them. I have told you about those letters — sensible, flattering — and how I said no by return owl, quickly, the way you step back from an open fire. The last refusal had taken me one line, and I was proud of the line, which shows you how little the years improve us. The papers called it modesty. You know by now what it was. It was a leash, and by then the leash had become a habit, and habit is the only cage I have ever trusted to hold me.

I will be honest about the trick hidden inside that, too. A reputation for refusing power is its own quiet kind of power. Say no to the Minister's office three times and you become larger than the Minister; the crown you would not take shines on you from a shelf. There is no way out of that circle. I have looked for one all my life. The best I found was to know the circle is there, and to say so, here, on this page.

The routine of a school is a kind of architecture, and I lived inside it gratefully. September: the boats on the black lake, the frightened first-years, the Sorting Hat's new song. October: the smell of pumpkin. The winter feasts, the spring exams, the June morning when the carriages roll away and the castle goes quiet like a heart between beats. Term after term, year after year, the same wheel. People imagine that a clever man suffers in a routine. It is the opposite. A routine is what I do instead of a greater good. Every bell that rings on time is a small war that never happened.

And the children came through, wave after wave of them. I learned their grandparents' faces first, then their parents', then theirs. I learned that the cruel ones are almost always frightened, that the lazy ones are almost always sad, and that the truly dangerous ones are polite. I kept a special eye on the brilliant lonely ones — the ones who shine and have nowhere to put the light. I knew that species. I had been its finest example.

Twice in those years the darkness rose again, and I must give it two paragraphs and no more, because this book is a confession, not a history.

The boy from the orphanage kept his promise of becoming. Tom Riddle went down into the dark arts the way other men go home, and came back up calling himself Lord Voldemort, and gathered followers, and taught this country the meaning of fear for eleven years. I fought him — at a distance, through others, with plans. I was the only wizard he was ever afraid of, they say. Perhaps. I have learned not to be flattered when a dark wizard fears me. It only means he has read my biography more carefully than my admirers have.

His first war ended in one night, in October of 1981, in Godric's Hollow. Of all the villages in England — that one. He went there to kill a family called Potter because of a prophecy, and he killed the father and the mother, and the curse he threw at the child broke, and struck him instead, and he was gone — not dead, I knew that even then, but gone. The boy lived. He was one year old, and he had a scar on his forehead, and half a street away from where he lay crying stood the house where my sister died and the churchyard where she is buried. Fate is not subtle. It simply trusts us not to read.

I had a part in what came after — placements, protections, plans. I sent the boy to his Muggle relatives, to grow up away from his own legend, and I have argued with myself about that decision on many nights since. It was a cold sum, and it was the safest thing, and both of those statements are true, and I no longer expect them to make peace with each other. I said two paragraphs. That was the second.

The wars filled my school with orphans, and I noticed that I collected them. Not officially; a headmaster has no favorites, and I kept the rule in public and broke it in private, in small ways, all my life. A word in a corridor. A name remembered. A job found for a boy nobody else would touch. Somewhere behind all of it stood a man in an orphanage office in 1938, offering caution to a child who needed something warmer, and never being sure — to this day — whether anything warmer would have been taken. I could not repair that. So I did what people do with the debts they cannot pay to the right person. I paid them to the wrong ones, for decades, and called it a school policy.

Through all of it, one village away, my brother poured drinks in a dirty bar and did not write to me, and I did not write to him. Our silence was old enough by then to have a pension. On the worst nights of the first war I knew — without a word passing between us — that the Hog's Head was one of the safest rooms in Britain, and that anyone I sent there would be scowled at and protected. We worked together for years, Aberforth and I, the way two oxen work: pulling the same cart, never looking at each other. It is not forgiveness. It is not nothing, either.

So the decades went. I taught, I watched, I refused things, I kept my instruments ticking, and every summer I survived was a summer in which the young man of 1899 had been outvoted again. If you had asked me what I was doing with my life, I would have smiled and offered you a sherbet lemon. What I was doing was standing guard on myself. Nearly a century at the same post. They give medals for less.

Then it was 1991, and one evening in June the Deputy Headmistress brought me, as she did every year, the list of children who would receive their first Hogwarts letters. I read it as I always did, slowly, with pleasure, the way other old men read the racing results. And near the bottom of the page, between two perfectly ordinary names, there it was.

Potter, Harry.

The whole staffroom had known this year was coming. Half the wizarding world had marked it on some private calendar. The boy who lived was eleven, and he was coming to my school, into my care, under my ticking instruments.

I sat with the list a long time after Minerva had gone. I was not thinking about prophecies or dark lords, not yet. I was thinking that this child had my old problem in reverse. I had spent my school years fighting to make my name mean me and not my father. Everyone knew his name before he could read it. I had run toward my fame; his had been nailed to him in his sleep, along with a scar and a debt he had never signed for.

Somewhere in the castle a clock struck. I wrote the boy's name into the register in my own hand, which I did not usually do, and I remember that my writing was very careful, like a man signing for a delivery he has been dreading and awaiting for ten years.

He arrived in September, small, thin, with his mother's eyes and his father's uncombed hair, and no idea at all why strangers went quiet when he passed. I watched him from the staff table and made myself a promise so old it was practically a relic: watch, protect, and do not reach. I had learned the cost of an old man's hunger pointed at a brilliant boy. This time, I told myself, I would only stand at the door.

That was the plan. Then came Christmas, and a mirror I knew far too well, and the promise had to grow more complicated — as promises do, the moment they meet a child's face. But that is the next chapter, and it deserves its own.

## 33. Socks

The Mirror of Erised stood, that year, in an empty classroom on an unused corridor — part of the defenses we had built around a different stone, Nicolas Flamel's, which is a story I will leave to one line, because it is not this story. I knew where the Mirror was because I had put it there. You may raise an eyebrow at that: a man who calls a thing the most dangerous object he knows, and then keeps it in his house. I can only answer that we keep our dangers close for the same reason we keep our wands close. We like to know where they are.

A little after Christmas, I noticed that a first-year boy had begun disappearing at night.

I will not pretend it took detective work. An old castle tells its headmaster things, and an old headmaster hears more than he is told. A door left ajar on a corridor nobody visits. Small footprints in December dust that began in the middle of a floor, as footprints do when a boy owns a certain cloak. I should say here, because it belongs to this book more than to any other: that Christmas I had sent Harry a gift that was in my keeping — his father's Invisibility Cloak. Ignotus Peverell's cloak. The third Hallow, the one Gellert and I had ranked last, all those summers ago, because it only hid people, and we could not imagine wanting to hide. Of the three, it is the only one that never cost anyone anything. That should have told two clever boys where wisdom lay. It told us nothing at all.

So: a boy, a cloak, and a corridor. I went and looked, one night, from the doorway, and there he was — sitting on the cold floor in front of the Mirror of Erised, cross-legged, quite still, the glass throwing its silver light on his face.

I did not go in. Not that first night. I told myself I was giving him privacy, and there was truth in that, but there was also this: I stood in a dark doorway watching a child watch the Mirror, and for several minutes I could not have moved if the castle had caught fire. I knew the room too well from the other side of it.

The second night he came again, and brought his friend, and tried to share the glass — tried to show his family to another pair of eyes, and could not, because the Mirror does not work that way; the friend saw only his own wishes. I watched the confusion on both faces and thought: there it is, the first lesson, arriving on its own. What the Mirror gives you cannot be shown to anyone. You stand in a crowd of your beloved dead, and to the person beside you, you are a boy staring at furniture.

I did not need to see into the Mirror to know what it showed him. The Mirror stands facing you and shows the deepest desire of your heart, and this child's desire was the simplest and oldest in the world. His mother. His father. The family he had never once seen, standing around him, waving, smiling — a whole crowd of Potters, I later understood, generations of them, come out of the glass to look at their boy. He had never even seen a photograph. He was eleven, and he had found a window to the dead, and he was doing exactly what you or I would do. He was sitting down in front of it forever.

I recognized everything. The hour of night. The stillness. The way he had stopped hearing the castle around him. Most of all I recognized the schedule — because a desire like that does not visit the Mirror; it moves in. First one night, then every night. First an hour, then until dawn. I knew the shape of that fall so exactly because I had performed it myself, in another century, when I was a young teacher with a dead family and a burnt letter behind me, kneeling in front of a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but in the glass.

Nobody had come for me, back then. Nobody had stood in the doorway. I had walked myself back from that Mirror with my own two hands, and it had been the closest thing of my whole life — closer than any duel — and some part of me has never entirely left the room.

That is why I intervened. Not wisdom. Recognition. When you have drowned, you do not stand on the shore debating whether the swimmer is truly in trouble.

On the third night I did not stand in the doorway. I was sitting on the floor by the wall when he arrived, an old man among the desks, and I let him find me there. I have found that children forgive you almost anything if you are already sitting down.

We talked. I will not report the whole conversation; some of it belongs to him. I told him what the Mirror was and what it does — that it shows not your face but your heart's deepest wish; that it gives no knowledge and no truth; that men have wasted away in front of it, watching, unable to tell whether what they see is real or even possible. I told him it would be moved to a new home, and I asked him not to go looking for it again. And I told him the thing I have repeated to students for decades, the only sentence of mine I hope survives me: that a man can dream so well that he forgets to live, and that the mirror is where such men go.

He listened. He was better at hearing hard things than most adults I have known; life had trained him early. And then, as he was leaving — already at the edge of the lamplight — he stopped, and turned, and asked the question.

What do you see, sir? When you look in the Mirror?

I had been waiting for that question for ninety years.

I do not mean I expected it that night. I mean that long ago, after my own nights at the glass, I had made a private resolution: if anyone ever asks me what I see, I will lie, and the lie will be small, and it will be warm. I had even chosen it. The lie had been folded away in a drawer of my mind for most of a century, like the very thing it was about.

And still — I want this recorded — for one heartbeat I considered telling him the truth. He had shown me his dead without meaning to; there would have been a kind of fairness in showing him mine. A dead sister, younger than the students he ate breakfast with. A brother, a duel, a floor. For one heartbeat the whole confession you are now reading pressed against my teeth, five years early, wanting out. Then I looked at him properly — eleven, tired, in pajamas, his parents still shining somewhere behind his eyes — and the heartbeat passed, and I am glad of it. There is a time for truth. Midnight, to a child, beside this particular mirror, was not it.

I looked at the boy, and I said that I saw myself holding a pair of thick woolen socks.

He blinked. I explained, gravely, that one can never have enough socks; that another Christmas had come and gone without my receiving a single pair; that people insisted on giving me books. And he laughed — no, he did not quite laugh, he was too polite — but something in his shoulders came down from around his ears, and he said goodnight, and went off to bed under his father's cloak, and did not go looking for the Mirror again.

Now. This book is a confession, so let me confess the socks properly.

It was a lie, and I have never regretted it, and I want to be exact about why. The truthful answer to his question was: I see my family alive, my sister unbroken, my brother's nose unbroken, my mother pouring tea — and I could not lay that at the feet of an eleven-year-old who had just been dragged, gently, away from his own dead. You do not hand a child your grief and call it honesty. He had asked me for a fact; what he needed was a door out of that room that both of us could walk through. The lie was not a wall between us. The lie was a gift.

And there was a second reason, less noble. One does not tell a child that the wise old man who has just lectured him about the Mirror is himself, at a hundred and ten, still the boy at the glass. Some of my authority that night was borrowed, and the socks were the interest I paid on it.

But here is the strange part, the part I only understood years later, writing this. As lies go, it was very nearly a confession.

Think about what socks are. Warm. Small. Cheap. Knitted by someone who thought about the length of your cold feet, of all unglamorous things, while their hands worked. Nobody has ever seized power for a pair of socks. Nobody has ever carved SOCKS over a prison gate. They are the exact opposite of everything I chased in 1899 — the mundane, the daily, the good at hand, the love that comes in wool rather than in visions. When I stood in front of that Mirror as a young man, what did I actually see? Not thrones. A kitchen. Tea. My people, safe and bored, on an ordinary evening. The greatest wizard of the age, and the deepest desire of his heart could be knitted by any grandmother in a week.

So when I told the boy I dreamed of socks, I was lying about the picture and telling the truth about its size.

I had the Mirror moved shortly after, to a place I will not name even here, and I did to it what I have done to every dangerous beloved thing in my life: I made it useful, I put it to work guarding something, and I did not visit it. That is my whole method, if you want it. I never learned to kill my desires. I only learned to give them a job far from me.

He believed me, I think — the boy. Children assume that old men want boring things. They are almost right. We want the boring things back.

## 34. The Ring

The house of the Gaunts had mostly given up being a house.

It stood — leaned — outside the village of Little Hangleton, in a tangle of nettles and dark trees, with a dead snake nailed to the door by some earlier hand. The Gaunts had been an old wizarding family gone rotten with pride, and Tom Riddle's mother had been born in that ruin, and there, I had reason to believe, her son had hidden one of his terrible anchors: an object into which he had sealed a piece of himself, so that death could not take hold of him. That is all the explanation this chapter needs. The war had returned; I was hunting those objects; the hunt led me, in the summer of 1996, to a shack in the weeds.

I want you to see me clearly as I go in, because in a few paragraphs you will not recognize me.

I was a hundred and fifteen years old, or within a few weeks of it. I had won the duel they still write songs about. I had spent a lifetime refusing power on schedule, checking the knots of my leash every morning. I had stood in front of the Mirror of Erised and walked away; I had heard the word Hallows spoken with hunger and had answered with caution; I had taught three generations of children that the first defense of a wizard is to think before touching. If you had assembled a committee to choose the one person on earth who could be trusted alone in a room with a cursed temptation, my name would have led the list. I would have voted for me.

Hold that man in your mind. Watch what an afternoon does to him.

The shack was one room of dust and ruin. Broken chairs, a cold hearth, the smell of old damp and older anger. I moved slowly, reading the air, and the air was thick with defenses — his defenses, layer under layer, patient as only a man who intends to live forever can be patient. I unpicked them for the better part of an hour, and I was calm, and my hands were steady, and I found the place at last beneath the floor: a loose board, and under the board a small golden box, and in the box a ring.

A heavy ring, ugly, old gold, made for a thicker finger than mine. Set into it, a black stone, cracked down the middle by some great violence. I lifted it into the grey light to read the curse on it — and I saw, cut into the flat face of the stone, thin as a hair, a mark.

A triangle. Inside the triangle, a circle. Through both, from top to bottom, a single straight line.

The Cloak. The Stone. The Wand.

I had last seen that mark traced by a boy's finger on a gravestone, in a churchyard in Godric's Hollow, in the August of 1899. Golden head bent over the stone of Ignotus Peverell, evening light in his hair, the only reverence I ever saw in him. I had stood at his shoulder — close enough that our sleeves touched — and we had sworn to hunt the three Hallows together, after. You remember that "after." I wrote a chapter about it. My sister lived inside that "after," unexamined, and it fell on her.

And I knew — in one white instant, with the full force of a mind that has never once had the mercy to be slow — what I was holding. The crack in the stone was where a curse had tried to destroy it and half failed. The line, the circle, the triangle were not decoration. This ugly ring out of a ruined house had come down through the Gaunts from the Peverells themselves, and set in it, black and cracked and real, was the Resurrection Stone. The second Hallow. The one I had wanted.

The one I had told myself I wanted for my parents — and had confessed, in these pages, that I wanted as an escape key wearing mourning clothes.

Here is what a century of wisdom said to me in that moment. It said: this is an object of Voldemort's, and therefore it is cursed. It said: he does not know what the stone is; he sealed his soul-piece into it for the family name, not the Hallow; the trap on it is his, and it will be lethal. It said: put it down, take the box, contain it, examine it in the castle with Severus beside you. It said all of this clearly, in good order, the way my mind has said clear and orderly things all my life.

And then it went silent. All of it. The headmaster, the war-winner, the refuser of ministries, the man of the leash and the knots — the whole assembled committee of men I had spent my life building — went silent together, the way a schoolroom goes silent when something extraordinary walks past the window. And through that silence came one thought, and it was not a thought of power, and it was not even a thought of grief. It was practical. It was gentle. It was seventeen years old.

I could say sorry.

That was all. Not: I could master death. Not even: I could see them. First, before everything, that. They could stand in front of me — Ariana, my mother, my father, him — no. Them. They could stand in front of me on this rotten floor, and I could finally say the sentence I have been rewriting for a hundred years, and they could hear it. Ariana could hear it. She liked the goats, and humming, and my worst small charms, and she could stand here and hear me say sorry, and the word would at last have somewhere to go.

I turned the ring once in my fingers. I am told the whole failure took less than a minute, and I believe it, but from inside it had no length at all — the way the drop from a tower has no length. Somewhere very far away, in a classroom fifty years deep, an old professor was telling a row of children to think before touching. I heard him quite clearly. He sounded wise. He sounded, above all, like someone else's problem.

I put the ring on.

A lifetime of wisdom against one apology, and wisdom lost in under a minute. Let no one tell you that people change. We only build better fences around the same animal. I had built the finest fences in Europe. I climbed them in one step, an old man moving like a boy, and I did not even notice the climb, because I was already looking past the fence at a kitchen with tea in it.

The stone was cold. Then my hand was fire.

The curse came up my fingers like something alive and starving — his patience, his spite, waiting all those years in the metal for exactly the kind of fool I had just become. I remember the floor. I remember, with a strange clarity, thinking two thoughts at once, both true: so this is how, and — even then, even burning — but I did not see her. That was the pain under the pain. The trap took my hand, and it did not even pay me the minute I had bought with it.

There were some hours after that of which I will spare you the details, because I have spared you very few in this book and I am owed one mercy. Broken defenses, a broken old man, and a long way home. But I had one advantage no other fool in my position ever had: at the end of the journey there was Severus Snape.

He did not gasp when he saw the hand, already black to the wrist. He did not ask what I had touched or why — he understood the what at a glance, and I believe he understood the why a moment later and had the courtesy to say nothing. He worked through the night. What he did was not healing; the curse cannot be healed. It was containment. He trapped it in my right hand the way you trap a fire in one room of a house — by closing every door on it and accepting that the room is lost. The hand is dead. It looks, as I believe I told you at the very beginning, like a burnt glove.

When he had finished, he gave me his verdict in the voice he uses for marking essays. A year. Perhaps a little more, with discipline. I have never had discipline about the things that matter; I have only had fences. Call it a year.

And so the frame of this book closes, and the two ends of the story meet, here, at my desk, at night, with Fawkes drowsing on his perch and this blackened hand lying on the page like evidence — which is what it is.

That is the hand you met on the first page. Now you know what it paid for. Not knowledge. Not power. Not even a ghost. One more minute in my mother's kitchen — the bill came to a hand and a year of life, and the minute was never even delivered. An old man reached for the same thing the boy reached for in 1899, by the same light, with the same excuse, and this time only the reacher was destroyed. You may call that justice arriving late. Most justice is late. It loses none of its accuracy on the road.

I have had a year, now almost spent, to sit with what the ring taught me, and here is the whole lesson, and it is short. The Hallows never once lied to us. The tale always said plainly what the Stone does: it brings back something less than the dead, sorrow wearing a familiar face, and no peace to anyone. The Wand always promised nothing but winning. The Cloak only ever offered to hide you. Every promise was written clearly on the label. We did the lying — Gellert and I, and the Gaunts, and Voldemort, and every hungry hand across seven centuries. The objects told the truth. The truth was simply not what we were shopping for.

I write this with my left hand, as I have written every page of this confession. The right one rests on the paper, black and quiet, holding it flat against the night breeze from the window. It has found its last job, you see. Even my mistakes end up working for the school.

## 35. One Year

Here the past runs out.

For a whole book I have written "I was" and "I did". Now there is nothing left behind me to tell. The story has caught up with this desk, this quill, this black hand that holds the page flat. From this line forward, everything is "I am". It is a strange grammar for a dying man. I find I like it.

I have perhaps seven months left, if Severus's arithmetic holds. His arithmetic always holds. The hand is worse than it was in the summer; the black has crossed the wrist now, slow as ink in water. It does not hurt much anymore, which I am told is not good news. I thanked him for his honesty. He gave me the look he gives students who thank him.

What does a man do with one year? I can answer that question from experience now, which is the only way I have ever truly answered anything.

First, he orders his affairs. I have spent the autumn arranging what must happen after me. Some of it is terrible; none of it is impulsive — I checked. I checked the way I have checked the knots of my leash every morning for most of a century. That is all I will say about the war in these pages. This book was never about the war, though wars kept walking through it.

And then, when the affairs are ordered, he discovers something that no healthy man will believe. A deadline is a kind of mercy. For the first time since 1899, I know exactly what I owe, and to whom, and by when. All my life I have carried debts with no numbers written on them. A debt without a number never lets you rest; you pay and pay and cannot tell when you have paid enough. Now the numbers have arrived, and the result — I did not expect this — is lightness. I walk the corridors lighter than I have walked them in decades. The portraits have noticed. Two of my predecessors have asked me, with some suspicion, what I am so pleased about.

I still teach, now and then, when a class can be borrowed. In April I taught first-years to turn a match into a needle, as I have in every decade of my life, and one small girl in the back row could not do it, and would not cry, and glared at that match as if it had insulted her family. I sat with her for twenty minutes. The needle, when it finally came, was crooked, and she looked at it the way I once looked at prizes. Whole ministries have given me less satisfaction. But I have made that point already; a dying man may be forgiven for repeating his favorite discovery.

Three debts remained. Three people. I have paid them in order of ease, because I am, to the very end, a coward about my debts.

Elphias first, because Elphias is easy. Easy the way bread is easy, or a warm room. I asked him to dinner in the winter, and we ate in my office, because his knees are old and the walk up from the gates is long. We talked for five hours. About Cairo. About dragons. About a hat he lost in Greece in 1901 and still mourns. We laughed until Fawkes woke up and complained.

In a hundred years my oldest friend has never once asked me about that summer. He knows there was a summer; everyone knows. He simply decided, long ago, that I would speak of it if I wished to, and that his work was to stand on either side of my silence and hold it steady. The finest conversation of my life has lasted a hundred years and has consisted, almost completely, of everything we did not discuss.

There is a lesson in Elphias that I was forty years slow to read. I collected brilliant people all my life, the way some men collect enemies, and the one who stood beside me at both funerals was the plain boy the school had laughed at. Cleverness is a lamp; it shows you everything in the room except the person holding it steady at your shoulder.

When he left, he shook my hand too long, the way he did in 1945. I believe he knows. I did not tell him, and he did not ask, and that too was the conversation.

The second debt cannot be paid while I live. I have accepted this, which is not the same as liking it.

I have written a letter to my brother. It sits in the left drawer of this desk, and it will be delivered to the Hog's Head when I am gone, by a hand I trust. It says what this whole book says, only shorter and without the fine sentences. It asks him for nothing — not forgiveness, not a visit to my grave, not one kind thought. Not even that.

I wrote fourteen drafts. Thirteen of them apologized. But an apology is a request; it asks the other person to do something with your guilt, to carry it back across the room to you, made lighter. Aberforth owes me no such service. I have said already what my brother does with apologies. The fourteenth draft only tells the truth and then stops, which is a thing I should have learned to do a lifetime ago.

He still does not know about my hand. One village away, twenty minutes' walk, and he will learn of my death the way strangers learn of it. I have had a year, and I have not walked those twenty minutes. I write it down plainly so that no one can ever say this book made me wiser than I am. Some doors I have never learned to knock on. His is the oldest of them.

Perhaps that is the honest shape of this whole book: a knock that a coward makes on every door at once, from a safe distance, in beautiful handwriting. I permit myself no illusion that it counts for more than it is. But the letter in the drawer is real, and the drawer will open, and he may do with the truth whatever he likes, including nothing. It is his either way. It was always his more than anyone's.

And the third debt.

The reports from the continent say that the prisoner of Nurmengard still lives. Top cell, an old man in the mountains, white-haired, thin, guarded by his own walls. They say he talks to himself up there. I understand that better than the guards do. The self he talks to is very probably me. Ours was a conversation that neither of us ever found another partner for, and we have both spent fifty years finishing each other's sentences across a continent, in silence.

All my life I told myself there was nothing I could write to him that would not be either a politics or a wound. That was false, of course. There was one page to write. I had simply not finished becoming the man who could write it. It took the ring to finish me — the stone, the black hand, the year with a number on it. Fear does not leave a man because he grows wise; I grew as wise as anyone alive and kept every fear I started with. It leaves when something larger arrives and asks for the room.

In March I did the thing I had not done in my whole long life. I wrote to him, and I sent it. One page. No politics, no terms, no wisdom. I copy it here because this book has earned the right to hold it:

> Gellert —
>
> No politics. I have given the world enough of our politics.
>
> I write to say one thing. I have stopped being afraid of the sentence — you know the one. Whichever of the three of us it was, she was in that room because of all three of us, and I was the eldest. I have carried it for almost a hundred years under other names: work, wisdom, patience. I am putting the names down now.
>
> Aberforth keeps goats still, and a bar that smells of them. I have come to think it is the best smell in Britain. You would laugh. You always laughed at true things.
>
> It was the best summer of my life. I am no longer ashamed to say so, and I no longer need to separate what you were from what you did. I am too old to carry two of you.
>
> Albus

The owl left in the last week of March. No answer has come.

I state this without bitterness, because I feel none. Owls to Nurmengard are slow; the mountains are high, and the guards read everything twice. Or he is composing. He always polished his best letters — sometimes for days, while I stood at my attic window watching for the light in his. He never sent a sentence he could improve. I can wait. Though I should not wait too long.

I do not know what he will do with my page. Burn it, perhaps; we were always a family of burners, he and I — the unopened letter, the list of wise measures, forty years of drafts. Or he will keep it under his pillow of stone. I find both thoughts bearable, which is how I know the letter was true. A true letter is finished when it is sent. Everything after belongs to the other person, and I have finally learned to leave other people their share.

It is night now, the deep blue hour when the castle finally holds still. The instruments on their thin legs tick and turn and breathe their small silver breath, keeping watch over things I will not name here. Fawkes sits on his perch with his head half under his wing, one dark eye open, pretending to sleep the way he has pretended for a hundred years. The quill is warm in my left hand. The right one lies on the desk, black and quiet, like something already buried.

The confession is nearly done. A few pages, no more, and then there is nothing left to confess.

And I find, to my surprise, that I am afraid of finishing it. Not of death — we have an understanding, death and I, and the terms are fair. Afraid of the last page. While I am writing, they are all alive in the room with me: my mother pours the tea, my father laughs with his wand behind his ear, Aberforth's nose is unbroken, a golden boy climbs the garden wall instead of using the gate, and Ariana hums. Every night I have sat down at this desk and raised the dead, which is the one magic I spent my whole life wanting, and it was here all along, in a bottle of ink. When I put the quill down, they will lie still again.

Every storyteller knows this fear, I suppose. It is why the old stories are so long.

But a debt with a number on it must be paid on time. One more chapter. Then I will let them sleep.

## 36. The Greater Good

June, and the castle is emptying for the summer. Trunks bump down the staircases; owls circle the towers, full of importance; somewhere below my window a boy is shouting about a lost toad, and it is, I promise you, the same toad that has been lost every June since 1892. The year I was given is nearly spent. I do not know how much remains — weeks, perhaps days. The hand has stopped telling me its schedule, and I have stopped asking. It is pleasant weather to be mortal in.

The book is done, except for this. I want to end where I began: with three words.

For the greater good. I wrote them at three in the morning when I was seventeen, in my best handwriting, to impress a boy across the lane. I have seen them carved in stone over a prison gate. I have spent a hundred years weighing them, and now, in public, on the page, I will weigh them one last time and be finished. Words are the only things I was ever given without asking for them. It seems right that the accounting should end with words.

*For.* The smallest word, and the guiltiest. Things done for people are so very often done to them. Nobody asked the Muggles whether they wished to be ruled for their own good. Nobody asked Ariana whether she wished to be an argument. When someone announces that a thing is being done for you, it is wise to ask whether you were consulted. My sister was never consulted about anything in her short life. She was carried along inside other people's sentences — mine first of all, and mine the longest.

*Greater.* Greater than what? That was always the question, and I never let myself ask it. Greater meant: bigger than the person in front of you. Bigger than a frightened brother, bigger than a tired mother, bigger than a girl on a sitting-room floor. It is a word for looking over people's heads. Every plan I have ever had cause to regret began with me looking over someone's head, toward a horizon where the good was supposed to live. The good has never once been found at the horizon. I have checked. I had a long life, and I checked thoroughly.

*Good.* Some good survived me, I think, though never the good I planned. A school kept warm and strange and open. A number of confused children caught at the door, one at a time, before they became what I almost became. A lie about socks, told kindly. Not one of these is great. That is precisely what is good about them.

So here is my amended version, arrived at after a lifetime of study, small enough at last to fit inside one human life. Not the greater good. The good at hand. The child in front of you. The warm milk. The letter actually sent. The twenty minutes actually walked — well. The good at hand, done with your own two hands, while they are still your own.

My amended version does not scale, as the arithmeticians say. It will never light a continent or fill a square with flags. That is its entire virtue. Evil scales beautifully — I have watched it done, twice, by two of the most gifted men of the century, one of whom I loved. Love does not scale. It goes one person at a time, on foot, carrying milk.

Aberforth had it right the whole time, which is the joke of my biography. I searched the libraries of Europe, I exchanged letters with the finest minds of the age, I stood in the mirror of the century and dueled my own reflection — and the answer was in a goat shed one village away. Warm milk and a silly song, given to one person, on time, every day. My brother is not a clever man. He is merely right, which I have come to understand is the rarer gift.

Ariana. I have written about you for a whole book, and never once to you.

So: your brother keeps your picture over his bar, above the bottles, where the lamplight reaches it. You are laughing in it. I made this entire book out of the fact that I still remember what your laugh sounded like — like water starting to boil, our mother said, and you laughed harder, and that was the kitchen, and that was us. I ask you for nothing. I only wanted you, once, to be spoken to instead of about.

There are nine notebooks at my elbow now, tied with ordinary string. I have not decided who is to read them — my brother, or a green-eyed boy, or an old man in a mountain cell, or no one at all. The question matters less than I expected. A confession, I have discovered, is not a delivery. The truth was in me like the curse in the ring, and writing it out was the only surgery I could perform on myself. The reader was never the point. The secret was the point, and the secret is gone, and I am lighter by exactly its weight.

And now the last page, and I want to be exact on it, because exactness is all I have left to give.

I am not afraid. This is not courage; I have never been sure I had any — the hat took a long time over me, and I have always suspected it was being polite. It is something simpler. The people I have loved best are all on the other side of the glass. They have been there my whole life. A garden hedge, a kitchen window, a mirror in an unused room, the cold shine of a stone in a cracked ring — I have spent a hundred years standing at one glass or another with my hand against it, looking at the lit room I could not enter. Soon, quite soon, someone will open it at last, from their side, the way a door is opened for a guest who has stood too long in the rain. I do not know what comes after. No one does; I have read everything, and no one does. But I no longer believe it will be dark.

Fawkes is awake. He has been watching me write these last lines with one dark eye, and now, very quietly, he has begun to sing. Phoenix song does not sound like grief. It sounds like the thing grief is guarding. I will not try to describe it further; some things are wasted on ink.

The candle on my desk is nearly finished, and so is the notebook. In a moment I will close it — one black hand, one live one, folding a hundred years flat — and then I will sit a while with the singing, and then I will go down to breakfast, because that also is the good at hand.

Outside the window, the sky is beginning to turn gold. There is, at the very end, exactly enough light to read by.

## Как читать эту книгу

Книга написана простым английским (B1 → лёгкий B2), но это настоящая проза, а не учебник. Несколько советов:

- **Не переводи каждое слово.** Сначала прочитай абзац целиком. Обычно смысл понятен из истории. Тапни по слову, только если без него никак.
- **Повторы — не случайность.** Ключевые слова (*guilt, power, love, fear, silence, forgive, remember, ashamed*) возвращаются из главы в главу. К концу книги ты будешь знать их навсегда.
- **Врезка «New Words»** в конце главы — самые важные слова главы. Просмотри их до или после чтения.
- **Кнопка перевода у абзаца** покажет русский перевод целиком. Полезно проверить себя после сложного абзаца.

## Мир этой книги: краткая справка

Эта история — фанфик по миру Джоан Роулинг. Она опирается только на книги (прежде всего «Дары Смерти»), а не на фильмы «Фантастические твари». События — с 1880-х по 1997 год.

- **Godric's Hollow (Годрикова Впадина)** — деревня, где живут волшебники и маглы. Здесь прошла юность Дамблдора, здесь похоронена его семья — и здесь через сто лет погибнут родители Гарри.
- **The Deathly Hallows (Дары Смерти)** — три легендарных предмета из сказки о трёх братьях: **Elder Wand (Бузинная палочка)** — непобедимая палочка, **Resurrection Stone (Воскрешающий камень)** — камень, возвращающий мёртвых, **Invisibility Cloak (Мантия-невидимка)**. Собравший все три — «повелитель смерти».
- **«For the Greater Good» («Ради общего блага»)** — лозунг революции Гриндевальда. Эти слова придумал не он.
- **Nurmengard (Нурменгард)** — тюрьма, которую Гриндевальд построил для своих врагов и в которой закончил сам.
- **The Mirror of Erised (зеркало Еиналеж)** — волшебное зеркало, показывающее самое глубокое желание сердца. Люди сходили перед ним с ума.
- **Azkaban (Азкабан)** — тюрьма волшебников на острове в Северном море. Там умер отец Дамблдора.
- **Muggle (магл)** — человек без магии. **Squib (сквиб)** — ребёнок волшебников, родившийся без магии.
- **Statute of Secrecy (Статут о секретности)** — закон, по которому волшебники прячутся от маглов. Юные Альбус и Геллерт мечтали его отменить.
- **Hogwarts (Хогвартс)** — школа магии, в которой Дамблдор провёл почти всю жизнь: учеником, учителем, директором.
- **Horcrux (крестраж)** — тёмный предмет, в котором волшебник прячет часть своей души. В книге упоминается один: кольцо с камнем.

О любви Дамблдора к Гриндевальду Роулинг сказала прямо, о причине сорокалетнего молчания — устами самого Дамблдора на вокзале Кингс-Кросс. Всё остальное между этими фактами — попытка честно представить, как такая жизнь выглядела изнутри.
