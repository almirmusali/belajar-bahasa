# THE FOUNDERS

### The story of four wizards who built a school — English for intermediate readers

---

## Читателю

Тысячу лет назад четыре волшебника построили замок на скале у чёрного озера. Вы знаете, что было потом: письма с совой, поезд с платформы, мальчик со шрамом. Об этом написаны семь книг.

Но о самих четверых не написано почти ничего. Годрик, Салазар, Ровена, Хельга — от них остались только легенды: сон про бородавчатую свинью, меч, диадема, чаша и запертая комната под замком. Известно главное: двое из них были лучшими друзьями — и поссорились так, что эхо той ссоры гремело ещё тысячу лет. Роулинг оставила этот лист почти чистым. Эта книга — попытка его заполнить.

Рассказчик здесь необычный: история рассказана Распределяющей шляпой. Сначала она была просто шляпой на голове Годрика — и молча видела всё. Потом четверо вложили в неё свой разум, и она заговорила. Она единственная, кто знал всех четверых изнутри. Ей и слово.

**Как читать эту книгу.** Правил нет — это роман, а не учебник. Просто читайте историю.

Читайте абзацами, а не словами. Наткнулись на незнакомое слово — идите дальше: смысл чаще всего догоняет через строчку. Если слово не отпускает — коснитесь его, появится перевод. Переведено каждое слово книги, застрять невозможно.

Кнопка перевода рядом с абзацем — запасной выход. Жмите её, когда совсем потеряли нить, и двигайтесь дальше. Главные слова книги — *brave, cunning, wise, loyal, trust, secret, choice* — вернутся к вам десятки раз, и к середине вы заметите, что читаете их не переводя.

Уровень — средний (B1): предложения простые, но это настоящая английская проза, и к концу книга незаметно растёт вместе с вами. Одна-две главы за вечер — лучший темп: закладка запомнит место, полоска прогресса покажет, сколько позади.

Открывайте первую главу. Шляпа уже прокашлялась.

## People in this Story

**Godric Gryffindor** — a big, loud wizard from the moors in the south-west of England. The bravest man of his time, and maybe the most reckless. He wore an old pointed hat. Remember that hat.

**Salazar Slytherin** — a thin, quiet wizard from the fens, the wet lands in the east. The cleverest potion-maker in Britain. He could speak to snakes. He was Godric's best friend. Yes — best friend.

**Rowena Ravenclaw** — a witch from a glen in the mountains of Scotland. People said she was the most intelligent witch of her age. She made a diadem — a small silver crown — with old words on it about wisdom.

**Helga Hufflepuff** — a healer from a green valley in Wales. She believed that food, work and kindness can repair almost anything. Her golden cup later became famous. She was the heart of the four.

**Edith** — a girl from a mill village, born to Muggle parents. The first student of her kind at the school.

**Helena Ravenclaw** — Rowena's daughter, born in the castle. Clever like her mother, and unhappy in her shadow.

**The Baron** — a young noble from Salazar's house, quick to anger, in love with Helena.

**Ragnuk the First** — the king of the goblins, the greatest metal-maker of his time. He made a sword. Who owned that sword — well, that depends on who tells the story.

**And me** — the Sorting Hat. For many years I was only a hat on Godric's head. I saw everything and could say nothing. Then, one evening, four wizards gave me a mind and a voice. I am the only one left who knew them all. So the story is mine to tell.

## Part One: Four Wizards

*Britain, near the end of the tenth century. Before the castle, before the houses, before the name. Four people who had not yet met.*

---

## 1. The Hat Remembers

I am only a hat.

I want to say that at the start, because people forget it. Every year they put me on the heads of frightened children, and I look inside those heads, and I say a word, and the word decides where the child will sleep, who will become their friends, what colours they will wear, and — sometimes — what kind of person they will grow into. That is a lot of power for a hat. So let me be honest with you from the first page: I did not always talk. I did not always think. For many years I was an ordinary pointed hat, brown and ugly, and I sat on the head of a big red-haired man, and I saw everything, and I could say nothing.

The man's name was Godric Gryffindor.

You know that name. You know all four names — Gryffindor, Slytherin, Ravenclaw, Hufflepuff — because a thousand years later, children still wear those names on their chests like small flags. But you know the names the way you know the names of mountains. You do not know the people. You do not know that Godric laughed too loudly and cried easily. You do not know that Salazar Slytherin, whose name later became a cold word, once carried a sick village boy on his back through the rain for six hours. You do not know that Rowena Ravenclaw was afraid of exactly one thing, or that Helga Hufflepuff, the kindest of the four, was the only one who never lost an argument.

I knew them. I am the only one left who did.

So this is their story. I will tell it slowly, because it deserves that, and I will tell it honestly, which is harder. Honestly means I must tell you about the bad days as well as the good ones. It means the story ends with a broken friendship, an empty chair at the table, and a locked door deep under the castle. If you want a story where nobody makes mistakes, close me now. This is not that story. This is a true one — or as true as a hat can make it.

Let me begin with the world, because you will not understand the four without it.

---

Britain, one thousand years ago, was a dangerous place to be magical.

There were no laws to protect wizards, because there was no wizard government. There was no Ministry of Magic. There was no secret world with its own shops and songs and newspapers. There were only scattered magical families, living among Muggles — that is our old word for people without magic — and trying very hard not to be noticed.

Some Muggles feared magic. Some hated it. Most, to be fair, simply did not believe in it, and that was the safest kind of Muggle. But belief moves like weather. A bad harvest, a sick cow, a child with a strange fever — and suddenly the village needed someone to blame. Then old neighbours looked at the quiet woman who lived alone with her herbs, or at the boy whose broken arm healed too fast, and the looking became talking, and the talking became shouting, and sometimes the shouting became fire.

I will not pretend it was fire every day. It was not. There were villages where a wise woman with a talent for healing was loved her whole life and buried with honour. There were Muggle farmers who quietly left bread at the door of the local witch, the way you leave milk for a cat. The world was not simple, and I promised to be honest. But every magical family in Britain knew at least one story with fire in it. Every magical parent, holding a new baby, felt two feelings at once: joy, and fear.

And here was the worst part. Magic does not wait for permission.

A magical child is like a kettle. The magic builds and builds, and if nobody teaches the child what to do with it, it comes out by itself — in bursts, in accidents. A frightened seven-year-old turns the dog blue. An angry ten-year-old breaks every window in the street without touching them. In our time, such a child gets a letter, a school, a wand, and seven years of patient teaching. In those days, the child got nothing. If the family was magical, the parents taught what they could, in secret, in the kitchen. If the family was Muggle — if the magic appeared, as it sometimes does, in a house where nobody had ever seen it before — the child had no one at all. Just the fear of their own hands.

Wizard children learned at home, or they did not learn. And a wizard who never learns is not a free, happy person with a wonderful secret. He is a kettle with the lid tied down.

Remember this. Everything that happens in this book — the dream, the castle, the school, and also the argument that broke it — grew out of this one fact: it was a bad time to be a magical child, and four people decided to do something about it.

---

They were very different people. That was the miracle, and later it was the problem.

If you had met them one by one, in their separate corners of Britain, you would never have guessed they belonged together. A loud swordsman from the southern moors, who ran toward trouble the way other people run from it. A pale, careful man from the eastern fens, who spoke to snakes more easily than to strangers. A Scottish witch in a stone tower, who thought most people were fools and had good evidence. A Welsh healer with flour on her hands, who fed every hungry creature that knocked on her door, and some that did not knock.

Four corners of the island. Four kinds of cleverness. Four different answers to the same question: *how do you live bravely in a world that wants you quiet?*

Godric's answer was: openly, sword first, and let them try.

Salazar's answer was: hidden, always hidden, because the fire is real.

Rowena's answer was: above it all, behind high walls, with a book.

Helga's answer was: together, around a table, because nobody survives alone.

None of these answers was wrong. I have had a thousand years to think about it, and I tell you: none of them was wrong. They were four pieces of one answer. For about twenty years, the pieces held together, and in those years the four of them built the only truly safe place for magical children in the world. The castle still stands. You may have seen it — from a boat, on a dark lake, with all its windows burning like gold.

Then the pieces came apart. That is in this book too.

---

People sometimes ask me — students, mostly, at night, when the common rooms are quiet and someone sneaks up to the office where I sit — they ask: "Hat, which of them was the best?"

They expect me to say Godric, because I was his hat. And oh, I loved Godric. When he laughed, the table shook. When he was ashamed, he said so, in front of everyone, which is a rarer kind of courage than the sword kind. But "best" is a child's question, and I am very old, so I answer it the old way: with a story.

The story I am about to tell you has no villain. I know how it looks from a thousand years away — one of the four walked out, and his name became dark, and children in green are still asked to answer for it. The truth is heavier and stranger. The man who left loved the school as much as the others. Maybe more. He built its deepest walls with his own hands. What broke was not his love. What broke was something between four friends who each held one piece of the answer and, at the worst moment, could not put the pieces together.

I saw it break. I sat on Godric's head at that last council, and I felt his thoughts under me like a storm under a roof, and I could do nothing, because I was only a hat, and hats do not speak.

Later — much later — they gave me a voice. You will read about that evening; it is my favourite chapter and I wrote it with great care. Since that evening I have had one job: every year, I look into the heads of children and find which piece of the answer shines brightest in each of them. Courage. Cunning. Wisdom. Loyalty. I have done this job for a thousand years, and every single year, some child sits under me and silently thinks the question they all think:

*Which one am I?*

Little friend, hold that question. Do not let anyone answer it for you too quickly — not your family, not your friends, not even a talking hat. First meet the four. Walk with Godric across the moor, sit with Salazar by the grey water, climb Rowena's tower, warm your hands in Helga's kitchen. See what each of them paid for their piece of the answer.

Then decide whose table you would have chosen.

We begin, as the best stories do, with a boy in the middle of nowhere.
## 2. The Boy from the Moor

In the south-west of England there is a wide, empty, beautiful land where the wind never fully stops. Muggles call such land a moor. It is all grass and stone and low hills, with small villages hiding in the folds between them, and in one of those folds, a thousand years ago, there was a village so small it did not have a proper name. People simply called it the Hollow.

Later, of course, it got a name. It is on maps now: Godric's Hollow. Villages, like hats, are sometimes lucky about the people they belong to.

Godric was born there in a long, low house at the edge of the village, the fourth child of a magical family that had lived on the moor for so many generations that nobody remembered arriving. His father kept sheep — with a little quiet magic, the way a wizard farmer does, so the wolves stayed confused and the rain mostly fell on the neighbour's field first. His mother was the person the village came to at night, when a child had a fever that ordinary medicine could not touch. The Muggle neighbours did not use the word "witch" about her. They used the word "blessed", and brought her eggs.

I tell you this because people imagine Godric's childhood full of swords and glory. It was not. It was full of sheep.

He was a big child, then a bigger boy, then an enormous young man, with red hair that never obeyed and a laugh you could hear across two hills. He had, from the beginning, the quality that later got a whole house named after him — but let me describe it carefully, because a thousand years of songs have polished it wrong.

Courage, in the songs, is not being afraid. That is nonsense, and Godric himself said so, often, to anyone who would listen. Godric was afraid of plenty of things. Deep water. His mother's silence when she was disappointed. Later, one particular friend's smile when the smile stopped being real. No — what Godric had was different. When something frightening appeared, there was a small, sharp moment — you know it, everyone knows it — the moment when your body asks: *towards or away?* And Godric's body, every single time, without discussion, answered: *towards.*

The first time it mattered, he was eleven.

A pedlar came through the Hollow that autumn — a travelling seller with a bent back, ribbons and needles and knives on his cart. Behind the cart, on a rope, walked a dog. It was a big grey dog with clever eyes, and it was limping, and the pedlar beat it when it slowed.

The village watched and said nothing, because the pedlar was a stranger and strangers move on. Godric watched, and the little question came — *towards or away?* — and his feet were already moving. He planted himself in front of the cart, eleven years old, round-faced, furious, and said the dog was hurt and the man was cruel and the rope should be cut.

The pedlar laughed at him. Then the pedlar raised his stick — at the boy, this time.

What happened next was told in the Hollow for thirty years. The stick jumped out of the man's hand. It rose into the air, politely, like a trained bird, and it began to beat the pedlar around the head and shoulders — not hard, but steadily — and it chased him, hopping and swearing, all the way to the edge of the village and over the hill.

Wild magic. The kettle, boiling over. Godric had not planned it and could not have repeated it. He stood in the road, shaking, amazed at himself, while the grey dog looked up at him with its clever eyes. His mother came running, and Godric prepared for trouble.

Instead she knelt in the mud and held his face in her two hands, and he never forgot what she said. She said: "Listen to me. That was well meant and badly done. If any man tells the wrong people what he saw today, it is not you who burns. It is me, because they always blame the mother. Magic is not a sword, Godric. Magic is a fire in your own house. You must learn it, or it will use you."

"Then teach me," said Godric.

And she did — she and his father and his grandmother, in the kitchen, after dark, for seven years: the family spells, the moor spells, the healing words, the hiding words. It was a good education, as home educations went. Godric was quick, though never patient. But the lesson he carried longest was not a spell. It was that sentence: *it is not you who burns.* Courage that only risks yourself is cheap, his mother meant. Real choices spend other people's safety too. Godric spent his whole life arguing with that sentence — loving it, hating it, and in the end building a castle because of it.

The dog stayed, by the way. Fourteen years. Godric named him Bran, and when much later a first-year boy named Brann came to our school, Godric chose him for his own table partly because of the name. Founders are not fair. Nobody is.

---

At eighteen, Godric left the Hollow.

Not because anything was wrong. Because the moor was a fold in the hills, and he could feel the size of the world beyond it the way you feel weather coming. Magical Britain in those days had no schools, but it had a kind of hidden road: a network of magical families, safe houses, fair-days and gathering places, where a young wizard could travel, work, learn a spell here and a trick there, and test himself against strangers. Duelling was the favourite sport of that road — formal duelling, wand against wand, with rules and judges and prizes.

Godric took to duelling the way rain takes to falling.

Within three years, people on the hidden road knew the name Gryffindor. Within five, they were inventing stories about him. Some were even true. He really did once fight three brothers in a row and buy them all supper afterwards. He really did refuse a rich man's gold to hex a rival, and then hex the rich man for asking. He really was, at twenty-three, probably the finest duellist in the south of Britain — and here I must stop and correct the songs again, because the songs make it sound like talent, and it was not talent.

I sat on his head for years. I felt how he worked. Godric fought the way he did because of the little question. In a duel, there is a moment — a spell flashing toward your face — when every mind on earth flinches away. His did not. It leaned in. While your mind flinched, his was already answering. That was the entire secret. Bravery, drilled and repeated and sharpened until it looked like magic beyond other people's magic.

It was on that road, at a horse-fair near the sea, that he bought me.

I feel you smiling. Yes — the famous Sorting Hat, bought at a fair, from a hatmaker's stall between a cheese cart and a man selling love-charms that did not work. I was new then: a good, honest, pointed hat, wizard-made, charmed to shed rain and stay on in a wind. Godric had just won a duelling purse and was in the mood to be splendid. He tried me on. I fitted. He laughed at himself in the hatmaker's little mirror, paid twice the asking price, and walked away wearing me.

I want to record something, hat to reader, and you may believe it or not: I remember that first hour on his head. Hats do not think, and I did not think, but heat is real even before there is anyone home to feel it — and that head was *warm*. Thoughts moved under me all day like horses under a blanket. For the next thirty years I went where that head went. Every duel, every rain, every argument, every mistake. When the four founders later needed a place to put their minds, they did not choose me because I was clever. They chose me because I had been there.

But that evening at the fair, none of it had happened yet. That evening there was only a young man in a new hat, walking through the crowd with a duelling purse on his belt, entirely pleased with the world.

And at the far end of the fair, at a quiet table, a thin pale young man from the fens sat watching the crowd with grey-green eyes, and noticed the loud red-haired duellist everyone was talking about, and thought — I learned this from him much later, in his own words —

*That one is either the most honest man in Britain, or the best liar I have ever seen. I should find out which.*

His name was Salazar. The next chapter is his, and I ask you to walk into it slowly and leave your thousand years of opinions at the door. You have heard his name used as a curse. Fine. Curses are easy. Understanding is the hard sport.

He was twenty-four that evening, he had buried more family than most old men, and he was about to meet the best friend of his life.
## 3. The Man from the Fens

Now travel east with me, across the whole width of England, to a country that is the opposite of a moor.

The fens were flat, wet lands near the eastern sea — half earth, half water, and never fully either. Rivers wandered there without deciding where to go. Reeds grew taller than a man. Paths existed in summer and vanished in winter, and a stranger who walked in without a guide might walk in circles until the fog ate him. Muggles at that time avoided the deep fens and told stories about them: lights that moved over the water, voices in the reeds, islands that were there on Tuesday and gone on Thursday.

Some of those stories were true. The deep fens were wizard country.

Magical families had settled there centuries before, precisely because the land defended them. You cannot burn what you cannot find. On the hidden islands among the reeds stood houses on wooden legs, gardens of strange herbs, boats that needed no oars. It was a closed, careful, quiet world — a world that watched the shore the way a cat watches a dog through a fence.

Salazar was born on one of those islands, in the oldest house in the fens.

The Slytherin family was ancient even then, and proud of it in the particular way of families who have little else left. Once, said the family stories, they had advised kings. Now they kept ancient books, ancient silver — including a locket with a serpent on it, which matters later in this story, so remember it — and one inherited gift, rarer than gold: the snake-tongue. Every generation or two, a Slytherin child was born who could speak to snakes.

Salazar was such a child. Before he could properly talk to people, he was talking to the grass-snakes on the warm stones by the water. His mother found him there one morning, three years old, hissing gently, with a snake raised up before him like a listening rope. She was not alarmed. She was proud. In the fens, the snake-tongue was a blessing, a sign of the old blood running true.

Hold that picture for a moment: a small boy in the reeds, talking to a snake, entirely happy. Whatever you have heard about the man, the boy starts there.

Snakes, by the way, are honest company. People are surprised when I say that. But a snake never pretends. It tells you plainly: I am cold, I am hungry, I am afraid, leave me, stay. Salazar told me once — much later, in the castle, on a night when he was sad and unusually willing to talk — that he learned to trust snakes years before he learned to trust people, and that the reason was exactly that plainness. "A snake has never once lied to me," he said. "Men lie twice before breakfast." I recorded the sentence. Hats have long memories, and that sentence explains half his life.

He grew up thin, quick, watchful, and clever — but clever is too small a word. Salazar had the kind of mind that saw systems: how water moves, how sickness spreads, how fear travels through a village faster than any illness. By fifteen he brewed potions better than the fen elders. By twenty he had invented remedies they still use — though the books no longer print his name beside them, which is one of the small quiet crimes of history.

And he would have become, I believe, simply the greatest healer of his age — if not for Mara.

---

Mara was his younger sister. Eleven years old the year it happened. Bright, laughing, fearless in the water — she swam like an otter before she could walk, people said. Of all the family, she was the one who loved the shore. The fens were safe, but the fens were closed, and Mara was not a closed person. There was a Muggle village at the edge of the wetlands, a market village, and she loved its noise: the stalls, the music, the children her own age. The family allowed it, carefully. Times were calm just then. The village knew the fen folk a little — quiet neighbours who paid in good coin and sold remedies that worked. There were Muggle children Mara called friends. There was a baker's wife who saved sweet buns for her.

You already feel it coming. I am sorry. I promised an honest book.

That summer, a sickness came to the village — a fever that took children. It was an ordinary fever; such things came and went in those centuries like rain. But fear needs a story, and a travelling preacher gave them one: the sickness was a punishment, and its cause was witchcraft, and had anyone seen anything strange?

Someone had. Someone always has. A girl from the marshes had been seen — on the riverbank, in the evening — talking to the water. Leaning over the river and whispering to it, and the river whispering back.

She had been talking to a grass-snake swimming below the bank. That is all. A child, chatting with a snake on a summer evening, in the language her blood had carried for a thousand years.

They took her at the market, quickly, before her people could know. There was a trial that was not a trial. The test for a water-witch, in that place, in that year, was the oldest and stupidest of all: the river decides. If the water rejects the witch, she floats. If she is innocent, the water takes her.

The water took her.

Her Muggle friends stood in the crowd. The children she played with. The baker's wife with the sweet buns. Not one voice said stop. I do not tell you this to teach you to hate that village — the village was drowning in its own fear, and fear is a sickness with no potion. I tell you because you must stand where Salazar stood to understand every single thing he does later in this book. He was fifteen. He reached the shore at dusk, having run and poled and swum from the deep fens when word finally came, and the market square was empty, and the river was flat and polite as glass.

The family did nothing. That must also be said. The old law of the fens was absolute: never strike the shore, whatever the shore does, because open war between wizard and Muggle has one ending — there are few of us and many of them. His father, grey-faced, said the law out loud that night, over Mara's empty chair. Salazar never forgave the men who drowned his sister. But listen, because this is the exact shape of his wound: he never fully forgave his own family either, for being wise, and careful, and right, and doing nothing.

Grief becomes belief. It happened slowly, over years, the way water shapes stone. By twenty-four, Salazar carried three certainties the way other men carry knives. One: the Muggle world will always, in the end, turn on us — not because Muggles are evil, but because fear is stronger than friendship, and he had seen the proof from the front row. Two: therefore, hiding is not cowardice; hiding is the first duty of anyone who loves their family. Three — and this one grew the thorns that tear through this whole book — every door between our world and theirs is a crack for the fear to come through, and doors must be counted, and watched, and, if needed, closed.

Keep those three certainties in your pocket as you read. They are wrong and right in a tangle that took me a thousand years to unpick — I will offer you my answer at the very end of the book, for what a hat's answer is worth. But they were not made of hate. They were made of a flat polite river and an empty chair.

---

Why, then, did such a man ever leave the fens?

Because of the fourth thing in him, the one nobody puts in the songs: Salazar was ambitious. He wanted to matter. The fens were safe and small, and his mind was huge and restless, and remedies for fen-fever were not going to be his whole life. He began to travel the hidden road — carefully, hooded, watchful — trading potions, collecting rare knowledge, listening at the edges of other wizards' fires. He was gathering, though he did not yet have the word for it, everything a school would one day need.

And so, one autumn evening, at a horse-fair near the southern sea, he sat at a quiet table with a cup he had barely touched, watching a crowd he did not trust, and noticed a big red-haired young man laughing in the middle of it — loud, open, glowing, wearing a brand-new pointed hat and spending his winnings on strangers.

Salazar watched him the way he watched everything: as a puzzle. *Either the most honest man in Britain, or the best liar I have ever seen.*

There was one way to find out, and it was the accepted way of the hidden road. The next morning, when the duelling ground opened, a thin pale stranger from the fens put down his name against the famous Gryffindor.

I was on Godric's head when the lists were read. I felt his thoughts jump — not with worry. With delight.

That duel deserves its own telling, and it opens the next chapter: the two best young wizards in Britain, meeting for the first time with wands drawn. The judges called it the finest duel seen on the southern road in forty years. It lasted almost an hour. It ended in a way nobody expected — including, for once in his careful life, Salazar.

But before you turn the page, I want to fix one picture in your mind, because the whole tragedy of this book lives inside it. Two young men facing each other across the grass. One learned as a child: *when the fire comes, stand and fight, towards, always towards.* The other learned, by a flat polite river: *when the fire comes, it comes from the people who smiled at you, and no one will save you but your own walls.*

They are about to become the closest friends either of them will ever have.

They are also, though neither can see it, already standing on the opposite banks of the river that will one day divide them — with a castle, a school, and a thousand years of children caught in between.
## 4. The Lady of the Glen

The duel, then. I promised it, and a hat keeps its word.

They met on trodden grass at the edge of the fair, in the grey light just after sunrise, with a rope around the ground and half the hidden road leaning on it. Duels on the road had rules: no killing spells, no touching the other's body with your hands, the fight ends when a wand leaves its owner's hold or a duellist yields. Within those rules, everything.

Godric fought the way I told you: leaning in, always half a heartbeat ahead of fear. Salazar fought like the fens. That is the only way to describe it. Nothing came at you straight. His spells curved, waited, doubled back; he made mist out of dry air; twice Godric's best strikes hit a Salazar who turned out to be reeds and river-light. An hour, the judges said later, though nobody was checking the sun. The crowd went silent in the way crowds do when they understand they are watching a story that will be told without them.

It ended like this: Godric finally read the pattern — there was always a pattern with Salazar, that was his one weakness, he was too intelligent to be truly random — broke through the mist, and disarmed him; and in the same second the ground under Godric, which had quietly become marsh while he was busy winning, swallowed him to the chest. The judges stared. One wand in Godric's fist, both men helpless, and a long, long pause.

Then Godric began to laugh. Stuck in magical mud in front of five hundred people, holding his opponent's wand, laughing until his eyes ran. And he called out, for the whole fair to hear: "A draw! I claim a draw and I claim supper with this man, because I have fought every wand in the south and nobody ever made the *ground* betray me!"

I felt the laugh from the inside, you understand. The whole head rang with it. And I felt what happened when Salazar, dripping and empty-handed, walked over, looked down at him — that careful pale face working through surprise, suspicion, arithmetic — and then, slowly, smiled a real smile, possibly his first real smile in years, and reached down his hand to pull the winner out of the mud.

*The most honest man in Britain*, then. Question answered.

They ate supper together that night, and breakfast the next morning, and rode out of the fair side by side. For the next four years the hidden road spoke of them in one breath: Gryffindor and Slytherin, the sword and the serpent, the loud one and the quiet one. Salazar's cunning kept Godric alive — I can count five separate ambushes those grey-green eyes saw before anyone else. Godric's warmth thawed Salazar; around the fire he began, cautiously, like a man testing river ice, to be young. They argued constantly and enjoyed it. Neither had had a brother. Now each did.

I would happily give you a whole book of those four years. But this story is bigger than two men, and while they rode the southern roads, its other half was already alive in the north. So come — it is time you climbed the glen.

---

Scotland, then as now, was a country of mountains and weather with some land attached. In a fold of the eastern Highlands there was a glen — a steep green valley with a fast cold river at the bottom — that Muggles somehow never entered. They did not avoid it; they simply never noticed it was there. Their paths bent around it, their maps forgot it, and if you asked a shepherd what lay beyond that ridge, his face went pleasantly empty and he began talking about sheep.

That was not an accident. That was the work of generations of one family: the family of the glen, whom the road called Ravenclaw.

At the head of the glen, on a shoulder of rock above a black loch — hold that loch in your mind; you will see it again with a castle over it — stood a tall narrow tower. In that tower lived Rowena.

By the time our story finds her she was near thirty, mistress of the tower after her mother's death, and already something between a person and a legend. The road had a saying — Salazar brought it south, having heard it in trade: *ask the glen*. It meant: when a question has beaten everyone, when the wise-folk shrug, when the book is missing a page — someone would say, half joking, "ask the glen," and someone, eventually, desperate enough, would actually make the long journey north, and climb the valley, and put their question to the witch in the tower.

She usually answered it. That was the legend part. Here is the person part.

Rowena had been a child who asked *why* the way other children breathe. Not sometimes. Always. Why does the river never freeze at the bend? Why does iron spoil some spells and not others? Why do Muggle paths bend around us — what exactly bends them? Her mother, a formidable witch with a tired sense of humour, answered the first thousand questions, taught her letters early in self-defence, and then handed her the family library — twelve generations of scrolls and stones and star-tables gathered in the tower's round rooms.

It was not enough. Nothing was ever quite enough. By twenty, Rowena had read everything in the glen and begun writing what the library lacked. She invented spells the way Helga — patience, we meet her next chapter — invented recipes: daily, casually, because the existing ones annoyed her. Some of her inventions you have used. The charm that makes a small bag deep enough for a tent? The glen. The spell that lets a map show only what its owner should see? The glen. She kept notebooks in a code of her own design, in ink of her own design, and once, when a scroll thief spent a whole winter copying her code book by candlelight, it turned out the code book was itself a trap, and the thief spent the next month unable to say anything except the word "moist". She let him recover eventually. Mostly.

You laugh, and you should — but I must show you the other side of the tower, because it is the sad engine of half this story.

Cleverness is lonely.

Not always, not for everyone — Salazar's cleverness found company in snakes and, later, in Godric. But Rowena's kind, the towering kind, the kind that finishes your sentence before you have chosen it, builds a particular wall around a person. All her life, conversations bored her before they began. She could see the ends of things from their beginnings: of arguments, of friendships, of a shepherd's story, and — this is the cost, pay attention — of her own attempts at closeness. She would meet someone promising, and within an hour her mind had run the whole road ahead: what they would talk about, where they would disagree, how it would cool, how it would end. And having read the book, why live it? So she stayed in the tower. It was not pride, whatever the road said. It was a kind of despair that looks exactly like pride from the outside.

Two things kept the wall from closing completely.

The first was letters. On paper, minds meet without the noise of faces, and Rowena wrote letters the way other people keep gardens. Her greatest correspondence — twenty years of it, hundreds of letters, carried by a relay of extremely fit owls — was with a Welsh healer she had never met, a woman who signed herself simply *H*, whose letters were full of kitchen smells and sharp gentle questions, and who was the only person alive that Rowena could not predict. We are one chapter away from her.

The second thing was a dream. Not the famous dream — the pig comes later, be patient — but a waking one, older and quieter. It had begun in her childhood, the day her mother told her about a girl in a fishing village, two valleys east, who could sing broken bones straight, and who had died at nine, of a winter fever, never having met another magical soul who could tell her what she was. Little Rowena had asked — of course — *why*. Why did no one teach her? Where do such children go? And her mother had answered, with the tired honesty that was her way: "Nowhere, my heart. There is no place for them. There is no place like that in the world."

*Why not?* said the child.

The question never left. It sat in Rowena through thirty years like a seed under a stone floor. In the margins of her notebooks, between spell-diagrams and star-tables, there began to appear — I have seen these pages, they are in the school's oldest vault — little sketches. A hall with long tables. A list of subjects, crossed out and rewritten. Sleeping-rooms in towers, because children like towers. A word in her private code, repeated, circled: a place where no bright child dies untaught. She showed the sketches to no one. The glen had taught her what happens to precious things: you keep them behind walls.

It took a kitchen-witch from Wales, a swordsman from the moors, and a serpent-tongued potioneer from the fens to teach her the harder lesson — that some precious things die behind walls, and the only way to keep them is to give them away.

But I run ahead. First, you must meet the letter-writer. First, you must meet *H*.
## 5. The Healer of the Valley

Wales is a country made of valleys, and in one of them — green, deep, with a river that talked to itself all night — stood a long stone farmhouse with the biggest kitchen west of anywhere. Smoke rose from its chimney in all seasons. Around the yard stood barns and beehives and an orchard of stubborn little apple trees, and over the door, carved into the stone by some grandmother generations back, was a single word in old Welsh that means, more or less: *eat first, explain later.*

That was the whole law of the house, and the house was Helga's.

Muggles knew the farm, and this is the first thing you must understand about Helga, because she is the opposite pole of this story from Salazar, and their two answers to the same terrible question will one day face each other across a council table. The valley Muggles knew her, traded with her, sent for her at every difficult birth of child, calf or lamb for twenty miles. Did they know what she was? Here is the truth: they were careful not to ask the question. She was Helga of the farm; her bread rose higher than bread rises; the sick who slept under her roof got well faster than sense allowed; and the valley wanted her exactly as she was. There is a kind of village silence that is not ignorance but protection, and Helga had earned it — not by hiding, but by being, for thirty years, impossible to do without.

Salazar, when he learned all this much later, said it could not last. That one bad harvest would burn her like all the others. And Helga looked at him over her mixing bowl and said, mildly, "You may be right, my dear. I have decided to be useful anyway." No one ever won an argument against Helga. Even I never worked out how she did it. She never raised her voice. She agreed with pieces of everything you said. And at the end you found yourself carrying firewood for her position while she buttered a scone for yours.

She was born on that farm, the youngest of many; the magic in the family came and went by its own weather, and in her it came strong and strange. Not battle-strong. Kitchen-strong, which the songs undervalue, because the songs were mostly written by men with swords. Helga's magic lived in transformations of the humble kind: flour into plenty, herbs into healing, a crowded table into a family. Food multiplied under her hands. That is rare, deep magic — you cannot make food from nothing, that is one of the laws, but what she could do was stretch it: one goose feeding twenty, a thin soup becoming somehow both thicker and more, the last winter apples lasting, lasting, lasting. Ask any house-elf: feeding-charms are the aristocracy of charms. And she brewed — not Salazar's glittering razor-edged potions, but remedies with deep bottoms to them: for fevers, for coughs, for sorrows. Her fever-draught is still made in our hospital wing to her exact recipe. A thousand years, and nobody has improved it. That fact would have pleased her less than you think; she would have wanted to know why nobody was trying.

The farm collected creatures the way low ground collects water. Lost dogs, lame horses, a blind owl, three ravens who had opinions about everybody, and — most importantly for our story — the small folk. The valley had always had house-elves, living thin and half-wild in the hedges after their old families died out or turned them away. You may know house-elves only as servants in great houses. Understand: an elf without a house is a broken thing; their magic needs a hearth the way a fire needs wood. One March night a small elf with ears like torn leaves knocked at Helga's door, bowed to the floor, and asked, trembling, whether the mistress of so great a kitchen had need of hands.

The mistress of so great a kitchen looked at him for a moment, then said what she said to everyone: "Eat first. Explain later."

His name was Pim. Within five years there were eleven elves in the valley, fed, safe, fierce with pride in their work. When Helga eventually went north to build a school — patience, we are nearly there — the elves went with her, all of them, refusing to be left, and that is how our castle's kitchen began: not with a founder's plan but with a procession of eleven elves carrying Helga's pots through the mountains. Pim's descendants work below the Great Hall to this night. They still tell it: *the Mistress said eat first.*

So there she is: the warm pole of our story. Now I must tell you the other thing, the thing under the warmth, because if you see only the scones you will misjudge her as everyone always did, right up until the moment she said no.

---

The same fever-spring that made her famous for twenty miles brought a boy to her door.

He was perhaps ten, a charcoal-burner's son from over the eastern ridge — outside her valley, beyond her protecting silence — and he was already deep in the fever when his exhausted father carried him in. Helga did what she did. The fever broke by the second dawn. And on the third evening, as the boy slept and his father slept, Helga, sitting beside the bed, saw the fire in the hearth lean toward the child — and the flames, for a moment, curl into little running shapes, deer and hounds, that circled the logs and looked at her.

The boy was doing it. In his sleep. The way magical children do, before anyone teaches them.

You and I know what that meant. Helga knew, in one falling heartbeat, that the sleeping child was one of hers — one of ours — born by magic's own weather into a Muggle family on a bare hillside with nobody, nobody at all, to tell him what he was.

She said nothing to the father. She could not; there were no safe words for it in that world; that silence is exactly the prison this whole book is about. She sent them home with bread and medicine and a standing welcome, and she began, quietly, to visit the eastern ridge more often than her herbs strictly required, watching over the charcoal-burner's boy from the edges, the way you shield a candle with your hand.

Two years she managed it. Then came a late killing frost, and after it a hunger-summer, and after that the sickness that hunger always invites — and it was not to Helga's valley that the ridge folk turned in their fear. It was to a wandering preacher of the burning kind. You remember Mara's chapter. I will not walk you through it twice; my cloth is old and some pages of me are worn thin exactly here. I will say only what is needed. The boy was twelve by then, and the fire had begun leaning toward him in the daytime too, and people had seen, and when the fear needed a door to leave through, they knew where to point.

Helga was away at a birthing two valleys west. She came the moment word reached her.

She came very fast. She was not, whatever the songs think, powerless in the battle way — I once watched her stop a charging bull with a word, and the bull apologised. She crossed the ridge that night like weather, and it did not matter, because fear had moved faster. What was done was done two days before she ever heard.

I will tell you what she did after, because it is the exact shape of her, as the flat river is the shape of Salazar. She did not curse the ridge village. She did not abandon it. All that killing winter, food kept arriving there — sacks at doors, no footprints in the snow — because, as she said to her ravens, the children of that village were hungry, and the children had done nothing. But something in the farmhouse changed. The neighbours felt it without naming it: the warmth was still warmth, yet somewhere under the kitchen floor a stone had shifted. Pim heard her, one deep night, alone among the sleeping pots, say aloud to the fire:

"There must be a wall around such children. A wall, and a roof, and a table. And I cannot build it."

She was wrong about the last part, as she was wrong about almost nothing else. But she was right that she could not build it alone. A wall needs a mason; a roof needs height; a table needs a hall to stand in. She had the table — she had been setting it her whole life. What she needed was a tower's reach, a serpent's caution, and a sword's plain courage.

She went inside, that night, and wrote a letter to Scotland. It was short, for her. Twenty years of correspondence, and the letter that moved history fit on half a page. The tower's copy survives; I have seen it; the ink is still angry. Six lines about the boy on the ridge. Then:

*You have written to me for twenty years about a place where no bright child dies untaught. I am done writing about it. Come down out of your glen, Rowena. Bring the sketches.*

And Rowena came.

That is the true order of events, whatever the histories say, and I want it recorded while there is a hat who remembers: the school that was to be the work of four began as the work of two — two middle-aged women who had never met in person, one who could imagine everything, and one who had decided, over a cold hearth in a hungry winter, that imagining was no longer enough.

The men, as usual, arrived later, made a great deal of noise, and got most of the statues. We will let them into the story presently. They improve it, I admit.

But it began with a letter from a kitchen.
## 6. Four by the Fire

Rowena reached the valley at the dark end of November, having travelled — this detail always pleases me — by every method she distrusted: horse, boat, borrowed broom in freezing rain, and finally her own two feet up the muddy valley road, because the last farmer's cart had a wheel she calculated would not survive the hill. It did not. She walked past its wreck with the satisfaction of a woman proved right, arrived at the farmhouse door soaked to the bone, and met, for the first time in the flesh, the person she knew better than anyone on earth.

Twenty years of letters, and neither had seen the other's face. Pim opened the door. There was a silence, I am told, of nearly a minute — the tall dripping Scottish witch on the step, the round Welsh one in the fire-light, each studying the other like a difficult text. Then Helga said, "You are thinner than your letters," and Rowena said, "You are exactly like yours," and that was that. Some friendships need to be built. This one only needed to be unpacked.

They talked for eleven days.

The sketches came out of Rowena's bag the first evening — the hall with long tables, the towers, the crossed-out lists of subjects, thirty years of a locked private dream — and Helga did to those pages what she did to everything: put them on the kitchen table, fed their owner, and asked the plain questions that pull a dream down out of the air and stand it in the mud where things must actually live. Who feeds these children? Who guards them? What do the parents get told, and what do the Muggle parents get told, and by whom, and how do you find the children in the first place — the hidden ones, the ridge-boys, the ones nobody has counted?

To nearly every question Rowena had an answer — she had had thirty years — and where she had none, having the question stated plainly by that voice, over that table, was like sun on frost. The plan grew by the day. But two questions would not die, and by the eleventh night they stood alone between the women like two stones in a field.

The first was safety. A school gathers the most precious and most dangerous thing our world owns — its children, its future, all of it in one visible place. A school is therefore a target: for the burning kind of Muggle fear, yes, but also — Rowena said this quietly, being a student of history — for wizards of the hungry kind, who would see a generation of young magic gathered under one roof and think of harvest. Neither woman could build walls equal to that. Warming-charms and star-charts do not stop a war band. They needed strength — real, tested, frightening strength — and it had to live in a person they could trust with children, which is a shorter list than the list of the strong.

The second stone was harder, and Helga was the one who set it down. The school they were drawing existed to reach exactly the children nobody reached — which meant walking into the Muggle world, again and again, forever: knocking on farm doors, explaining the unexplainable to frightened parents, carrying a secret across that border a hundred times a year without ever once dropping it. One dropped secret, one talkative uncle, one preacher of the burning kind with a real address in his pocket — and their gathered treasure of children becomes the largest fire ever lit. "What we need," Helga said, "is someone who thinks like the fear itself. Someone who sees the crack in every wall, the lie in every smile — someone the world has taught to expect the worst, so that we may plan for it while hoping better." She paused, and added, because she was honest: "The trouble is that such people are made by suffering, and suffering does not usually improve them."

At which point — and if this book were invented, little reader, I would not dare invent what comes next — there was a knocking, a great cheerful hammering, on the farmhouse door.

On the step, soaked, steaming, enormous, stood a red-haired man of about thirty, holding the splintered wheel of a farm cart, with an old pointed hat dripping on his head. Behind him in the rain, hooded, silent, stood a thin pale companion, looking at the warm doorway the way a cat looks at a river it is nonetheless about to cross.

"Your pardon, mistress," said the red-haired man, "but we found a farmer sitting in the mud by his broken cart halfway down the valley, and mended what we could and carried the rest, and he says the load was bound for this house, and also — forgive me — that yours was the only fire in Wales worth walking to in such weather. He is a poet, your farmer. We fixed his wheel. This one is past fixing. Might we trade the news of the road for a place at your hearth until morning?"

Chance? The road-folk argued about it for a century. Godric to his last day swore it was pure luck and better weather-sense. Salazar held that nothing about that November was chance: word of a healer who fed all comers had long been trade-currency on the hidden road, and he had — his own words, years later, delivered with the driest of smiles — "a professional interest in people whom entire valleys agree to protect." Perhaps the truth is only this: warmth is a lighthouse. All four of them, in their different darks, had been steering by it for years.

Helga looked the strangers up and down. And said the law of the house.

They ate first. They explained later — much later, for the explaining, once begun, would not stop. The travellers' names went around the fire, and were recognised; Gryffindor and Slytherin were trade-currency too by then. The talk was small, and then less small. Rowena, who despised talking to strangers, discovered a stranger who had read the star-tables of the far south, and had *corrections* to them; the corrections were impertinent; two of them were right. Helga watched the big man notice every draught in her house and quietly place himself between the cold and everyone else, all evening, by what appeared to be accident. The fire burned low. Pim brought more wood without being asked, then sat in the shadows by the oven, listening, as elves have always listened, to the hinge-nights of history.

It was Godric — of course it was Godric — who blundered through the door the women had spent eleven days circling. Somewhere past midnight, warmed and unbuttoned, he was telling the fens-and-road tales of the last years, and he came, without knowing the ground, to a fever-spring on the eastern marches, and a boy in it — a miller's boy, magic-born to Muggle folk, taken by the burning fear a bare week before the two riders passed through. The room went still in a way he did not at first understand. He pressed on, softer: how he and Salazar had stood in that cold market square where it was done; and how he, Godric, had raged at the sky and wanted names and a reckoning; and how Salazar had stood a long time silent by the market cross and finally said the thing Godric could not stop hearing since.

Salazar, from the shadows, in a voice like a door not quite closing, finished it himself. "I said: put away your sword. There is no one here to fight. This was not done by wicked men; it was done by frightened ones, and you cannot kill fear with iron. It has no body. It has only doors. And we — " he stopped; the fire ticked; the two women did not breathe — "we leave our children standing in its doorway, one by one, alone, and we call the survivors lucky. I buried a sister in that doorway. I have no interest in luck. If our kind is ever to be safe, someone must build the place the fear cannot enter. One gate. High walls. And every child of our blood inside them, taught, and armed, and *counted*."

In the silence after, Rowena Ravenclaw reached into her travelling bag, took out thirty years of secret pages, and laid them on the table without a word.

What was said around that kitchen table between midnight and the grey Welsh dawn, no complete record survives — and I will be honest with you as promised: I was on a peg by the door, being at that time an ordinary hat, and even the finest hat hears poorly from a peg. Pim, in old age, told the young elves that the four voices went on until the birds began, and that the candles burned out unnoticed, and that at one point the big man wept and did not trouble to hide it, and at another the tall grey lady laughed three times in a row, "which," said Pim solemnly, "frightened the ravens."

But the ending is recorded, because all four of them told it, always the same way, and when the tellings agree, even a hat believes. As the first light came up the valley, Helga Hufflepuff — who had said the least and steered the most, that being her particular magic — set four cups of morning ale on the table, and looked round the fire at the swordsman, the serpent-tongue, and the tower-witch, none of whom had slept and none of whom wished to, and said:

"Well. We shall need a great many beds. Where do we build it?"

And that question — where — has the strangest answer in this whole true story. It arrived eight nights later, in a dream, wearing the shape of a large, ugly, extremely determined pig.

The next chapter, I am delighted to report, is about the pig.
## 7. The Warty Hog

The four stayed together in the valley through the turn of the year, and the plan grew from a dream into lists — Rowena's lists, mostly, though Helga's hand appears in the margins wherever the lists forgot that children must eat. But one line stayed empty at the top of every page. *Where.*

It was not a small question. Say it with their voices and you will hear how it splits four ways.

Godric wanted the school where the people were — near the roads, near the villages, a bright door in the middle of Britain. A hidden school, he argued, teaches children that they are a shameful secret. "I will not raise a generation in a cellar," he said. "Let the walls be strong, but let them stand in the daylight."

Salazar wanted the opposite so completely that on bad evenings the two of them circled the kitchen like weather fronts. Daylight was exactly the danger. One gate, unreachable except by those brought to it; mountains, water, distance; a place Muggle maps had never touched. "You call it a cellar," he said. "I call it the first place in a thousand years where no one has to listen for the crowd."

Rowena wanted defensible ground and useful stars — high, north, dry — and had opinions about rock quality that lasted, on one occasion, from supper until the following supper. Helga wanted good water, land that could feed a great house, and — she said it perfectly seriously — a kitchen with morning light, "because porridge made in the dark tastes of the dark, and the children will know."

They argued through January. They argued through February. Four people who among them knew everything, and could not choose a hilltop. I have thought about this deadlock for a thousand years, and here is my finding: the argument was never about geography. Each of the four was trying to build the place that would have saved *their* dead — Salazar's sister, Helga's ridge-boy, Rowena's singing girl from the fishing village, and for Godric, every soul he had reached too late on every road. You cannot settle such an argument with a map. It had to be settled another way.

It was settled another way.

On the eighth night of March, Rowena dreamed. She was not, she always insisted, a dreaming witch; she distrusted prophecy on principle and had written a rather cutting monograph about it. Nevertheless. On the eighth night of March she dreamed she stood on the farmhouse step in her travelling cloak, and out of the dark of the yard came a pig.

Not a handsome pig. She was always exact about this, being exact about everything: an enormous, ancient, mud-coloured boar, with little wise wicked eyes and a hide covered in warts — a warty hog, the ugliest animal she had ever beheld, awake or asleep. It stopped in front of her. It looked up at her with the expression — I quote her account word for word, it is preserved in her own hand — "of a schoolmaster whose patience, though vast, has ended." Then it turned and trotted away north, and stopped, and looked back over its shoulder.

Even asleep, Rowena Ravenclaw knew what *follow me* looked like. She was simply too proud to obey a pig immediately. In the dream she folded her arms. The hog sighed — she insisted upon the sigh — walked back, took the hem of her cloak in its mouth, and *pulled*.

Then they were walking, the way dreams walk you: hills pouring past like water, days folding into strides. She was aware of mountains rising, of the smell of the country changing — peat and pine and cold stone — of the hog trotting tirelessly ahead through a land that grew grander and emptier and more like her own bones with every step. And then it crested a last ridge and stopped, and she came up beside it, and looked down.

A black loch, long and still, holding the last of the sunset like a blade holds light. Above the water, a great shoulder of grey rock, high and proud, with room on its crown for towers. Old forest crowding the far shore, deep and dark and full of eyes. Mountains behind, closing the horizon like a hand closing. It was defensible. It was hidden. It was beautiful in the specific way that makes the chest ache — and it was, she realised with the particular shock of dreams, *her* loch: the water of her own glen lay somewhere south of those same peaks, fed by the same snows. The hog had led her home, or nearly home — to a place a half-day's flight north of the tower where she had spent her whole walled-up life dreaming of a hall with long tables.

The hog looked at the rock. Then at her. Then — and this she reported with a straight face to three of the most powerful wizards in Britain over breakfast — it said, in a voice like a cartwheel on gravel: "Well? Will it do?" And she woke.

Breakfast that morning is one of the scenes I most regret hearing only from a peg. Rowena, who had staked her reputation on the emptiness of dreams, laying this one out flat on the table because honesty outranked pride, and daring anyone to laugh. Godric laughing anyway — not at her, he swore, at the pig, at the *sigh* — until Helga's look stopped him. Salazar asking two quiet questions: whether the rock in the dream had shown any door or cave at the waterline — it had, she said, startled; a low dark opening where the loch met the cliff — and whether the hog had had a torn left ear. It had, she said, more startled still. Salazar sat back, and said nothing for the rest of breakfast, and never afterwards explained; fen-folk hold that certain old spirits of the land wear the boar-shape when they choose to be seen, and if one had chosen, that March, to take an interest in the children of Britain, he did not consider it his secret to discuss.

What could they do? They went.

They went on foot and wing through the spring, all four together, north out of Wales up the long spine of the country, and I went too, on the head where I lived. I remember — in my cloth, the way I remember everything from those years, as warmth and weather rather than words — the journey lengthening and lightening as it ran. Somewhere on those roads, four planners became four companions. Helga fed them out of a bag that held more than a bag holds. Rowena corrected the maps as they walked, and was twice corrected by Salazar, and by the second time it had become their private sport. Godric sang. He sang badly. The others let him, which tells you everything about the state of the company by April.

And on an evening at the end of April, they came over a last ridge in the eastern Highlands — Rowena walking ahead now, faster and faster, like a woman hearing her own name called — and stopped, all four, without a word.

A black loch, holding the sunset. A grey shoulder of rock with room for towers. The old forest, watching. The mountains, closing the horizon like a hand.

It was exactly the dream, and it was better than the dream, because it was cold and windy and real, and the air off the water went into the four of them like strong drink. They stood in a row on the ridge for a long time. Helga cried a little, and said it was the wind. Godric took off his hat — took off *me* — and held me to his chest, which he did only at weddings and burials, so I understood the size of the moment from the inside.

It was Salazar who finally spoke, and he asked the practical question, because someone always must: "And what do we call it?"

Silence. Then Rowena — tower-proud, dream-led, standing above the great work of the rest of her life — began, helplessly, to laugh. The third laugh of that famous winter, and the largest. "We call it what it is," she said. "I was brought here by an enormous warty hog. I owe the creature a debt, and I pay my debts. Let the name keep the joke, so that the school never grows too grand to remember it began with a pig."

*Hogwarts.*

Godric roared. Helga clapped her hands like a girl. Salazar — I want this recorded, because history has straightened his mouth into a thin line and forgotten what it could do — Salazar smiled his real smile, the mud-and-mist-morning smile, and said: "Perfect. No dark wizard will ever be able to say it aloud with a straight face. That alone is a defence."

A thousand years later, the joke is still in the name, and almost nobody remembers it is one. But you know now. Tell somebody.

They camped that night on the rock itself, under the stars of Rowena's charts, on the bare stone crown where the Great Hall now stands. Four bedrolls, one fire, the loch breathing below, the forest watching across the water. Tomorrow there would be surveys and arguments and goblins and twelve years of the hardest work four wizards ever did.

But that night, I am told — and this once, the four tellings agree to the word — nobody planned anything. They lay by the fire under the wheeling stars, tired and happy and thirty-something and slightly drunk on mountain air, and they talked about nothing: roads and pigs and porridge and whether Godric could sing (no), and one by one they dropped off, until only the fire was awake.

Children sleep on that rock now. Hundreds of them, every night, in the towers that grew where the bedrolls lay.

Sleep well, little reader. Tomorrow we build the castle.
## 8. A Castle from the Stone

How do you build a castle?

If you are a Muggle king, the answer is: with the lives of other men. A thousand workers, thirty years, a mountain of silver, and a graveyard's worth of accidents. If you are four wizards on a rock at the edge of the world, the answer is stranger, slower in some ways and faster in others, and it begins — this surprises people — with listening.

You cannot simply command stone. This is a thing wand-waving stories get wrong. Stone is old and patient and has its own opinion, and a wall raised against the stone's opinion will stand exactly as long as the spell is fresh and not one winter longer. The first month on the rock, therefore, no wall rose at all. Salazar and Rowena spent it flat on their faces — sometimes literally, ear to the ground — learning the great shoulder of rock the way you learn a language: where it was strong, where it was tired, where water ran secretly through it, where it *wanted* to be opened. Rowena mapped. Salazar, who had grown up reading the moods of drowned land, went deeper; he found the caves — the low dark opening at the waterline from Rowena's dream was only the first door of a whole hidden country of them, worn by ten thousand years of patient water — and he came up from that first long exploration with wet hair and shining eyes and said the sentence the others would remember at the end of everything:

"It goes down further than we will ever need."

You know things he did not know when he said it. Let it lie. In that bright first summer, it was only good news: natural cellars, natural wells, foundations half-made by time itself.

Then the building — and here I must break a legend, gently, the way Helga broke bad news. The songs say four wizards raised Hogwarts alone, by pure might, in a season. It took eleven years, and it took help. Might they had; but a castle is not one great spell, it is a hundred thousand small agreements — this beam to this socket, this stair to this landing, water here but never there — and no four minds hold so many agreements at once. What the four did, each after their kind, was this.

Rowena designed. It was the joy of her life. Every tower, every hall, every impossible stair came off her drawing-boards, and into the design she poured her particular signature, which was wit. She had noticed — typically — that a fortress which cannot change is only a riddle waiting for a patient enemy, so she built change into the bones: stairs that move, doors that wander, rooms that are not always where they were. A thousand years of students have cursed her for it on the way to lessons. She would have been delighted. The moving stairs are not a decoration, little reader; they are a sword that has never yet been fully drawn.

Salazar warded. Layer under layer, patient as sediment: the walls that turn away Muggle eyes — walk up to our rock as a Muggle, and you will see a mouldering ruin and a sign advising you of unsafe footing, and you will suddenly remember an appointment elsewhere — the gates that weigh intentions, the stones that remember faces. And the deep work, down in his beloved caves, where he was happiest and, increasingly, alone. He asked for the dungeons as his province the way Rowena asked for towers, and no one argued; someone must love the cold parts of a house. His house within the house grew down there, green-lit by loch-water windows, beautiful in its own drowned way.

Helga provisioned. Say the word plainly: she kept everyone alive for eleven years. She broke ground for gardens on the south slope before the first wall stood; she tamed the half-wild bees of the forest edge; her eleven elves — twenty by the end, for word travels among the hearthless — built the kitchens with her as the true first room of the castle, and from that room fed the whole slow miracle. And it was Helga who dealt with the neighbours, because there were neighbours: a straggle of magical families had followed the building the way gulls follow a plough, and settled below the rock, and become, without anyone deciding it, the beginnings of the village you know as Hogsmeade. Helga learned every name. Within three years the village would have gone to war for her. Within five, there was a market. She had conjured a community out of a construction site, which is a spell none of the other three could have begun to cast.

And Godric — Godric did everything else, which history undercounts because "everything else" fits badly on a statue. He was quarry-master and beam-hoister and weather-watcher; he was the strong back in every emergency and the loud heart of every discouraged evening. Above all he was the guard. Eleven years is a long time to be interesting news in a wild country. Things came out of the mountains, in those years, that tested the unfinished walls: wolf-packs of the ordinary kind and of the other kind; a hill-troll with grievances; once, in the ninth year, a band of wizards of the hungry sort, come to see whether four builders far from anywhere might be relieved of their gold. Godric met each arrival at the edge of the light, and the mountains gradually learned the new fact of the country, the fact that would keep the school safe for centuries: *the rock is guarded.* The hungry wizards' leader, disarmed and dangling upside down over the loch, is said to have asked what power held the place. Godric, considering, answered: "Four friends." Then he let the man down politely and fed him supper, because his mother had raised him properly.

But I promised you goblins, and the goblins matter — a thread of them runs through this book to its very last chapter.

Stone will agree to stand; but the bones of a great building — the deep clamps and pins that hold a tower's heart together, the hinges of gates meant to outlast kingdoms — such work wants metal, and metal-mastery belongs to the goblin nations, and no honest history writes it otherwise. In the third year, Rowena's surveys met the upper galleries of a goblin delving, deep under the mountains east of the loch; and instead of pretending otherwise — the usual wizard habit, with the usual bloody results — the four went down, openly, to ask leave and offer trade.

The negotiations lasted a winter. Goblins do not sell quickly to wizards, having been robbed by them too often, and the king under those mountains was Ragnuk the First, hardest and finest of his line. But a bargain was struck, metal for gold and, more valuable than gold, for potions and healing of Salazar's and Helga's making, such as goblin-kind could not brew. Ragnuk's smiths forged the castle's iron bones, its great hinges, the clamps in the towers' hearts — goblin work, meant for forever, singing faintly if you put your ear to the stone, which I have, being often left on ledges.

And in the last year of building, Ragnuk himself forged one thing more: a sword. Silver-bright, goblin-silver true, rubies in the hilt, balanced like a thought — the finest blade of the age, made to the measure of Godric's arm, in payment of an honour-debt; for in the sixth year Godric had gone down into a flooded gallery, where the mountain had turned on the delvers, and carried out alive the king's own son. Wizard gold had bought the hinges. The sword was not bought. The sword was *given* — and yet, mark this and fold the corner of the page: in goblin law, a made thing belongs finally to its maker, and a gift is a lending stretched long, and what was given to Godric was, in Ragnuk's deep reckoning, still and forever Ragnuk's. Neither friend explained his law to the other. Each assumed his own was the world's. Out of exactly such silences, chapters come later in books like this one.

Eleven years. And then a morning — early autumn, the rowans red around the loch — when the scaffolds were down, and the four walked the whole of it together, from Helga's kitchens up through the Great Hall, whose enchanted ceiling Rowena unveiled that day and would explain to no one ("a lady keeps one secret"), up the moving stairs that bowed as their maker passed, to the top of the tallest tower.

Below them: gardens, village, loch, forest, and the castle itself falling away in grey-gold terraces, finished, breathing, *waiting*. Four people not far from fifty, standing where there had been bare stone and a pig's opinion.

They were quiet a long while. Then Helga said what all four were thinking, because that was her office in the fellowship.

"It is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen," she said. "And it is entirely empty. Go and fetch the children."

The next part of this story — the best part, I think, though the saddest is also in it — begins with boats on dark water, and the first hundred children in the history of the world who ever came home to a school.
## Interlude: The Elf's Tale

Between the parts of this book, I will step aside three times and let another voice speak. A story told by one witness is a lantern held from one side; the founders deserve daylight. This first voice is Pim's — the elf who knocked on Helga's door with his ears like torn leaves, and who outlived her, and whose telling of the building years was handed down word for word in the kitchens for generations. The elves recited it for me on the thousandth anniversary of the school, all of them together in the firelight, the way it is always recited. I have changed nothing but the spelling of the sighs.

*

The Mistress said we were going north, and so we went north. That is the whole first year of the story, and young elves always want more of it, and there is no more of it. She said: "Pim, we are going to build a school on a rock, and the rock is eleven days' walk, and the pots must come." And Pim said what an elf says, which is: "Then Pim will carry the big one."

Eleven elves went north. Eleven! Elves of the hedges, elves who had eaten beetles in the bad years, elves nobody wanted — going to build the great thing, with pots on our backs. The ravens flew over us all the way and told the mountains we were coming.

Now the young ones always ask: what was the rock like, when there was no castle? And the answer is: it was like a loaf not yet baked. The wizards looked at it and saw walls and towers. Elves looked at it and saw a cold place with no kitchen, and elves were right, because elves are always right about the important thing first. So while the four great ones walked about pointing and arguing — they argued beautifully, like weather; you could dry washing in the wind of it — the elves dug the first hearth into the rock's south side, out of the wind, and by the third night there was bread. And here is the thing the young ones must remember, because it is the deepest law in this story: the kitchen is older than the castle. The fire came before the walls. Everything they built, they built around a warm place that elves made — and that is not Pim boasting, that is just the true order of things, and the Mistress said so herself, often, and once she said it to the tall lady in front of everyone, and the tall lady thought about it in her tall way and said, "That is correct, and it is also the history of the world," and wrote it down.

The four great ones, as the kitchen saw them. The young ones always ask, so:

The red one was the easiest to feed and the hardest to keep fed. He ate like three horses and thanked you like you had saved his life, every time, every meal, thirty years. When the beams went up he carried the ends nobody else could lift, and he sang while he did it, and his singing was so bad that the stones fit themselves faster to get away from it. That is a joke the Mistress made and Pim is allowed to repeat it because she laughed first.

The green one — the quiet one — the young ones always go big-eyed about the green one now, because of the stories after. Listen to Pim: in the building years, the green one was the one who remembered. Remembered that elves feel cold through their feet, and had the cellar floors laid warm-stoned before anyone asked. Remembered which elf had the cough. He never said the kind thing. He *did* the kind thing and walked away quickly, like a thief in backwards, and if you thanked him he pretended a great interest in a wall. Elves understood him better than wizards did, Pim thinks. Elves also know about doing the good thing sideways because the front way hurts too much. When he left — but that is not the building years, and Pim's telling stops at the walls.

The tall lady drew the whole castle on papers, and the papers were beautiful, and sometimes wrong, and here is a secret a thousand years old: when a paper was wrong — when a stair could not go where the drawing said, because stone is stone — the elves did not tell her. The elves just built it the possible way, one hand-width different, and she would come with her paper and look a long time and say, "Hm. I improved this in my sleep, evidently," and correct the paper. She knew. Of course she knew — she knew everything in that castle down to the mice. It was a game two proud peoples played so nobody had to be wrong out loud, and it worked thirty years, and masons and mistresses both went to bed with their pride warm, and that, young ones, is diplomacy, and elves invented it.

And the Mistress. No. Pim will not describe the Mistress. Pim has tried, every telling, one thousand years of tellings, and every time the words come out the size of a teaspoon and the Mistress was the size of the harvest. Pim will only tell the one morning, and the young ones may build her from that, the way you build the sun from one window.

It was the bad winter of the building — the seventh — when the passes shut early and the stores were short and there was ice inside the walls that had no roofs yet, and everyone was thin, and the wizard workers from the villages were thin, and Pim came before light to the hearth and found the Mistress already there, counting the flour with her hands. Not with her eyes. With her hands, the way you count when the counting matters. And Pim stood in the door and was afraid, because elves know flour-arithmetic from the bad years, and Pim could see the answer from the doorway, and the answer was: not enough.

And the Mistress looked at the flour a long time. And then she rolled up her sleeves and said — not sad, not brave, just the way she said everything, like the weather being told what to do next — she said: "Well then, Pim. Today we bake the small loaves, and we bake them twice as good, so that no one measures what they can taste. Fetch the honey we were saving. We were saving it for a day the loaves are small. Here it is."

We bake them twice as good, so no one measures what they can taste. The young elves say it now over every thin harvest, and it has kept the kitchens of this school through three sieges and one dragon and more Februaries than the ledgers hold, and the wizards upstairs think it is a saying about baking.

It is not a saying about baking, young ones. It is the Mistress, whole. Everything they built up there on the rock — the towers, the wards, the houses, the thousand years — all of it was the big loaf, for the days the world's loaf came out small. That is what a school is. That is what she was for, and what the four were for, and what elves are for, and the fire came before the walls, and the kitchen is older than the castle, and that is the end of the building years as Pim tells them.

Now, the pots want scouring. The story does not scour the pots. Down you get.

*

That is Pim's tale, as the kitchen keeps it. The pots in question, I am told, are still in service.

We return now to the wizards upstairs — to the boats on the black water, and the first September, and the year the school learned its own name.

## 9. The First Journey

The castle was finished. Now came the harder thing: filling it.

Understand the problem. There was no register of magical children in Britain. There was no owl-post system, no lists, no records — secrecy had seen to that. The hidden families knew each other in chains and clusters, fen knowing fen, glen knowing glen, but nobody knew the whole; and beyond the known families lay the others, the scattered sparks, the ridge-boys and singing girls born by magic's own weather into Muggle houses, whom no chain of gossip would ever find.

So, in the winter after the building ended, the four founders did the thing I still consider the bravest in this whole book, braver than any duel in it: they split up, and went out into Britain, knocking on doors.

Each took a quarter of the island, after their kind. Godric rode the south and west, openly, hat on head — I saw the whole tour — into every hidden-road hall and moor-top farm, where his name opened doors by itself, and he stood by a thousand hearths saying the unheard-of sentence: *there is a school.* Say it slowly to yourself, in a world where it had never been true. There is a school for our children. High walls, warm beds, seven years of teaching, and no fear. The looks on the mothers' faces, hearth after hearth, are stored in my cloth forever. Hope, arriving in a body's face, looks strangely like pain for the first moment. I never tired of watching it land.

Salazar took the east, hooded, quiet, moving up the chains of the old families — the proud, closed, frightened houses like his own — and he spoke to them in their own grammar. To the fathers he talked of walls and wards, one gate, a guarded rock; to the grandmothers he named the ancient blood-lines gathering there, and was heard. Some houses that would have barred the door to loud Godric opened for the pale man who understood, from inside, exactly what they were afraid of. Fair is fair, and this book is honest: dozens of the school's first families came because Salazar Slytherin sat in their cold halls and was believed. The school needed his darkness as much as it needed Godric's daylight. It always had.

Rowena flew the north. She had, typically, solved the finding problem as an *instrument problem*: two years earlier she had begun charming a great book — you may have heard of it; it is still writing — a book that listens across Britain for the particular bright disturbance a magical birth makes, and writes down the name. The Book of Admittance sat chained in its tower even then, with its silver quill. But the Book found only the future; for this first harvest of children already alive and half-grown, older arts were needed, and Rowena rode the winds of the north with instruments of her own design, dowsing for sparks, landing in snowy farmyards where an eleven-year-old had been quietly moving cups without touching them and thinking herself alone in creation, or mad.

And Helga went to the ones the others could not reach: the Muggle-born. Hers was the quarter with no map at all, and the most dangerous work, and she did it the way she did everything, by kitchen and by kindness. She had gathered, through her healing networks and her valley-silences, rumour of strange children in three dozen villages, and she went to them as what she was — a healer on her rounds — and sat in Muggle kitchens, and drank what she was given, and watched the child, and talked to the mother about nothing at all. And where the spark was real, there came the moment — she chose it like a midwife choosing the moment — when she set down her cup and said, gently, with the whole authority of her grey hair and her healed hundreds: "Now then. I think you have noticed something about your Gwen. And I think it has frightened you. Let me tell you what I know."

Not one of Helga's first-year visits ended in fire. Not one, ever, in thirty years of them. Her secret, she said, was that you must love the parents, not merely manage them: "The mother is not an obstacle between me and the child. She has been guarding a frightening miracle alone, in the dark, for eleven years. She is the child's first protector. I come to tell her she is no longer the only one." Some doors, even so, were closed on her; some sparks she was made to leave behind in villages she then watched for years from afar. But when the founding spring came, there were eleven Muggle-born children on the school's first roll, each one fetched personally by Helga Hufflepuff — and yes, one of them was called Edith. Patience. Edith gets a whole chapter; she earned it.

The letters went out that summer. There were no owl-relays yet; the four delivered most by hand, and the wording of the first letter was fought over for a week — Rowena's draft was a page of terms and instructions, Godric's was four lines, and Godric's won, because Helga read them both aloud at the kitchen table and asked which one an eleven-year-old would sleep with under their pillow. I still have the words by heart. Every hat in my line does.

*To the family of ————. There is a school. It stands in the north, and it is safe, and it is warm, and children like yours are wanted there. Teaching begins at the harvest moon. Send us your bright one, and we will send you back a wizard. — G. G., S. S., R. R., H. H.*

Send us your bright one. A thousand years, little reader, and no one has written a better sentence about what a school is for.

---

They came at the harvest moon, from all four corners: on ponies over the mountains, by cart up the new lake road, some few by broom, one fen family, magnificently, by boat up the rivers the whole way. The plan for the arrival was Rowena's, and it is the one piece of pure theatre she ever permitted herself, and it worked so well that the school performs it still, every single year, without knowing whose heart it is performing.

The children were gathered at dusk on the far shore of the loch. Behind them, the mountains and the whole of their old lives. Before them, black water, and on the great rock across it, the castle — which the founders, that one night, had asked to light *every window*.

Boats were waiting, lantern-hung, spelled to steer themselves. The parents were not taken across; that parting on the shore was the price, and the founders stood with the families as it was paid. And then the boats moved out onto the still black water, four children to a boat, and the small faces turned up, and the castle came toward them over the loch with all its golden windows burning, and the whole first year of the school of Hogwarts fell silent at once, together, in the middle of the lake.

I was on Godric's head, in the lead boat. Under me, that whole crossing, his thoughts ran on one single track, around and around, like a prayer, like a fist unclenching:

*No child of ours, alone again. No child of ours, alone again. No child of ours — *

There were one hundred and thirty-seven of them, that first year. The doors of the Great Hall stood open, firelight on the water. Helga was on the steps, of course, first to meet them, arms wide, elves behind her with hot bread.

They landed. They came up the lawn in a long straggling wondering line. And the first child through the doors of Hogwarts — history remembers her name, and so must you — was a small fierce fen-country girl named Maeve, who stopped dead on the threshold, looked up at the enchanted ceiling for a long moment, and then turned to the tall grey lady beside the door and asked, in a voice that carried the length of the Hall:

"Is it true a *pig* chose this place?"

And Rowena Ravenclaw, founder of the school, terror of the learned world, looked down at the first student ever to stand in her Great Hall, and said gravely: "It is. The finest judge of ground I ever met. Come in, and mind the stairs — they move."

The children came in. The doors closed on the cold. And the school — the school itself, the thousand-year creature made of stone and food and lessons and voices — took its first breath.

It has not stopped breathing since. But that first night, nobody yet knew a single one of the rules. There were no houses, no tables, no colours, no Sorting — nothing but one hundred and thirty-seven strangers and four middle-aged founders in an echoing hall, all of them, if we are honest, slightly terrified of each other.

How the houses happened — and it did *happen*, more than it was planned — is the next chapter. It involves an argument, a compromise, four tables dragged across a stone floor at midnight, and the beginning of everything I was later born to do.
## 10. Four Tables

Here is a thing nobody tells you: the houses of Hogwarts were not in the plan.

I have read the plan — all nine drafts of it survive, in Rowena's hand with everyone's arguments in the margins — and there is nothing in it about houses. The plan divided children by age and by subject, sensibly, the way you would divide anyone. The houses were not designed. They grew — in about six weeks, out of the plain daily fact that four very different teachers were teaching, and children, like water, find their own level.

It began with the lessons themselves. Each founder taught what they loved, and each loved differently, and within a month every child in the school knew the difference in their bones. Godric's lessons were weather: loud, outdoors when possible, full of movement and laughter and mild danger, defence and duelling and flying and the great outdoor charms. Salazar's were held in the green-lit rooms below: potions, the subtle arts, herbs and their powers, the reading of intentions — quiet as a held breath, precise as surgery, and, for the children who tuned to that frequency, thrilling beyond anything the loud lessons offered. Rowena taught in the towers: charms, runes, star-craft, the architecture of spells — fast, demanding, blazing with ideas, impatient of slowness but boundlessly patient with honest confusion, which she considered the smell of thinking. And Helga taught everywhere and everything in between: healing, growing, the magics of hearth and making, and also — nobody called it a subject, but it was the deepest one on the roll — how to live: how to share a room with a stranger, how to lose a contest, how to apologise so it counts.

And the children began to sort themselves. Nobody commanded it. After lessons, the ones who had caught fire in the duelling yard followed Godric about like gulls; the quiet precise ones drifted down to the green light where things were exact and the teacher never, ever shouted; the tower stairs grew a permanent population of arguers; and Helga's kitchens filled each evening with the ones who wanted, more than glory or secrets or answers, to belong somewhere warm. Within the month, the drift had names among the children themselves — they spoke of Godric's lot and Slytherin's folk, of tower people and kitchen people — and friendships were setting along those lines, fast and deep, the way they do at eleven.

The founders watched this with, I must record, entirely unequal enthusiasm.

Godric thought it was wonderful. Natural as weather. Children finding their kin — where was the harm? Salazar, interestingly, agreed, though his reason was his own: affinity, he said, was the oldest teacher; a master teaches best the students tuned to his own string. It was Helga who stood against it, alone at first, and her words were quiet and have never stopped being true. "We are building walls inside our wall," she said. "We came together — the four of us, four strings that do not match — and everything good we have made, we made *because* we do not match. Sort the children by their strings, and each will spend seven years among echoes of themselves. The brave will never learn caution. The clever will never learn kindness. The kind will never be dared. And the cunning" — she looked at Salazar, and she said it with love, which made it worse — "the cunning will spend seven years learning that the rest of the school is *other*. We know where that road goes. All of us at this table know exactly where that road goes."

Read that speech twice, little reader. It is the whole of this book in nine sentences, spoken in the sixth week of the school's life by the person everyone underestimated. A thousand years later, the question she raised that night is still open. I should know. I am the one who was eventually given the job of answering it, one child at a time, and I have never once put a child in a house without hearing Helga's voice under my brim.

Rowena, that night, was the balance, and she found the compromise — of course she did; it was a design problem, and design was her country. She had watched the drift too, and what she saw in it was not walls: it was *belonging*, arriving early and free. A school of a hundred and thirty-seven was already too big to be one family; a child needs a table where their absence is noticed by supper-time. Twelve dormitories divided by nothing but age would give no such table. Four hearths would. "The strings are real," she said. "Helga is right that a string alone makes a poor education, and you three are right that it makes a strong home. So: let the houses have the *hearths* — the table, the dormitory, the loyalty, the game. And let the school keep the *lessons*: every subject taught to every house, all four of us teaching all of them, mixed and mingled every working hour. Homes apart, days together. The echo Helga fears comes from locking both."

Homes apart, days together. It was adopted at midnight, four to nothing — Helga voted for it, saying she had lost the argument she meant to lose and won the one she meant to win, a sentence Godric claimed to understand years later and did not — and the four of them, too pleased to wait for morning, went down together and dragged the Hall's long benches into four tables with their own hands. I attended, on the head of the loudest furniture-mover. The elves, scandalised, repaired the floor by breakfast.

The children came down that morning to four tables and a founder standing at the head of each, and the school's first Sorting — the only one ever done without me — took place there and then, before the porridge, by the simplest method imaginable: *choose your table.* One hundred and thirty-seven children, sorting themselves by walking, while four founders stood very still and learned, in real time, what they had built.

Most of the drift confirmed itself, as you would guess. But the morning kept its surprises, and I record two, because they set the pattern of a thousand years. A big shy moor-country boy named Brann, whom everyone had marked for Godric's table since the day he out-flew the flying-master, walked instead, red to the ears, to Helga's — because, he explained years later, courage he had from home already; what he had never had was a crowded table. And Maeve — small fierce Maeve of the threshold, fen-born of a fen family, whose folk had followed Salazar's hooded lamp out of the east and assumed her sorted before she was born — Maeve looked a long time at the green-lit table where her cousins sat, and then crossed the whole width of the Hall, in a silence she pretended not to notice, and sat down at Rowena's right hand. She had come, she said, for the moving stairs. She stayed forty years; the school's first great spell-scholar after Rowena herself; but her cousins' table did not speak to her for three of those years, and there, too, honestly recorded, is a pattern that has lasted a thousand years.

By that first winter the houses had their colours — chosen by the children, ratified by the founders with the good sense not to interfere — and their creatures, and their doors, and their private legends, and the corridors rang with the names as if they had always existed: Gryffindor. Slytherin. Ravenclaw. Hufflepuff. The rivalry arrived with the first snowball. The founders, watching four snow-forts rise on the lawn along strict house lines, wearing four sets of colours, told each other it was all in play.

It was all in play. Play is how children rehearse the world. That is what makes it wonderful, and that is what makes it dangerous, and both of those facts have chapters coming.

But before the shadows, you are owed the sunlight — the ordinary marvellous texture of the school in its young years: the lessons and the ghosts, the forest and its bargains, the first feast of winter, the day the goblin king came to visit his hinges. The next chapters are the happy ones, and I will take my time with them.

A hat learns this above all trades: happiness must be recorded as carefully as sorrow, because it is the thing the sorrow later happens *to*.
## 11. Lessons and the Forest

Let me give you an ordinary day, from the school's fifth year. I have a thousand to choose from. I choose a Tuesday in October, because I remember it entire, and because nothing important happened, which is exactly why it matters.

The castle wakes in layers. First the kitchens, before light — the fires never fully die there, and the elves are at the day's bread while the stars are still working. Then the birds in the owlery, then the ghosts — we will get to the ghosts — then the youngest children, who wake early out of excitement for the first year or two, until the castle becomes ordinary to them, which it never truly does; ask any grown wizard what they dream of, decades later, and it is those corridors. Then the bells; then one hundred and some heads of hair at four loud tables eating Helga's porridge (made in the morning-lit kitchen, as designed; she was right, everyone always tasted the difference); then the day.

Lessons, that Tuesday. In the north courtyard, Godric had the third-years at wandwork, which he taught like swordwork, feet first — "a spell is a thrown thing, and you throw with the whole body, not the wrist" — and the courtyard rang and flashed till the windows above it gave up rattling. He was not, I will confess it for him, a natural teacher of the slow. Patience he had for danger, not for dullness; a child who froze got warmth, but a child who would not *try* baffled him. The fix he found was pure Godric: he made the strugglers his officers, set them to teach the step they had just barely learned to the ones a step behind, and discovered — the school still uses this — that nothing teaches like teaching. By that fifth year his courtyard had a dozen small instructors strutting about correcting stances, and the proudest of them were his own former stragglers.

Below, in the green light, Salazar had the fifth-years at potions. I wish — this is an old hat's honest wish — that every child who has ever shivered at his name could stand one hour at the back of that classroom. No shouting, ever; he considered shouting a confession of failure. A cold clear voice, an absolute standard, and underneath it, visible to anyone watching the eyes and not the reputation, a bottomless attention. He saw everything: the girl whose knife-work shook because her wrist was wrongly taught, and was quietly re-taught; the boy who was ahead of the lesson and bored, and found, next class, a second flask on his bench with a note in green ink: *This one is beyond you. Prove me wrong.* His students did not love him the way Godric's loved Godric, out loud, with nicknames. They loved him the way you love a hard mountain you have climbed: privately, permanently, a little fiercely. At reunions, decades on, it was always the old Slytherin students who wept.

In the towers, Rowena had the seventh-years at spell-architecture, which that morning meant unbuilding one of her own charms on the blackboard to show its joints, then setting the class to improve it. Nobody had improved it in five years of the exercise. That Tuesday — I told you I chose this day for a reason; the reason is coming — a hand went up, and it was Maeve's, and the improvement held. Held, and was better. Rowena looked at the board a long moment, with the whole class not breathing. Then she took up the chalk, wrote at the top *Maeve's Variant*, initialled it, and said: "Copy this down. It is now the standard form." That evening she told Helga it was the finest hour of her teaching life. "The point of the wall I climbed," she said, "was always that someone would one day stand on my shoulders and see past me. Today the school outgrew me by three lines of chalk. We built the right thing."

And Helga, that Tuesday, taught first-years in the walled garden — simple growing-charms, and how to hold a bruised creature so it trusts your hands — and settled two dormitory feuds without either party noticing they had been settled, and spent her free hour where she spent all her free hours: at the long kitchen table that any child in the school could reach by tickling the pear in the painting by the cellar stairs. That was her true classroom. Homesickness, friendship trouble, a letter from home with bad news, the particular misery of being twelve and unchosen — it all came to that table sooner or later, and it was all fed, and it all talked itself out into her listening silence. The elves kept a stool by the ovens they still call the crying stool, and keep polished.

That is a Tuesday, in the good years. Now, the forest — because the forest bounded those years the way the sea bounds an island, and because that same Tuesday ended inside it.

---

The forest across the loch was old when the founders came. Not old like a tower; old like the fens, old like weather — one of the last uncut woods of the north, and everything that needed deep shade to live in had been retreating into it for a thousand years. The founders knew from the first week that it was inhabited. Fires between the far trees; hoofbeats with a rhythm no horse makes; and on the seventh night, at the edge of the trees, by moonlight, watchers: tall figures, still as trunks, men to the waist and horses after, with drawn bows and eyes that took the measure of the newcomers and gave nothing back.

Centaurs. A nation of them, ancient in that wood, star-readers and deep-thinkers, with every reason history could supply to loathe the neighbours they had just acquired. Wizards had driven their kind from wood after wood, south and east, with fire and fence and treaty-breaking. Now wizards were building a fortress across their water, and gathering young, and the stars — the centaurs said later — were bitter with warnings that year.

Everything hung, that first season, on who crossed the water first, and how. And here the fellowship of four showed its quality, because the obvious embassy — Godric, strength meeting strength — was the wrong one, and all four knew it, and the one who went was Rowena. Alone, unarmed, at night, by boat, with instruments. She walked into the trees to the first watcher she could find, laid her star-charts on the moss between them, pointed at the mark that had puzzled her for a month, and asked — the first words ever spoken between Hogwarts and the forest — "What do your people read here? Mine cannot agree."

You do not disarm a scholar-people with gifts or treaties. You disarm them with a real question. The watcher looked at the chart a long time. Then he sent for others. What followed was, by every account, the strangest scholarly conference of the age: a circle of centaurs and one Scottish witch, arguing star-craft on the forest floor until dawn — and out of it, slowly, that winter, came the Concord of the trees and the rock, never written, never broken, its terms so simple every first-year has them by heart within a week. *The wood is theirs. The rock is ours. The water is shared. No wizard cuts or hunts or herds beyond the tree-line; no child crosses it at all; the school's fires stay this side of the shore.* And a clause added by the centaur elder Torvus, wisest of his folk, aimed with a long look at Rowena's instruments: *questions may cross freely, in both directions.*

That clause built more than the rest together. Questions crossed, year after year: Rowena's people carrying charts to the tree-line, Torvus's people appearing, silent as dusk, at the shore with a herb unknown to Helga or a warning of hard weather for the lake boats. The children, told the wood was forbidden, called it — children are precise — the Forbidden Forest, and the name stuck for all time, and does its work: a door marked forbidden is the best-guarded door in a child's mind, and also, of course, the most polished.

Which returns us to Tuesday. Because that October evening, two second-years of Godric's house, dared by a third, went over the water and past the tree-line after a glimpsed unicorn — and the school's machinery of miracles turned quietly over: the gate-stones told Salazar's wards, the wards told Salazar, and before the fear had even properly begun under the darkening trees, the two truants met not wolves but Torvus himself, who walked them in silence — which they described, years later, as the worst punishment of their lives — back to the shore, where all four founders stood waiting in a row.

No child was ever harmed in that forest in the founders' time. The Concord held through everything this book must still tell you — held, in fact, through a thousand years, which is more than can be said for some promises made among the wizards themselves.

Godric's two truants were given to Helga for sentencing, that being the school's custom for large crimes. She fed them, listened to the whole shameful tale, and set the punishment that became legend: every evening for a month they were to sit with Rowena's first-years and teach them the names of the stars — "since," she said, "you were so eager to get closer to them."

The school, in its fifth year. That was the shape of it. Hold the shape in your hands a while, little reader.

Now I must tell you about the sword, and the day the goblin king came to see it — the first cold wind, though nobody on the rock that day knew a wind when it blew through them.
## 12. The Sword of Ragnuk

In the school's seventh spring, word came up from the mountain galleries: Ragnuk the First, king under the eastern peaks, would visit the castle whose iron bones his smiths had forged.

Understand how extraordinary this was. Goblin kings did not visit wizards. The whole long history between the two peoples argued against it — centuries of wizard treaties made in summer and broken by winter, of goblin-made treasures "bought" at wand-point or simply taken, of goblin memory, which forgets nothing, meeting wizard memory, which forgets whatever is convenient. But the building years had built something rare alongside the castle: eleven years of kept bargains, of Helga's remedies and Salazar's potions honestly traded, of Godric going down into the flooded gallery for the king's son when no goblin could pass the drowned spell-seals that wizard raiders had left in the deep centuries before. Ragnuk was hard, proud, and old in judgement; and he had judged, against all his experience, that these particular wizards might be a different kind. He came, I believe, to test that judgement. Kings test things.

He came at the summer solstice with a company of forty: smiths, counsellors, spear-bearers, and his son — the young goblin Godric had carried out of the dark, now grown, silent, watchful. The school received them with honours the founders invented for the day, there being no precedent to follow: the four at the gates on foot; the Great Hall's enchanted ceiling showing, in courtesy, not sky but the deep stone vaults of a mountain hall, star-veined with silver, which made the goblin company stop in the doorway — and made Ragnuk himself look up for a long moment and then look at Rowena, who inclined her head, scholar to scholar. Gift for gift, measure for measure. It began, truly, well.

For three days it went well. The king walked the castle testing his smiths' work with a tap of one long finger, and pronounced it good; the children, warned into unnatural politeness, broke it only once, when a first-year asked the king directly whether it was true goblins could hear gold — and Ragnuk, in the frost-crackle silence that followed, answered gravely that it was, that gold sang in the dark like a bird, and that wizard gold sang loudest, being most often lonely; and the Hall laughed, and the visit warmed. On the second evening there was even, between the king's counsellors and Rowena and Salazar, the beginning of real talk — of a standing peace, of trade in knowledge, of goblin history taught at the school by goblin voices. Nothing like it had been spoken of between the peoples in five hundred years.

On the third evening, Ragnuk asked to see the sword.

It was brought — Godric wore it only on guard-rides by then; it hung in his tower between times — and laid on the high table on a cloth, and the Hall's candles found the rubies and the silver and the deep-cut name of its maker, and Ragnuk stood over it a long time, wearing the expression I have since learned all master-makers wear before their finest work: pride and grief in one face, because the work is finished and will never be made again for the first time.

Then the king said, conversationally, to Godric: "It has served you well, my gift?" — and Godric, who had buried none of his dead yet and was in the summer of his life and of his luck, answered from the heart, meaning every word as pure thanks: "It is the best thing I own."

Own. One word, little reader. The room did not change temperature at once; only the goblin faces stilled, and Salazar — I felt it through Godric's very scalp, the way one feels a friend stiffen beside one — Salazar set down his cup.

You hold, from the building chapter, the two laws that met over that cloth. In goblin law the maker owns the made thing forever; the "gift" of a masterwork is a lending of great honour, and the honoured holder guards and bears it *for* the maker until death returns it home. In wizard law — in moor law, in the law of Godric's whole open-handed life — a gift given is given, and to suggest taking it back is close to insult. Neither man had ever stated his law, because each assumed it was simply the world's. Now the two laws stood up together in one word, in front of forty goblins and four founders and four hundred children.

Ragnuk said, softly: "Own."

And Godric, hearing at last the shape of the ground — he was not subtle, but he was nothing like a fool — did the best thing available to a man standing where he stood: he told the truth. "In my people's way of speaking, King, a gift belongs to the given. If your people speak otherwise, teach me how, for I would not wrong you in your own hall's tongue for a hundred swords."

It nearly saved everything. Ragnuk was silent a long moment, and those near him say something in his old face was moved — the honest ignorance of the answer, perhaps, which goblins meet from wizards so much more rarely than honest theft. What he said, at the end of the silence, was this, and goblin memory and my memory keep it word for word:

"Learn, then, wizard, what no wizard troubles to learn. The sword is my hand's child. While you live, it is your honour and your burden, and I am glad of the arm that bears it, which pulled my son from the dark water. But silver does not die, and you do. When your hand is dust, the sword comes home to my line. That is not a term I set upon you. It is the shape of the world. A made thing returns to its maker, as a river returns to the sea."

To which Godric — and every version agrees, wizard and goblin, though they colour it differently — answered gently: "And in the shape of *my* world, King, the sword will go where all I have goes: to those who come after me, to guard what I guarded. I cannot promise a dead man's hand. I can promise this hand, while it lives, to your people's friendship."

Stalemate — courteous, complete. Two honest men, two whole civilisations of law behind their two sentences, and no bridge. The visit ended next morning with full honours and cooler eyes; the standing peace was not spoken of again; the young prince, at the gates, looked back once, at Godric, with a look nobody has ever translated. The trade in remedies continued. The talk of goblin voices teaching at the school did not.

The founders argued the matter that night in the tower — argued for years afterward, on and off, and it is one of the rare arguments that did not split along the line you would guess. Salazar held that Godric was in the wrong: not for his law, but for never asking about the other's. "You accepted the greatest work of a living maker without once inquiring what the acceptance meant in the maker's world. That is not courage's fault, it is comfort's. The strong may stay ignorant; everyone else studies the strong. They know all our laws, Godric. Name one of theirs you knew before tonight." Rowena, the treaty-scholar, agreed with the diagnosis but saw no cure — the laws were truly incompatible; a wizard could not bind his heirs, a goblin could not unmake the shape of the world; honesty had simply reached the bottom of the well. And Helga said the thing I have carried longest. "You are all speaking of the sword," she said. "Nobody in that hall was grieving about a sword. The king came here hoping we were different, and we are — a little, and only a little, and tonight he measured exactly how much. The sword will keep. What we lost tonight is a marriage nobody proposed."

The sword did keep. It hung in the tower; it did its guarding; the rubies drank the firelight through all the years that follow in this book, and about its later road — the dead man's hand, the vanishing, the thousand-year quarrel that Ragnuk's line keeps warm to this day — I will tell what I know when the story reaches it. The sword outlived every person in this chapter. Made things do. That is rather the goblins' point.

But I have wondered, some slow centuries, about Helga's unproposed marriage — about the school where goblin history was taught in goblin voices, the peace that was three days old when a single word ended it. Doors close so quietly, little reader. Not with slamming; with a solstice morning, full honours, and cooler eyes, while everyone tells everyone the visit went well.

The next door, though — the next door was opened, and opened wide, and it changed the school forever. It is time to tell you about the girl from the mill.
## 13. The Girl from the Mill

I said earlier that eleven Muggle-born children stood on the school's first roll, fetched one by one by Helga. That is true, and it is also, in the way of careful sentences, a curtain. Behind it stands a fight — the first real fight the four founders ever had — and it happened before the first boat ever crossed the loch. It is time to draw the curtain back, because everything in the second half of this book grows from the seed planted in that room.

The fight was in the winter before the school opened, at the long kitchen table in the castle, over a list. Helga had laid it on the table: thirty-one names gathered on her rounds — children of Muggle houses, sparks in the dark, with villages and ages and small notes in her round hand (*sings to calm the cows; moved a cart off her brother; afraid of herself*). She proposed to begin visiting families at once.

And Salazar said no.

I want to give you his argument whole, not the tidy monster the centuries have made of it, because on that first night it was not madness. It was the fen-law, spoken by its last and cleverest son. The school's one absolute possession, he said, was secrecy. Every magical family on the roll had guarded the secret for generations, had paid for it in fear, understood it in the blood. But bring in a child from a Muggle house, and the secret walks out the gate every summer into a village with no stake in keeping it: to proud parents who may whisper to a sister, a priest, a market-day crowd; to cousins who watch the strange child come home each year stranger. "One roll of names," he said, quietly, tapping Helga's list, "is now the most dangerous parchment in Britain. Thirty-one open doors into this castle. I buried my sister through one such door. I will not sleep behind thirty-one of them."

And more: he questioned — here the road begins that ends where you know it ends — whether the school *owed* these children anything. The old families had built the hidden world, paid its taxes of fear, kept its laws. The stray sparks belonged, he said, to no one; nature had scattered them into houses that could not raise them; it was pity, yes, but was it duty? "We have one fortress and finite strength. I would spend all of it on our own before I spend a drop on the accidents of weather."

Silence in the kitchen. And then Helga did the thing for which, if I have taught a thousand years of children anything, I hope she is remembered as long as the castle stands: she did not answer the argument. She reached across the table and turned over the list, where Salazar's tapping finger still rested, so that his finger lay instead on the top name of the reverse side, which she had written there, alone, in larger letters, that morning — knowing, because she always knew, exactly how the evening would run.

The name was Mara's.

"Your sister," Helga said, into the stillness, "was not the fen-folk's loss, Salazar. She was drowned as a *Muggle village's* witch, by neighbours, out of fear that nobody had ever taken away. There was no school to find her, no teacher to stand in that square with her, no letter under her pillow to say: you are not alone, you are not wrong, there is a place. You have told me the story yourself. Now tell me: which list was she on? Ours — or the one you have just called the accidents of weather?" She let the quiet do its work, and then finished, very gently, because she was not fighting him, she was fighting *for* him, and both of them knew it: "Every name on this parchment is somebody's Mara, eleven years old this winter, alone with a fire nobody will teach her to hold. You may vote against the risk, my dear, and you may be right about the risk. But you will do it looking at her name."

Godric said afterward it was the only time in forty years he ever saw Salazar unable to speak. The vote, when it came, was three to one — Godric hot for the children, Rowena cold and clear for them ("a mind is a mind; I decline to sort miracles by their doorsteps") — and Salazar, given the floor for a last word, looked a long time at the turned list and said only: "Record my objection. And record this also: I will hold the wards over those thirty-one doors myself, since no one else here believes doors exist." He kept that word, it must be said, for twenty years, with a vigilance no one else could have sustained. The tragedy of Salazar — write it in the margin, little reader — was never that he loved the school too little.

So the doors opened. And through the seventh of them, in the school's first September, came Edith.

She was the miller's daughter from a wheat-country village three days south — a small brown quick girl, second of six, who had been grinding at her father's mill since she could walk, and whose spark had shown itself, as sparks will, through the family trade: on the night the autumn flood took the millwheel off its axle, the wheel had risen streaming out of the race and set itself back, true to a hair, while the household stood in their nightshirts and the nine-year-old in their midst stared at her own hands. It was the miller himself, oddly enough, who half-guessed and half-dared to hope — millers being practical men who count what they see — and when Helga came, in her healer's shape with her standing welcome, he asked her straight out over the flour-dusted table, before wife or priest could hear: "She's one of *those*, isn't she. The blessed kind." A pause, and then, in the voice fathers keep for the one question that matters: "Is there anywhere on God's earth that can teach her?"

There was, as of that September. She crossed the loch in the fourth boat.

I would love to tell you Edith walked into legend from the first morning. The truth is better, because the truth is about the school and not about luck. Edith arrived two years behind the family-taught children in everything — could not read, knew no star-names, held her borrowed wand like a broom-handle — and on the third night, Maeve of the fens found her crying into her pillow with her boots still on, ready to walk home. What happened next happened because of everything this book has told you so far: because Helga had built a kitchen where a crying child could be fed at midnight; because Rowena had built a library and the rule that any question asked honestly must be answered; because Godric had built the custom of strugglers teaching stragglers, and assigned Edith a small instructor with vast patience; and because — this is the part history refuses to believe, and I was there — because Salazar Slytherin, holding his wards over her dangerous door, thereby read her weekly letters home before the owl flew, and found in them a mind describing the castle's marvels with such piercing mill-practical exactness (*the stairs move on a schedule, I have written it down; the ceiling is not sky, it lies about rain*) that he began, without one word to anyone, leaving books on her bench. In green ink, unsigned: *This one is beyond you. Prove me wrong.*

She proved him wrong for seven years. She was reading by All Hallows, arguing by spring, and by her third year the two-year gap had closed and begun opening the other way. Her particular gift — of course — was the magic of made things: mechanisms, mills, the charmed machinery of the castle itself; by her sixth year she had done what no student and no founder had managed, and mapped the moving stairs entire, and Rowena pinned the map to her own tower wall and wrote under it *Edith's Chart*, and there it hung for three hundred years until the stairs, I am convinced out of pure vanity, changed their pattern.

Understand what Edith became in the school's inner weather. Not a mascot; the school was too busy to pet her, which was its kindness. She became *evidence*. Every year the council met to weigh the next list of Muggle-born names against the standing risk, and every year the argument was a little shorter, because every year she was there — first as the girl acing her examinations, then as the young woman staying on, at Rowena's asking, to teach the charmed mechanics she had half-invented. The first Muggle-born student to become a teacher at Hogwarts. The doors Salazar warded stayed shut; the disaster he stood watch for did not come, year upon year upon year; and I could feel it in him on Sorting mornings, when the stray sparks came up the Hall — not peace, never peace, but a man revising, slowly, against his own oldest grain, the mathematics of the risk.

Then came the summer of the school's nineteenth year, when the mathematics changed back, all at once, in fire — and this book must go where it has always been going.

But not yet. The village of that fire is still, in this chapter, a wheat-country village where a miller is proud of his strange daughter. Leave it golden a little longer. First you are owed a feast — the first Winter Feast, the invention of the ghosts and the cup and half the traditions your own school nights are made of — and I mean to serve it properly, at full table, with the candles lit.
## 14. The Winter Feast

Every tradition you have ever loved was once somebody's improvisation.

Remember that, next time you sit under floating candles at the long tables on the last night before the winter holidays, with the Hall smelling of pine and orange peel and the ceiling snowing upward. It all looks eternal, as if the feast came with the stone. It did not. Nearly all of it was invented in a single December, the school's second, mostly in a panic, and I am going to tell you how, because the story explains something about the founders that the grand chapters cannot: they were never too great to be silly, and half of what has lasted longest began as a joke that worked.

The panic first. The school's first December, nobody had thought about winter at all — there had been a school to invent — and when the holidays came, the plan was simply to send everyone home. Then the weather spoke. A storm shut the mountain passes a week before the leaving-day, and word came by half-frozen owl that the lake road was under snow to a rider's shoulder, and the founders faced arithmetic no plan had covered: ninety children could not go home, some because of the passes, some — this was quieter arithmetic, Helga's kind — because home was a place best not sent back to for a mere fortnight, and a few, the stray sparks, because a magical child's return to a Muggle village in the season of long dark nights and church suspicion needed more preparing than a snowed-in week allowed.

Ninety children, then, stranded on a rock at the edge of the world at midwinter, most of them away from their families for the first turning of the year in their lives. The founders knew exactly what was coming, because it began at once: the castle filled with the specific silence of homesickness, which is louder than noise. By the third evening two dormitories were crying in relays and even Godric's table had gone quiet.

It was Godric who broke first, into the crisis, headlong, as designed. He came down to Helga's kitchen where the other three sat over the problem, threw the doors open, and announced — I was on his head; the idea arrived complete, mid-stride, between the door and the table; that is truly how he worked — "We are going to out-feast their memories. Every child up there is grieving the feast at home. Very well: we will make the feast *here* so enormous, so absurd, so entirely beyond anything a sane household would attempt, that in years to come their children will be homesick for *this*. Helga — everything. Spend everything. I will get the tree."

"The tree," said Rowena.

"The *tree*," said Godric, already leaving.

He came back over the loch at dusk two days later hauling, by a rig of his own devising, a fir tree forty feet high, cut — with scrupulous ceremony and a formal request, the Concord being the Concord — from the permitted margin of the forest, with Torvus's dry blessing: the centaur is recorded to have observed that wizards were the only creatures in the wood who mourned midwinter by decorating its casualties, but that as omens went, a tree carried by a laughing man was among the better ones. It stood in the Great Hall with its head brushing the enchanted ceiling, and the school, pouring in to gape at it, forgot for one clean hour to be homesick. The elves — Pim at their head, scandalised for the second time in this book and enjoying it enormously — declared the tree a fire hazard and then dressed it themselves by ladder and levitation with every shining thing the castle could spare, and Rowena, watching, was heard to say that if the fir was to be a tradition it wanted proper lights, and sat down that same evening and invented the floating candle — the everlasting, dripless, cool-flamed candle of the Hall you know — in, her notes say, four hours, including testing. The notes add, in her hand, a sentence I treasure: *Frivolous application. Possibly my best work.*

The feast itself Helga built in five days with twenty elves and no restraint whatsoever, and it laid down at one sitting the deep grammar of every Hogwarts feast since: the tables groaning past sense, the dishes of every corner of the island so that every child would meet one taste of home — fen eel for the fen children, Godric's moor cheeses, bannocks for the glen, and for the Muggle-born, whose feast-tastes no wizard kitchen knew, Helga had done the simple enormous thing and *asked them*, child by child, in the week before, so that a mill-country girl in her second bewildering year came down to find, among the golden absurdities, her own mother's exact pepper-bread, and had to leave the table for a while, and came back, and ate four of them.

And the cup. You have waited patiently, and here it is, at the head of the high table, where Helga set it that night full of hot spiced cider: her golden cup, the two-handled one from her grandmother's dresser in the valley, which the songs would later hang with mystery. Its actual office, instituted that first Winter Feast and never afterward broken while she lived, was this: it went down the tables, hand to hand, the whole length of the Hall, and each child drank once and named aloud, before passing it, one good thing the year had given them. Ninety children, four founders, a handful of stranded villagers from below the rock, and the cup going round in the candle-light while the storm shouted outside — that was the whole ceremony, and by the time it came back to Helga it had done what it was built to do, which was to make ninety strangers into a household. The elves called it the Mistress's cup and polished it like a relic. They were righter than anyone knew, but its later road — the theft, the long dark, the fire it ended in — belongs to the far side of this book, and tonight is a feast. It stays golden tonight.

The ghosts came with the singing. This is the part I am asked about most, and the truth is gentler than the legends. The castle had been gathering ghosts quietly from its first year — a great warded fortress at the edge of the world is, to the restless dead, what a lit window is to a traveller — but they had kept to the deep corridors, uncertain of welcome, as the dead are. That night the school sang. Badly, gloriously, Godric conducting with the carving-fork — the old turning-of-the-year songs that wizard and Muggle households then still shared, and the sound went down through the stones; and one by one, drawn up out of the dark like divers rising, the ghosts came into the Hall doorway and stood listening. Silver on the threshold. The Hall faltered; a first-year shrieked; and Helga — of course Helga — stood up at the high table and said into the silence, in exactly the voice she used at her own door in the valley: "You are letting the cold in, friends. Sit or sing, but shut the door." And the dead of Hogwarts came in and joined the feast of the living, and have attended every one since, and that is the whole of how it happened. No pact, no summoning, no mystery. Somebody told them to shut the door and meant *from the inside*.

Past midnight, when the children had been poured into their towers heavy as grain-sacks and the candles were burning low over the wreckage, the four founders stayed at the high table, tired in the deep good way, passing Helga's cup a last quiet round among themselves. And Salazar — this is my favourite recorded sentence of that whole bright decade, and I give it to you exactly — Salazar looked down the ruined golden Hall, at the tree, at the drifting candles, at the silver dead chatting with the sleeping-draughted living, and said: "We have been fortifying the wrong things. No wall we have raised will hold this school together as long as tonight will. Mark me: when every stone of our work has been forgotten, they will still be doing *this*." He was right, little reader. A thousand years, and you have sat inside his sentence every December of your school life.

Then he set down the cup, and the talk turned, as it always turned in those years, to the spring lists and the wards and the work; and the good years rolled on, feast after feast, and I have given you four chapters of them now, which is as many as history gave the founders before the weather of the world turned.

One more golden chapter — a birth, and a crown, and a boy with a temper standing at the edge of the picture. Then the wind. I am sorry. I was there, and I am still sorry.
## 15. Helena

In the school's twelfth year, Rowena Ravenclaw astonished everyone, herself first of all, by falling in love.

I will keep the man brief, because history kept him brief. He was a Scottish lord of a small mountain holding, magical blood some generations back and none to speak of in himself; he came up the lake road one autumn about a boundary question, was answered in a quarter of an hour, and stayed — nobody quite understood by what means, least of all Rowena — through the whole of a Highland winter. He was not brilliant. The school braced itself for her verdict on his mind and never got one, which was the verdict. What he had was different, and rarer in her weather: he was *restful*. He treated the most intelligent witch of the age not as a mountain to climb or an oracle to consult but as a rather tired woman who worked too much, and he asked her nothing, and she — who could see the end of every conversation from its beginning — reported to Helga, bewildered, that she could not predict him at all, because he did not want anything from her. "I have married the only unreadable book in Britain," she said the following spring, and it was as close to giddy as the school ever saw her.

The marriage was short and, by every account, happy. He died of a winter fever before their daughter turned three — ordinary death, no mystery, the kind the age dealt out evenly to lords and millers — and Rowena folded the grief away into the same deep drawer as her mother's death and her own predictions, and did not remarry, and would not speak of him, and kept, for the rest of her life, one of his plain woollen cloaks on a hook in her tower study where a portrait might have hung.

The daughter was Helena.

She was born in the castle — the first child ever born inside the walls, which the elves held to be enormous luck and celebrated for a week — and she grew up as the school's own child, with a hundred older siblings every year and four founders for godparents, and I want to be careful here, because the end of her story is so dark that every teller lets the darkness leak backward and paints the childhood black to match. It was not black. She was a bright, quick, laughing child; Godric taught her to fly before Rowena thought it decent; she knew the kitchens' every secret and Pim's every story; she was, for years, simply happy — and she was her mother's whole heart, and here, precisely here, the trouble began, because Rowena Ravenclaw loved her daughter the only way she knew how to love anything: by *investing* in it.

Watch how it happens. Nobody in this chapter is wicked. The mother, who was given a tower and a library and twelve generations of learning, gives her daughter the same, earlier and better: reading at four, runes at seven, the finest education any child of the age received. The daughter is clever — truly clever, Ravenclaw clever — and for a while mother and child meet inside the learning like two friends inside a garden, and those are their best years. But the school around them keeps a ledger the mother never sees. Helena is not a student among students; she is *the founder's daughter*. Every master who teaches her measures her, without meaning to, against the most intelligent witch of the age; every triumph is received as inheritance — *her mother's daughter!* — and every stumble as news. Children her own age go quiet when she does well and quieter when she does badly. And the mother, who can read every mind in Britain except the two she loves, sees her daughter excelling and withdrawing, excelling and withdrawing, and reads it as the family weather — the tower-loneliness she knows so well — and prescribes for it the only medicine she owns: more learning, higher walls, the two of them together in the lamplight. The medicine is the disease. There is no villain in this paragraph, little reader, and it is the saddest one I have ever dictated.

Helena grew, and the gap in her opened the way such gaps do — not as hatred, never as hatred, but as a question she could not put down: *what am I, apart from her?* At seventeen, at eighteen, she was the most learned young witch in the castle and could not hear it said without hearing the unsaid half — *of course she is* — and she began seeking the one thing the school could not grade: notice that belonged to her alone. She found some of it in teaching the little ones, at which she was genuinely gifted, gentler than her mother, better at meeting confusion halfway. She found more of it, and this the whole castle could see except the two people inside it, in the eyes of a young man at the Slytherin table.

The Baron — his name is lost; his title swallowed it, then his crime swallowed his title — came to the school in Helena's own year, out of one of the proud old eastern houses that Salazar's hooded lamp had gathered in the founding winters. He was not subtle and not brilliant; he was strong, dark, generous by fits, mortally proud, and cursed with a temper like deep water — slow to move, and then all at once, entire. Salazar, who read his students the way Rowena read texts, kept him close all seven years, taught him with particular patience, and said of him once, to the other founders, in the flat voice he used for true things: "The boy has one flaw and it will take one lifetime. He does not know how to be refused. Everything else I can teach."

He loved Helena from about the fourth year on, with the whole clumsy weight of his nature — gifts too large, silences too long, devotion trailing her through the corridors like a cloak she had not asked to wear. And Helena — be fair to her, every teller must be fair to her here — Helena was nineteen, and starving on the thin diet of being someone's daughter, and his eyes were the one mirror in the castle in which she appeared *first*. She did not love him. She has said so herself, in the long silver afterwards, to the few she speaks to, and the dead do not bother lying. But she did not send him away either. She kept him — a warmth to stand near, a devotion to sip at — for years, in the cruel innocent way of the young who have never yet seen what a heart does when it finally understands it has been a shelf ornament. Salazar saw it, and warned Rowena, once, stiffly, across the width of all their other quarrels: *your daughter is playing with slow water*. Rowena, by then, heard everything Salazar said through the argument they were already having — we have reached the years where the chapters overlap, little reader; the school's weather and this family's weather darkened together — and she answered coldly that her daughter's affairs wanted no serpent's reading, and the one conversation that might have turned the story was over in nine words.

And the diadem. It is time, for the crown comes into the world in this same twelfth-to-twentieth stretch of years, and the songs have it all backwards. The songs say Rowena made her diadem to crown her own wisdom. She made it for Helena. I watched the making — it was done in the tower study, over two winters, beside the plain woollen cloak on its hook — and it was, in its maker's whole intention, a mother's gift: the deep drawer opened at last, everything she was and knew and could not say across the lamplight, worked into silver and a sapphire the colour of the loch in June. Into it she bound her life's craft — clarity, memory, the quieting of the mind's noise — meaning it as an inheritance, meaning it, in the only tongue she spoke fluently, as *I love you: here is everything: outshine me*. And on the band, in the old letters, she set her own famous device, the sentence the school had teased her with for decades, half motto and half joke: about wit and treasure — you know the words; your own Ravenclaws still wear them.

She never gave it. That is the hinge, and there is no reason for it that fits in a sentence, which is how you know it is true. The right occasion was always one term away: when Helena finishes her studies; when she is settled; when things between us are easier. The gift grew heavier as it waited, the way withheld gifts do, and became — kept in its case in the study both women passed daily — a third presence in every lamplit silence: the mother's masterpiece, made *for* the daughter and shown to her and never yet placed in her hands, until Helena could no longer see the love in it at all, only the verdict: even her gift to me is a monument to herself.

You know, roughly, what is coming. Not yet. Years yet. On the night this chapter closes — a spring night in the school's twentieth year — the diadem is in its case, and Helena is twenty, laughing at something Edith has said down at the kitchen table, and the Baron is watching her from the doorway, and Rowena is in her tower drawing the next marvel, and the four founders still dine together weekly, though the dinners have grown quieter, and the school stands golden on its rock between the mountains and the water, still — for one more chapter — in the good years.

Look at it there a moment, all lit up.

Now the wind.
## 16. The Quiet Argument

The wind, when it came, did not come as a storm. It came as a lock.

In the school's twentieth spring, on an ordinary Thursday, the great gates of Hogwarts refused a visitor for the first time in their existence. The visitor was a tinker, a mender of pots, known on the lake road for years — and the gates, which weighed intentions, weighed his and went cold under Salazar's touch when he came down to see. The tinker mended pots. He also, it proved, collected talk for money, and had been collecting it for a season: which farms sent children up the lake road, which villages the healer visited, who paid for goods in old coin. Somewhere south, someone was buying knowledge of the school. Salazar questioned him — alone, with the gates shut, by methods he did not report and nobody asked after, which was itself a small lock clicking somewhere in the fellowship — and let him go at dusk, memory thinned, pack mended free of charge. Then he came up to the tower study where the other three waited, and stood a moment in the doorway, and said:

"It has begun. I have been telling you for twenty years that the world would notice us, and it has noticed. Now we will find out which of us built for weather."

What had changed, out in the world? Nothing sudden; everything slow. The founders' generation had been born into scattered danger — a village fear here, a burning year there, local as thunderstorms. But through the very decades the school rose, the weather of the Muggle world was organising. Kingdoms were knitting together; and where power organises, fear organises with it. The old wild talk of water-witches was becoming, in the south, in the growing towns, something with clerks: lists, questions asked in courts, preachers who travelled with letters of authority. Wizardkind, which had survived a thousand thunderstorms, was watching the first weather-front assemble — the long front that would, in the coming centuries, drive our whole world under: the Statute your age lives inside was its final treaty. The founders could not see that far. They only saw the sky go strange. But Salazar had been reading that sky since a summer evening by a flat polite river, and to him it was not strange at all. It was familiar. It was the sky over the market square, scaled up to the size of Britain.

So began what the histories, following my own account, call the Quiet Argument — the two years of council evenings in which the fellowship of four, still whole, still loving, argued itself slowly toward its crack. I sat through every session on Godric's head. I would rather have sat through battles.

Understand the terrible thing about the Quiet Argument: everyone was right.

Salazar's proposals came first, and they were, at the start, pure sense. The tinker had shown a door; Salazar counted doors. The village of Hogsmeade knew too much and chattered; let it be brought inside the wards, formally, oath-bound — done, and no one objected, and the village stands oath-bound to this day. The lake road showed the school's traffic to any watcher on the southern hills; let arrivals come by night and by water — done; you yourself, little reader, crossed the loch in darkness for exactly this reason, in a boat whose custom is older than any castle ghost. The owl-post flew readable; Rowena ciphered it. Sensible lock after sensible lock, and the school grew safer with each, and if the Quiet Argument had stopped there, this book would be a short happy history of the world's first magical school.

It did not stop there, because each autumn brought the lists — Helga's lists, the stray sparks, the Muggle-born children — and each autumn Salazar stood over them longer. The Muggle-born were not, he granted now, a betrayal waiting to happen; twenty years and Edith had settled that; he was not a man who argued against arithmetic. The problem had moved. Each name on the list was a *thread*: a magical child tied to a Muggle family, a family tied to a village, a village tied — this was the new fact — to clerks and courts and travelling men with letters of authority. Every thread ran both ways. Down the thread, in the worst case, came watchers to the school. But up the thread — and here his voice would drop, and here, I must record, the other three began to listen with their eyes down, because the case had weight — up the thread, into an unprotected Muggle house, went the school's fire: a family that knew, and could be made to tell; a mother who could be questioned by men with lists; a village that could watch a strange child come home each summer, in a Britain where watching was becoming a paid profession. "We are not only risking ourselves," he said, one November night in the school's twenty-first year. "We are hanging our address around the necks of milk-cow families with no wall, no wand, and no idea what they hold. The kindest thing we do to them is also the cruellest. I know how this ends. I have known since I was fifteen."

And Helga — Helga, who had fetched every one of those children herself, who knew every mother's name — Helga answered him each time with her own unanswerable ledger: thirty years of visits and not one fire; the protections she wove over every doorstep; the plain fact, which she would deliver while carving, that the children existed whether or not the school came for them, sparks in the dark either way, and that every list left unfetched was a row of Maras left standing in the doorway of the fear alone. "You count the danger we carry to them," she said. "Count the danger we leave them in. My arithmetic has faces, Salazar. Yours has weather. Faces first."

Rowena sat between — genuinely between, the only years of her life she could not solve for the answer — building compromises the way engineers shore a cracking wall: the fostering scheme, whereby Muggle-born children's families gained a standing watcher from the hidden world; the memory-hedges, her own design, that let a frightened parent hold the secret safe even under questioning; the summer-houses, half school, half refuge, where a child could pass the dangerous seasons. Good engineering, all of it; some of it outlived the founders by centuries. But shoring holds a wall; it does not stop the ground moving. And the ground was moving, because beneath the policies, unspoken, the two arithmetics were becoming two *worlds*: Helga's school, which existed for the children — all of them, wherever magic scattered them — and Salazar's school, which existed for the survival of a people. On the sunny days those were the same school. The sky was no longer sunny.

Godric, through those two years, was the strangest figure at the table: silent. The histories cannot make sense of it — Godric, silent? — but I sat on his head, and I will tell you what the silence was. It was a man holding a door shut with his back. He agreed with Helga with his whole hot heart. He heard the sense in Salazar with his whole trained judgement. And he knew — because he knew his friend down to the bedrock, the fair-ground, the mud, the thirty years — he knew what none of the others yet believed: that this was not, for Salazar, a policy question that could lose gracefully. It was the river. It had always been the river. Somewhere under the councils, the fifteen-year-old was still standing on the empty shore, and the day the school voted, finally and forever, to keep hanging its fire around Muggle necks, that boy would conclude — with the cold totality Godric had watched him apply to every conclusion of his life — that this castle, too, was a family that would be wise, and careful, and right, and would do nothing. And then he would act. Godric did not know what the act would be. He only knew that his friend had begun, in the twenty-first year, to spend nights in the deep places of the castle again, alone, as in the building time; that the pale face at council had taken on the inward look of a man already keeping a completed decision company; and that when he, Godric, said once, lightly, testing, "You are digging again, old friend — what grows down there?", Salazar had smiled the mist-morning smile, a beat too late, and answered: "Foundations. I like to know what we stand on."

I felt Godric choose, that night, not to press. Friends do not audit friends. It was the most loving mistake in this book, and the most expensive.

For the completed decision Salazar was keeping company, down in the drowned dark below the drowned dark, under the deepest wards he ever wove, had a shape by then. It had a door that opened to one language only. It had, coiled small and new and patient inside it, a guardian.

We have come to the part of the story I was made too late to prevent and too early to forget. Turn the page, little reader. The Burning Year is upon the school, and after it, nothing in this book stands where it stood.
## Interlude: The Ghosts' Parliament

The second borrowed voice belongs to the dead.

You met them at the first Winter Feast — the silver figures in the doorway, told to shut the door from the inside. What you have not been told is how the ghosts of Hogwarts govern themselves, and why there are so many, and what the castle looks like to someone who will be attending it forever. My informant here is the silver lady older than the school — she who declined to be nearby when the new cold woke; she has haunted this rock since before it had walls, and she granted me, once a century or so, what she calls an audience and I call a chat. This is assembled from ten of them.

*

You must first understand, hat, that the dead did not come here for the castle. We came for the same reason the living came: because it is warded, and warm in its way, and *going concern*. A ghost haunting a ruin is a candle in an empty room — burning, certainly, but for whom? The unfinished dead are unfinished precisely because we could not stop attending to the world; do not ask us to attend to it from a hilltop with sheep. We require an audience. We require, though your kind puts it less honestly, employment.

And the school, almost alone among the works of the living, employs us.

Think what a ghost is good for, hat. We do not sleep. We do not forget. We cannot be bribed with anything the living value, and we have seen every folly the castle contains attempted before, usually twice, usually by someone's grandfather. In the first century the masters understood this by accident; by the third it was custom; by now it is constitution. The dead of Hogwarts watch the stairs at night, and mind the youngest ones who cry into their pillows in October — a ghost is a strangely comforting listener for a homesick child, being proof that worse things than a bad first term are survivable, in a manner of speaking. We remember where everything is buried, in every sense, and which walls are original, and what the founders actually said, against what the histories have improved them into saying. A living institution with a permanent memory attached: no other school has managed it, because no other school convinced its dead they were wanted. That was the Welsh one's doing, with her open door at the feast — you were there; you know. We are wanted, so we stay. Your kind builds monuments to keep the dead present and then cannot bear to speak with them. This place set us a chair at the table. It is not complicated, hat. It is only rare.

As for governance — yes, there is a parliament. You may laugh; the living always laugh; then they discover it has met continuously for nine hundred years and holds the castle's longest unbroken record of proceedings, and they stop laughing and send a clerk to consult it. We convene at the dark of each moon, in the disused chapel under the north range, all the dead of the rock in attendance — attendance is compulsory, which the living find amusing from beings who can pass through walls, until they understand that for us *belonging somewhere on purpose* is the entire order of business. We have a Speaker — the office rotates by century — and rules of debate of exquisite uselessness, and we argue. What do the dead argue about? What the living argue about, minus the property: precedence, memory, manners, and love. Whether the new ghost of a drowned fisherman may take the east tower walk that has belonged to the Grey Lady these two hundred years. Whether a certain poltergeist — no, hat, I will not speak of him, we have voted him *outside the fellowship of the properly dead* on four hundred occasions and he attends anyway — whether he can be contained, expelled, or merely survived. Whether to tell the headmaster of the year what the walls have heard, or let the living learn at the living's pace, which is the perennial great question, and the answer of nine centuries is: we tell what endangers children; the rest earns its keeping. The dead are the only faction in this castle with no ambitions, hat. It makes us the safest counsel in it, and the least consulted. Note it in your book. The living will find it uncomfortable, which is the mark of the true entries.

You will want the two of them. Everyone wants the two of them. The Baron and the Lady — my successor in the east walk, as it happens; I ceded it; she needed the window and I had had two centuries of it. Very well, but only what is mine to give.

They came within a season of each other, silver out of the south, and the parliament received them as we receive all our kind — with the Asking. Every new ghost is asked, once, formally, at their first dark-of-moon: *what holds you?* Not to pry, hat. Because the answer is the ghost's charter; it tells the fellowship what the newcomer will need of us for the next thousand years. Most answers are small and terrible: a word unsaid, a door unlocked too late, a child unfound. The Lady stood before the parliament of the dead, in the chapel her grandmother's generation had known, and gave the shortest answer in our rolls. She said: "A question my mother asked too late, and I answered too cruelly. I am holding the true answer, sirs, until it is called for." And the Baron, a month behind her, chained head to foot in iron of no forge this world keeps — asked the same Asking, in the same chapel, with the whole fellowship silver-still — said only: "Her leave to be gone."

Her leave to be gone. Nine hundred years, hat, and neither charter has closed. She holds an answer no one living has yet asked her for — your seven books came close, I gather, and drew off a lesser secret; the deep one keeps. He holds a sentence only she can lift, and will not ask her to lift it, because asking would be one more demand from a man whose crime was demanding; so he wears the chains as a promise to demand nothing more, forever. And she knows what he holds, and cannot yet will its lifting, and so they move about one castle on two orbits, never touching. The eleven words between them in a millennium — you mentioned them once; you count better than we do — I was present for seven of them, and they are not for the book. I will tell you the shape only: they were on the night the Chamber matter ended, at the top of the east walk, at dawn, and it was she who spoke, and the seven I heard were the beginning of a leave-taking that neither of them has yet had the courage to finish. Even the dead ripen slowly here. It is that kind of castle.

One thing more, and then my walk wants me. You asked, last audience, why I stayed when the rock had no walls — what a ghost haunts in a wilderness. I have decided to answer, since you are writing everything down at last, and since it bears on your founders more than they knew. I stayed, hat, because this rock was always a threshold. Long before the pig, long before your four — the old peoples of this land knew the loch and the stone above it as a *thin* place, where the two sides of things lean close. The forest folk know it still; ask them why their nation never left. Your founders thought they chose this ground for the wards and the water. Perhaps. Or perhaps thresholds call to threshold-people — to the ones standing between worlds: between magic and the mundane, between the living and the dead, between the child at the shore and the castle of lit windows. Everyone in your book, hat, is standing in a doorway. It is the most doorway-ridden story I have ever heard told. I merely note that they built it on the biggest doorway in the north, and that the dead noticed, even if the architects did not.

Now go and finish your chapters. And hat — when you write of the dead, write us as citizens, not as weather. We have kept this school nine hundred years of nights. Somebody may as well say so in print.

*

Somebody now has, my lady.

We return to the living — to the last golden chapter before the wind, and then into it. The parliament of the dead will keep the watch while we read. It always does.

## 17. The Burning Year

The school's twenty-second year is called, in the old records, simply the Burning Year, and no master has ever had to explain the name to a student. Some names teach themselves.

It began far from the rock, in the wheat country three days south — in Edith's country — where the harvest failed. Not dramatically; wet, then blighted, then thin; the granaries went into winter half full, and hunger, that oldest recruiting officer, went round the villages all the dark months whispering that someone must be to blame. By spring the whisper had found its preacher — a travelling man with letters of authority and a gift, the records agree, for weeping in the pulpit — and the preacher had found, in village after village, the oldest door of all: *there are those among you who are not as you are. The blight is their tithe. Give them to God and the wheat will stand.*

That country was thick with threads, as Salazar would say. Two generations of Helga's rounds had passed through it; three of the school's children had come out of it, Edith first and brightest; her family still ground corn there, prosperous now, mildly mysterious to their neighbours — the strange daughter who had gone north to some far convent school and come back each summer straighter and stranger, and had lately stopped coming, being grown and a teacher; the mill that never seemed to fail, even in the blight year. *Especially* in the blight year. Little reader, you have read sixteen chapters of this book. You do not need me to draw the rest.

Word reached the school, as bad word does, too slowly and then all at once. A market-day arrest; an assize; the preacher weeping; the miller's household named — for the mill's unnatural luck, for the vanished daughter, for a neighbour's memory, thirty years cold, of a millwheel climbing out of a flood-race by itself. Helga was on the road within the hour of the owl, with Godric beside her, and Edith between them, white as flour, whom nothing said in the tower could have held — it was her mother, her father grown old, her brothers. Salazar came down to the gates as they mounted. What he said there has been reported a hundred ways. I was on Godric's head, and I will give it exactly, because its exactness is the whole of him: he took the reins of Helga's horse a moment, and said, quietly, "Bring them all out. The whole family, the whole household, whatever it costs, and burn the thread behind you. Do not stop to be seen. Do not stop to be right. I will hold the school." And then, to Edith alone, in a voice I had never heard him use, the voice of the fifteen-year-old: "Do not arrive at dusk. I arrived at dusk."

They rode three days. They arrived — because the world is not a story — half a day late.

Not the worst late. I must be careful here, both for truth and for you; this book does not look away, but neither does it linger. The assize had moved faster than law, the preacher's weeping having done its work; of the household, the men were held in the tithe-barn for trial; the mother, who had answered the questioners proudly — *my daughter is where God's gifted go; you could not spell her lessons* — the mother they had already taken to the riverside. The river-test, the old one, the stupidest one. And here alone the Burning Year showed mercy, if you can bear the shape its mercy took: the crowd at the water had botched their knots and their courage both, and the mother, half drowned, had been dragged out breathing by two of her own terrified neighbours — the fear having broken, as it sometimes did, on the plain sight of a known face going under — and hidden in a hay-barn, alive, when the riders came.

What the wheat country remembered afterward, for three generations, in tales that curdled slowly into legend, was this: that at the trial of the miller's men there had appeared at the back of the assize a red-haired giant in outlandish dress, and beside him a round grey woman with a healer's bag who looked at the preacher — only looked at him — until he lost the thread of his weeping; that when the verdict came in as it was always going to come in, the giant had walked forward through the crowd as through standing corn, broken the prisoners' irons with his hands, and invited the assize, with terrible courtesy, to take them back; that the men-at-arms who tried woke at sunset in the millpond, dry, unharmed, and unable ever afterward to say what had happened in between, except that it had involved, and here every teller agreed and every listener scoffed, *a sword singing*; and that the whole household of the mill — men, mother, cousins, the very cat — was gone by morning, as if the ground had eaten them, leaving flour in the bins and not one bootprint on the road.

Gone north, of course. Gone up the lake road by night, gone across the black water in the silent boats, gone inside the walls — the whole thread, as instructed, burned behind them. The school took them in as it had taken the stranded villagers of the first winter, and Helga set the old miller to a hopeless project of water-mills for the castle that kept his hands busy to the end of his days, and the mother — the proud mother, who had boasted of her daughter to men holding a list — the mother sat by the hospital-wing fire wrapped in blankets for a month, and it was Salazar Slytherin, the school being curious about nothing so much as this, who came most evenings and sat with her, mostly silent, sometimes asking, in his flat quiet way, what the questioners' questions had been, word for word, which she alone found she could bear to answer, because he alone did not flinch while listening.

You would think — the school thought — that the rescue would heal something. A household saved, where a sister had been lost; the fifteen-year-old's ride, run again, and this time won. I record what happened instead, because the Burning Year's true burning was here, and it burned inward, in council, a week after the mill family passed the gates.

Salazar rose with the year's lists in his hand — the next autumn's names, eleven of them, Muggle-born, gathered as always by Helga's rounds — and he did not argue the old arguments. He laid beside the lists a second parchment: his own, compiled through the spring by his own watchers, and it was a map of the wheat country with fourteen more villages marked. "The preacher has letters patent and a route," he said. "The route is the same as ours. Not by malice — by logic: he hunts where the sparks are, and we harvest where the sparks are, and from this year forward every door Helga knocks upon has been knocked upon, or will be within the twelvemonth, by the other knock. The weather I promised you has arrived. So. I am done proposing hedges." He looked around the table, at each of the three faces, slowly, and I felt Godric's scalp go cold under me, and Salazar said: "I propose the wall. No new threads. No more children of Muggle houses — not this autumn's eleven, not any autumn's, not while the burning weather stands. The ones inside are ours and I will defend them to my last breath, Edith and her mill-folk first of all. But the door closes. A people that cannot close its own door in a storm is not a people, it is a hearth waiting for rain."

That is where the quiet ended, little reader. What followed was not quiet, and it lasted, on and off, for two more years, and it has never entirely stopped; you can hear its echo in your own common room, whichever one is yours. Helga's answer that night — the eleven names, read aloud, one by one, ages and villages, into a silence nobody dared break — belongs to the school's treasury of terrible moments, and I will not paraphrase it; some things are what they are only at full length in the voice of the person who dared. The vote did not come that night. Everyone at the table knew what everyone would vote. Nobody yet dared make it real.

They had, though none of them knew the number, eighteen months of fellowship left.

And Salazar went down that same midnight to the drowned dark below the drowned dark, where the completed decision waited, coiled small and patient and growing. And for the first time in this book, I must follow him down there — because the next chapter belongs to the part of him nobody at the council table ever saw, and a hat has promised you honesty, and honesty, in the end, is going to have to look at what love built in the dark.
## 18. What Salazar Loved

Before I take you down the stairs, I must tell you about the child in the reed-boat. The histories do not contain her. Only three beings ever knew, and two are dead, and the third is a hat, and I have carried her long enough.

It was the winter after the Burning Year — the vote still unmade, the council still circling it, the fellowship holding its breath. Salazar had watchers in the south by then, threads of his own, and one of them sent word of a thing his map had predicted: the preacher's route and Helga's route had crossed. A marsh-country village at the mouth of the eastern rivers — not on any school list, too small, too poor — had found itself a spark: a fisher family's girl, six years old, far too young for any letter, who had been seen in the reeds at dusk, becalmed water moving around her boat against the current, moving *for* her, the way water will for the very young ones before they know shame. Six years old. Five years too young for the school to come for her, in a village the burning weather would reach by spring.

The council could have done nothing. Understand that with your whole mind: under every rule the four had built together, there was nothing to be done. Too young to school, too Muggle to move, family unbreachable — six-year-olds cannot keep secrets, so the parents could not be told, and a child cannot be taken from untold parents; that is not rescue, that is the very thing we feared done in our own name. Helga's method needed years of rounds and trust; there were no years. The case fell, whole, through every floor the school possessed.

Salazar went himself. Alone, hooded, by water, in January, telling no one — I know because Godric did not know, and I know what Godric knew.

What he did there took him nine days, and it was this. He did not touch the child. He went instead through that village like winter fog, night upon night, and he re-taught it its own memories. The elder who had seen the boat and the obedient water now remembered an otter — an otter of unusual size, a wonder-tale worth telling, worth *replacing* the other tale. The two boys who had watched from the bank remembered the otter too, and remembered being afraid of the deep-water eddy there, which now had a name and a small dread about it, so that watchers would not linger by it at dusk again. The child's own mother, who had begun — the watcher's letter had caught this, and it had brought Salazar out of his tower in a night — who had begun to be *frightened of her own daughter*, and to pray over her sleeping, and to speak of the hollow-eyed saint at the river shrine: the mother now remembered a dream in which the saint had told her the girl was luck-touched, water-blessed, a keeper of nets, and that her strangeness was holy and must be *hidden from priests*, who would take her away to a far convent. Fear did not leave that house. Salazar could not remove fear; no one can; he knew that better than any man alive. What he did was what he always did: he *aimed* it. The mother's fear now guarded the secret instead of guarding against the child. The village's watching eyes now watched an eddy. The thread that would have led the preacher's route to a six-year-old in a reed-boat had been picked apart, strand by strand, and rewoven into ordinary marsh-tale.

And then — this is the part I carried alone, this is the reason the chapter exists — then he did the thing that had no strategy in it. Before he left, on the last night, he went out on the black water to where the child's family's nets were staked, and he called the marsh-snakes, in the tongue that was his birthright and his sister's, and he set them a charge, the long patient kind of charge that serpent-kind will hold for a bribe of warm stones and safe nesting: *watch the small one in the reed-boat. While she is small, let no harm come at her from the water. When she is grown, if the water ever again moves for her where eyes can see, one of you will find the fen-folk and say so.* A guardian-web of small cold faithful watchers, woven around a Muggle-born child he would never meet, whose name he never learned, in the same months — the same *months*, little reader — that he stood in council demanding the door be closed on her kind forever.

He told no one. There was no report to the council, no ledger of it, no green-ink note. I know because of a single seam of luck: years later, at the end of everything, in the last conversation this book will record between him and Godric, he spoke of her — one sentence, you will hear it when we arrive there — and I was on Godric's head, and a hat forgets nothing. That is my whole evidence, that and one other thing: the fen-watchers' tradition, which persisted for centuries after him, quietly, among the marsh families of the east — the custom of *listening to snakes about children* — which historians of our world have never satisfactorily explained.

Now. What do we do with this, you and I?

The easy tellings do easy things with it. One kind says: see, the monster had a soft heart, how sad, how redeeming. Another kind says: see, the hypocrisy — he saved one to soothe himself while voting to abandon them all. Both tellings are cheap, and you have not read eighteen chapters to be served cheapness. Here is what I believe, at the bottom of a thousand years of turning it over.

Salazar Slytherin loved the children of magic — all of them, the listed and the unlisted, the pure-blooded heirs and the sparks in the reed-boats — with the fiercest and most damaged love in this story. That is not the mitigation of what he became. It is the *mechanism* of it. Watch the gears. Love, in him, had been fused young to one conviction: that love without walls is how you lose what you love — that his family's open trading childhood had killed Mara as surely as the water did. So every motion of his heart toward a child arrived pre-translated into the only safe language he owned: protection, concealment, control. He could not open a door for the reed-boat girl; opening doors was, in his deepest grammar, the act of harm. So he did what his grammar allowed at full strength: he closed every door around her, gently, invisibly, and set serpents at the locks. It was tenderness. It was genius. It was also — say it plainly, hat — it was a man deciding, alone, at night, without appeal, what a village would remember, what a mother would believe about her own daughter, and what a child would be permitted to know of herself, forever. For her good. Entirely for her good. There is a name for the place where that road ends, and there is a chamber at the end of that road, and we are nearly at its door.

Because here is the turn of the gears that the reed-boat winter makes visible, and I want you to see it happen, since no one at the council table ever did. The same months. The same man. Upstairs, in the lamplight, he was losing — honourably, three to one, again and again — the argument to close the school's door. And every defeat upstairs sent him downstairs with the same conclusion tightening in him, the conclusion of the boy on the empty shore: *they are good, and they are wise, and they are right, and they will do nothing — so the walls that matter must be built where votes cannot reach them.* The village in the marshes was such a wall. The watchers in the south were such a wall.

And below the drowned dark, under wards no other living wand could have unpicked, the last wall was nearly finished: a chamber only his blood-tongue could open, and in it a guardian — not a web of small cold faithful watchers this time, but one great one, the greatest his tradition knew, bred in secret from an egg, growing in the warm dark year by year, patient as its master. A king of serpents, set a charge, the long kind, the kind held for centuries: *if ever the day comes that the school itself falls to the fear — if the burning weather breaks the gates and my people's children stand where Mara stood — wake, and be their last wall.*

That is what I believe it was for. I said at the start of this book that I would tell you honestly what I know and honestly what I do not, and here is the boundary stone: I never went down those stairs; no one did; what the chamber was *for* I have assembled from the man, not the deed. What history knows is only what the charge became, a thousand years on, in the keeping of an heir who inherited the walls without the love: a weapon against the very children it was — I believe, I choose to believe, I have staked this whole chapter on believing — built to defend.

Love, translated into the grammar of fear, compiles into monsters. That is the sentence I came down these stairs to give you. Carry it carefully; it is heavier than it looks; and it is not done working, because upstairs, in the lamplight, the vote could not circle forever.

In the spring of the school's twenty-fourth year, it stopped circling.
## 19. The Door Below

You now know more than the castle knew. Remember that as you read this chapter, because this chapter is about the castle almost finding out — and about the strange, sad machinery by which people who love each other decide not to know a thing.

The signs were there, in that last year of the fellowship. I have spent centuries collecting them, the way you reassemble a broken bowl found in a field, and I will lay the pieces out for you in the order the castle met them.

The first piece belonged to Edith. Of course it did — the school's own engineer, the mapper of the moving stairs, the woman who knew the castle's mechanisms the way a miller knows his mill. It was Edith who noticed, in the autumn after the Burning Year, that the castle's water was wrong. Not badly wrong: wrong by a miller's margins. The springs under the rock fed the wells and the pipework — much of that pipework Salazar's own design from the building years, he being the one who understood drowned country — and Edith, re-charting the lower systems for a project of her water-mill-mad old father's, found flows that did not sum. A spring that gave less than it should. A warm current in a cold main. A pipe, marked on her own three-year-old charts, that now ran behind new-faced stone. She brought it, naturally, to the master of the deep levels. Salazar received her in the green-lit study, heard her out with the absolute attention that made his students love him, took her chart, complimented the work — and told her the truth. That is the detail I need you to sit with. He did not lie. He said: "The deep rock is mine to keep, and some of what I keep there is not for charts. Leave the lower levels to me, and chart nothing below the third cellar. That is not a request, mistress engineer." And Edith — who owed him her green-ink books, her seven years, half her mind — Edith bowed, and burned the page, and kept his counsel for sixty years. She told me at the end of her life, an old woman talking to a hat on a shelf because everyone else who would understand was dead: "I knew there was a door down there. I chose to believe I knew the man." She was not wrong to believe it, little reader. That is the horror of this chapter. Everyone who chose trust chose reasonably.

The second piece belonged to the ghosts. The dead feel the deep places; they drift there, in the small hours, below where the living go. That winter, the castle's ghosts stopped drifting below the third cellar. No decree, no discussion — the dead do not hold councils — simply, one by one, they found reasons to prefer the towers, the way birds leave a wood before weather. One of them, a silver lady older than the school who had haunted the rock before the rock had walls, said a thing at the Winter Feast that stopped conversation along ten feet of table. Someone had asked her, in feast-courtesy, whether the cellars kept her well. She looked down the Hall with the long sight of the dead and said: "There is a new cold under the old cold. It is asleep. I decline to be nearby when it is otherwise." Then she passed through the wall, as the dead do when they consider a subject closed. It became a school joke by February. Rowena heard the joke — Rowena heard everything — and did not laugh, and filed it, and did nothing. She had, that winter, a daughter visibly unravelling and a fellowship visibly cracking, and a scholar's deepest habit besides: a single anomalous data-point is noted, not acted upon. Reasonable. Everyone in this chapter is reasonable.

The third piece belonged to the children, because it always does; children live nearer the floor. Among the young Slytherins of that year there arose one of those whispered dormitory legends that masters never hear until decades later: that on certain nights, if you pressed your ear to a certain wall of a certain lower corridor, you could hear the castle breathing — a long slow hush, hush, under the stone, slower than any sleeper. Second-years dared each other down to listen. Some heard nothing. Some heard it. One boy — his name survives in the legend itself, Aldous's youngest brother — swore until he was grey that on the coldest night of that winter, ear to the stone, he had heard the breathing *pause*, as if the deep thing had noticed the small warm ear against its wall — and then resume, slower. The dormitory legend had it that the castle dreamed, and that the dreams were kept in the cellars, and that Master Slytherin fed them. Children, little reader, are the best historians the world has: wrong in every detail and right in the shape.

And the fourth piece belonged to Godric, and it is the piece that cuts.

I told you he had chosen, in the twenty-first year, not to press — that friends do not audit friends. But I sat on his head, and I will tell you what that choice cost to maintain, because the histories paint him either blind or betrayed, and he was neither. He was a man doing sums he hated. He knew Salazar was building below — knew it as you know a thing about a brother: from the hours, the mud on the hem, the extra weariness, the too-smooth answer. He knew the wards on the deep stairs had changed; he had walked down one night in the Burning Year, on an honest errand, and met a barrier that knew him — that was the point, it *knew* him, it was courteous to him personally, it turned him around with an apology woven into it, and there is no more precise way for one wizard to say to another *not even you*. He stood a long time on that stair. I felt the whole war in him. He could have broken the barrier; it would have cost him something, but he could have; he was Godric Gryffindor, and it was his castle too. And what held his hand was not weakness, and it was not innocence. It was a piece of arithmetic that I heard him do, in the dark, in so many words, and I have never once repeated until this page: *If I break this, I break him. He has been refused by every door in his life except mine. Whatever he keeps down there, he keeps because the vote is going against him and he cannot bear to be defenceless again. If I force it into the light, I prove to him that no one — no one, not even I — will ever let him hold one locked thing. And then I lose him. The vote will pass. The door will be his loss. Let him keep one cellar, and I will keep the man.*

Let him keep one cellar, and I will keep the man. He turned and went back up the stairs.

For a thousand years, every teller of this story has stood on that stair with Godric and voted. The stern tellers say: there, that night, the Chamber of Secrets was ratified — that trust without verification is just abdication wearing a warmer cloak, and that a headmaster's first duty was to the children above the floor, not the friendship below it. The gentle tellers answer: he could not have known; the barrier could have hidden anything, a library, a laboratory, a grief; you do not smash your brother's one locked drawer on suspicion, or love is impossible and every family is a search-party. Both tellings are right. That is not a rhetorical flourish, little reader; I mean it as flatly as I have ever meant anything in this book. Both are right, and they cannot both be acted on, and Godric had one night, one stair, and no hat that could talk yet, and he chose the man over the cellar.

I have wondered all my long life whether I would have told him, if the four had made me two years earlier than they did. I contain him; I contain what he knew; I contained, later, enough of Salazar's patterns to have guessed the rest. I believe I would have told him. I believe it would have broken the fellowship two years sooner and emptied the chamber a thousand years earlier. I believe the arithmetic of that trade — friendship against futures — comes out differently for a hat than it does for a man, because a hat is not lonely, and loneliness is the thumb on every scale in this entire book.

So the door below stayed shut, and known-and-not-known, in the way of families. And upstairs, the fellowship came, at last, to the night it could no longer circle.

The vote was called in the spring of the twenty-fourth year. It was Salazar himself who called it — formally, in the old words of their founding compact, which no one had ever invoked, because invoking them meant *binding*: whatever the four decided under the compact bound the school forever, past argument, past amendment, past death.

He meant it to be forever, one way or the other. He got his wish, little reader. Both ways.
## 20. Three Against One

The compact vote was set for the first night of May, in the Great Hall, after the students slept. Four chairs at the high table. No masters, no clerks, no record but memory — and one hat, on one head, taking the only minutes that survive.

They came separately. That was new, and it was the night's first grief. For twenty-four years the four had arrived at councils in twos and threes, mid-argument, mid-joke, Helga carrying something warm. Tonight each door of the Hall opened alone: Rowena first, with papers she would never look at; then Salazar, from below, pale as his collar; then Helga, who stopped in the doorway a moment and looked at the spaces between the chairs — measuring, I think, with a housekeeper's eye, how far apart four people can sit at one table before it is no longer one table; then Godric, from the walls, where he had been walking since dusk, with me on his head.

I will give you the shape of the night and the words that must be exact. The rest I will not reconstruct; grief invents dialogue, and I promised you a hat's minutes, not a bard's.

Salazar spoke first and longest, and he spoke — this surprised them, I felt it surprise Godric — not as the council's familiar cold strategist, but nakedly, from the beginning. From the reeds. He told them about Mara — the whole of it, the market, the river-test, the friends who watched, the flat water, the fen-law that answered nothing with nothing. Rowena and Helga had had the story only in fragments; he had never once told it entire, in twenty-four years, and he told it now standing, in the Hall his hands had helped raise, to the three people he loved most alive. "You have wondered all these years," he said, "why I cannot simply trust the weather as you trust it. I have brought you the weather. Her name was Mara. She trusted it." And then he built, stone by stone, the case I have already given you — the preacher's routes, the threads, the burning arithmetic — and he ended with the sentence he had carried, I now believe, since January in the marshes: "I am not asking you to love them less. I am telling you that we cannot hold a door open in a wind that has learned our address. Close it — for a generation, for a century, until the weather breaks — and I will spend the rest of my life making the inside of these walls so great that history forgives us. Keep it open, and history will have nothing to forgive, because there will be nothing left to write about. Those are the stakes as I see them, and I call the vote."

And the vote went round the table, each in the old order of their founding, each binding the school forever.

Godric voted first, because he had signed first, all those years ago, on a kitchen table in Wales. He looked at his friend the whole time he spoke. "Every wall we ever raised," he said, "we raised to make the children safe. But safe *for* something, Salazar — safe to stand in daylight someday. If we survive as a locked box, we do not survive; the box does. The girl in the reed-boats" — he did not know, that night, how that image would land; he had chosen it by chance, or by whatever chooses such things; I felt Salazar go absolutely still — "the girl in the reed-boats is why there is a school. I vote the door stays open."

Rowena voted second. She spoke very quietly and did not manage her usual architecture. "I have run your weather forward, Salazar, further than you have," she said. "You are right about the next century. I believe you are right about the next three. It will get worse than even you say, and the day will come when this school hides entirely from the Muggle world, and your ghost will be entitled to stand in this Hall and say *I told you*." — She was describing, little reader, with terrible accuracy, the world you live in. — "But I have run it past that, too, and here is what I find: the hiding ends, or the hidden die. A people that seals its blood in a box spends itself in three centuries — fewer gifts, thinner lines, the old houses folding into themselves like burnt paper. The sparks in the Muggle houses are not our danger. They are our future arriving by the only road left. I vote the door stays open — not because it is kind, though it is. Because your wall, given time, kills exactly what it guards."

And Helga voted third, and she did the thing that this book, if it has taught you her at all, has taught you to expect: she stood, and walked the length of the high table, and took Salazar's face in her two hands — the gesture of Godric's mother, in the mud, a lifetime ago, though she could not have known it — and she said: "My dear. My dear friend. Mara did not die because a door was open. Mara died because she stood in that square *alone* — because there was no one, in all the world that loved her, standing where the fear could see them and say: *she is ours*. You would answer the aloneness with higher walls, and I understand, and I have understood for twenty years, and I will understand you to my grave. But walls do not keep children, Salazar. *People* keep children. Standing next to them. Where the fear can see." She let him go, gently, and went back to her chair, and said, formally, into the silence: "The door stays open. While I live it stays open, and I have planned to live a long time."

Three against one. Under the compact. Forever.

Nobody moved. The candles — his candles, her candles, the candles of twenty-four Winter Feasts — burned utterly straight in the still air. And here I must record the strangest fact of that whole night, the fact the histories refuse because it does not fit either telling: Salazar took it *well*. He sat a long moment, looking down the Hall at the four empty house tables — at his own table, green-trimmed in the shadows — and then he thanked them. Quietly, without irony, in the voice of the man who had ridden three days to say *do not arrive at dusk*. He said the compact was the compact; that they had heard him fully, which was all he had asked; that three such minds against him obliged a wise man to at least consider that he was wrong. He asked — and got — one undertaking: that his wards and watchers, the whole defensive web of his life's work, be maintained whatever came. He gathered his papers. At the door of the Hall he turned, and the last thing he said that night was to Helga, and it was this: "You said people keep children. I will hold you to the whole of that sentence, Helga. All of you. To the whole of it."

And then he was gone down the stairs, and the three sat looking at each other in the guttering light, daring, for a little while, to hope. I felt the hope in Godric's very scalp — the enormous, exhausted, brotherly hope: *he heard us; he is still ours; the fellowship holds.*

It was not false, that hope. That is the last cruelty of this chapter, and then I will let you rest before the duel. What broke the fellowship, in the end, was not the vote. Salazar was a man who could lose a vote; he had lost them for twenty years and stayed. What broke it came six weeks later, and it was small, the way the fatal things are small — a sentence reported up a corridor, a young man's boast, a name spoken where it should never have been spoken.

The name was Mara's. And the mouth it came from — I am sorry; you have perhaps grown fond of him; so had Salazar — the mouth was the Baron's.
## 21. The Duel

The Baron, that spring, was twenty-one and burning his life at both ends. Helena had refused him at last — truly refused him, after the years of keeping him like a warmth to stand near — and he had taken the refusal the one way Salazar had always said he would take any refusal: entire, like deep water moving. He did not weep or rage where the school could see. He grew instead a hard bright shell of pride, drank too much in the village with the young men of the old houses, and talked. That was all. He talked.

And on a June evening, in a Hogsmeade tavern yard, in a circle of young pure-blooded men trading the grievances of their class, the Baron — wanting, the way the wounded want, to prove himself deepest in the master's confidence — said approximately this. That the vote of May was the old fools' folly. That Master Slytherin understood, even if the school did not, what came of trusting the mud-born and their kin. That the master had lost his own sister to Muggle filth — drowned in a fen river while her precious neighbours watched — and that a man who thought *that* master would forever sit quiet under three soft hearts' outvoting was a man who knew nothing. That things were being prepared, below, for the day the soft hearts learned better.

Mara's name he did not know. The shape of her death he had — because Salazar, in seven years of patient teaching, in some late green-lit evening of trust with the student who reminded him perhaps of his own proud damaged young self, had once let the story's silhouette show. It was the only gift of his inner life he ever gave a student. The boy had kept it four years, and spent it in a tavern yard to win an argument.

By morning it was in the village. By the second evening it had climbed the lake road, as everything climbed the lake road, and reached the castle wearing the clothes such things wear after two days of retelling: *Slytherin's sister was killed by Muggles — that is why he hates them — and he is building something under the school for his revenge, the Baron says so, the Baron has it from the master himself.*

I want you to feel the mechanism of the catastrophe, because it is the same mechanism as everything else in this book, running one last time at fatal speed. Every person in the chain acted from something human. The Baron spoke from wounded love. The retellers retold because secrets about the mighty are the only wealth the small ever hold. And the three founders, when it reached them —

They believed it. Not the revenge; none of the three believed the revenge, then. They believed the disclosure. And they were right to; that was the poison; the thing was *true*. The most private grief of the most private man in Britain — the grief he had finally, freely, laid before them in the Hall in May, as the deepest act of trust of his life — was in the taverns, in the corridors, in the *dormitories*, six weeks later. And Salazar, hearing it come back to him from the mouths of children — hearing Mara's death made a story, made a *reason*, made the school's explanation of him —

Salazar concluded what the whole grammar of his life demanded he conclude. Three people had held that grief entire. He had told no one else — except one student, years ago, a silhouette only, a boy who loved him. And a locked mind does not suspect the boy who loves him; it suspects the council chamber. He concluded that one of the three — or all three, in the soft conspiratorial way of majorities — had spent his secret to the school, to explain him, to manage him, to inoculate the corridors against his politics by dressing his conviction as damage. *See, children, why poor Master Slytherin fears the Muggle-born; be gentle with his votes; his sister, you know.* He concluded that the last door he had ever opened had been walked through and the contents distributed.

He was wrong. Little reader, hold this with both hands: he was *wrong*, and it was the only time in this entire book that his pattern-reading failed him, and it failed him because the pattern-reader was inside the pattern. Fifteen chapters ago I told you what the flat river taught him: that betrayal comes from the ones who smiled. The lesson sat in him for fifty years like a bent bone, and when the one wound that could not be borne reopened, the bent bone bore the weight, and broke where it had always been going to break.

He did not investigate. That is how you know the wound was mortal; the finest investigator of his age did not investigate. He went to find Godric.

He found him in the north courtyard at first light, where Godric had gone every dawn for thirty years to work his forms alone — the courtyard where he taught the third-years to throw spells with the whole body. What the castle heard, and it heard it through every window on that face of the building, was Salazar's voice, once, at a pitch no student of his had ever heard, saying words that the hearers disagree about to this day — except for four of them, the four that mattered, which every window agrees on:

"— was it you, brother?"

And Godric, standing there in the wet grass with his wand still raised from his form, understanding in one terrible assembling instant what had happened and what was being asked and what it meant that it could be asked — Godric, who had turned back on the stair, who had chosen the man over the cellar, who had kept, for two years, out of love, a silence about the deep places that now stood in his own mouth like guilt — Godric hesitated.

Perhaps two seconds. I was on his head. It was not the hesitation of a guilty man; it was the hesitation of an innocent one who has just seen the size of the misunderstanding and the impossibility of saying anything that will fit through the closing door. But Salazar had spent his life reading hesitations, and this one he read with the bent bone, and it was the last evidence his broken pattern needed.

He struck first. The histories, even the kind ones, agree he struck first.

I will not give you the duel as spectacle, though spectacle it was — the two greatest wands of the age, in earnest, at last, in a stone yard at sunrise, while the school woke screaming to its windows; the courtyard was a year rebuilding; the scorch across the north wall was left unrepaired by Godric's order and you can put your fingers in it today, little reader, seven flagstones up, they show it to the first-years as a caution and they are right to. I will give you instead the three moments I have kept a thousand years.

The first: that Godric fought the whole duel — I felt every choice — *leaning away*. The one direction his body had never once taken. Every form he had was built on the little question answered towards, and he spent that dawn answering it away, away, shielding, turning, grounding Salazar's fury into the stone, because the only victory he wanted was a friend exhausted enough to hear four words — *it was not us* — and Salazar, who knew Godric's fighting body better than any being alive, read the leaning-away too, and it enraged him past anything, because a Godric who would not strike was a Godric who pitied, and pity, to that pride, in that hour, was the confession completed.

The second: the end. Salazar, overreaching at last into some fen-feint his exhaustion bent a half-turn wide, and Godric's wand answering with the economy of forty years — one turn, one word — and Salazar's wand describing its high glittering arc across the whole courtyard into Godric's open left hand. The oldest gesture of their friendship, the fair-ground gesture, the draw-and-supper gesture. And silence. And the two of them looking at each other across the wrecked yard, chests heaving, fifty-odd years old, with the whole school at the windows, and Godric — I felt the choice form, the maddest and most Godric choice of his life — Godric reversing the wand, walking forward through the smoke, and holding it out to its owner. Hilt first. Undefended. The little question, answered towards, one last time.

And the third moment. Salazar took the wand. Looked at it in his hand. Looked up at his friend's open unguarded face at arm's length, and I was near enough to see what I saw, and I will swear to it in whatever court the centuries convene: he almost stayed. The bent bone almost held. There was a heartbeat — one — in which the whole future forked, in which this book has a last chapter where four old friends sit by a fire.

Then he said, very quietly, in a voice with nothing left in it: "Even now you have to win." And he turned, and walked through the parting, staring crowd of children — his own house's children pressed against the front, Aldous at their head, mute — and he did not go to his tower, and he did not go to the gates.

He went down. And the school stood in the courtyard and on the stairs and at the thousand windows and listened, through the stone, to the deep doors closing, one below another below another, like a heart being locked for winter.
## 22. The Leaving

He was below for three days. The school above him held its breath.

Lessons were suspended, then grimly resumed, because Helga said children left idle with a catastrophe will build a worse one out of rumour. The rumour-mills ground anyway. The Baron was found — it had not taken the masters a day to trace the tavern-yard chain to its source — and what passed between him and the three founders in Rowena's tower study no student ever learned; but he came down from it grey as paper, and he did not speak above a murmur for a month, and something in him that had been merely proud before was, after, broken-and-proud, which is the dangerous alloy; remember it when his last chapter comes. Helena, who understood precisely what her refusal had set rolling and precisely how unfairly the weight of it fell on her, went silent in her own way, high in the tower, with her mother — the two of them, for once, wordless in the same room, joint stewards of a guilt neither had earned whole.

And Godric sat on the bottom step of the deep stairs, before the first of the closed doors, for the better part of three days and the whole of three nights. Not working the wards. Not calling through the stone, after the first hour. Just sitting, with me on his head and his elbows on his knees, the way men keep vigil at a sickbed, so that the first thing his friend met, if he came up, would not be an empty stair.

On the third dawn, Salazar came up.

The door opened without sound — his doors never dared creak — and he stepped through it dressed for the road. That was the first fact, and it reached the whole castle within minutes by the strange telegraphy of great houses: *dressed for the road*, the hooded travelling grey of the founding journeys, the same worn river-boots, a single bag. Behind him, before the door swung to, Godric saw — I saw — a corridor swept bare and lightless. Whatever the deep places held now, they held it alone, and sealed.

The two of them looked at each other on the stair. Three days is a long time; the fury was gone; what stood in its place was worse, because it was calm. And there on the stair took place the last private conversation of the greatest friendship of the age — quiet, unhurried, and final — of which I am the only living record, and which I set down here entire, because the school deserves to have it right after a thousand years of inventing it.

Godric said: "It was not us. It was never us. You know the shape of it by now — you will have traced it in the dark down there, you trace everything. It was a boy who loves you, wanting to be believed."

Salazar said: "I know." Just that. And then, after a moment, with a dreadful gentleness: "That is why I am going, Godric. Not the vote. Not even the telling. I have been three days learning this, so hear it, because it is the truest thing I will ever say to you. When I believed you had betrayed me — for a day and a night I believed it entire — I was not surprised. Fifty years my closest friend, and the oldest part of me received the news like a guest it had kept a room for. Do you understand what that means? The river did not only take my sister. It taught me a shape, and I have pressed the world into that shape for fifty years, and when at last the world would not fit, I broke *you* to make it fit. It will happen again. Next year, or in ten. Some corridor whisper, some hesitation on a stair — and the room in me will receive its guest, and I will strike first, again, and one of those dawns, brother, one of us will not lean away." He looked up the stairwell, toward the sleeping floors, the thousand small breathings. "I am the one danger in this castle that my own wards cannot answer. A keeper who cannot trust is a wall that falls inward. So the wall is removing itself."

Godric said — and his voice, the great courtyard-filling voice, was so low I felt it more than heard it: "Stay. We will build differently. I will open every door I have; I will give you my nights; we are four old people, Salazar, we have buried the age between us — who else is there to sit with?"

And Salazar smiled, the true one, the mud-and-mist one, one last time, and answered: "You cannot open a door in me, Godric. Only I can, and I have tried for fifty years, and I could not even learn the name of a child I set serpents to guard a winter past — a little Muggle-born thing in a reed-boat, whom I saved with everything I have and could not once go near, because near is the thing I do not know how to be. That is your answer, if you ever wonder in the years to come whether you failed me. You did not fail me. I was the finest lock-maker of the age. A lock-maker's masterpiece" — he touched his own chest, lightly, ironically, precisely — "locks."

Then he went up the stairs, and Godric followed, and the castle did the thing for which I love it most of everything it has ever done: it lined the way. No order was given — Helga afterward swore none, Rowena the same — but the school knew, the way houses know, and from the entrance hall to the gates, in the grey June morning, they stood: the masters; the elves, Pim foremost, holding bread he had baked in the night for the bag of a man who had never once been kind to him and never once unjust; the mill family entire, the old miller with his cap in his hands; Edith, who stepped out of the line and said something to him, very short, that neither ever repeated — I have always believed, from the shape of his face after, that it was the green-ink words: *prove me wrong* — and the students. All of them. His own house at the end, nearest the gates, Aldous at their head, and the young Slytherins did the thing that has made me proud of that house through every dark thing history has hung on it since: as their founder passed, every one of them, unrehearsed, bowed — not the classroom bob, but the full old bow of the eastern houses, the bow for the head of a family — and held it until the gates.

At the gates he stopped. He did not turn to the crowd; that was never in him. But he turned to the three who had walked at his back — to Rowena, to Helga, to Godric — and he said the last words the school ever heard from Salazar Slytherin, and they were these:

"Keep the compact. The door you voted open — keep it open, since two of you were right and I could never be the man who was wrong gracefully. And keep my house, if it will stay. Teach them the true things: that the quiet mind sees, that the long way round arrives, that loyalty is not a house's colour but its marrow. What is worst in me they will be accused of forever — let what is best in me be what you answer with." A pause. Then, only to Helga, whose face at that moment I will not describe, because some things even a hat leaves in the private dark: "You said people keep children, standing where the fear can see. You were right. It was always you that was right, of the whole four of us. I could stand in front of anything, Helga. Except behind me. Look behind you, all your days — for my sake."

And he walked out of the gates of Hogwarts, down the lake road, into the June morning mist off the water, grey into grey — and the mist took him, and the age of the four founders was over, twenty-four years, one month, and eleven days after a pig looked back over its shoulder at a woman on a Welsh doorstep.

The school watched the empty road for a long time.

Where he went, what the fens received back, what became of him in the years left to him — this book knows a little, and will tell it when the story comes round to two old men and a fire. But that morning the castle turned from the gates and faced, for the first time, the question it has been answering ever since, the question that is the whole fourth part of this book:

What do you keep, when the keeper of walls is gone and the wall he could not stop building sleeps under your floors? What do three friends do with the works, the wards, the house, the empty chair — with everything a broken brilliant man leaves behind in a school full of children?

They did, on the whole, honourably. Come and see.
## 23. After Him

Schools are like children: you find out what you have actually taught them on the day something breaks.

The morning after the gates closed, the castle woke to arithmetic nobody had ever done. One founder gone, and with him: the deep wards, or the hand that held them; the potions mastery; the subtle arts; a quarter of the teaching; the whole invisible web of watchers in the south; and — heaviest, strangest — a house. Seventy-odd children in green trim, from the proud old eastern families, whose founder had walked out of the gates while they bowed, and whose name was already being said in the other three common rooms in that particular flat tone children learn faster than any spell.

Every decision of that first year, the three remaining founders got broadly right, and I want the record to show it, because history hurries past this stretch to get to the deaths, and this stretch is where the school you attend was actually saved.

The first decision came within the week, and it was Godric's, and it was the keeping of his friend's last request in the teeth of his own house's mood. There was talk — loud talk, and not only from students — of dissolving the fourth house. Fold the green children into the other three, retire the name, let the wound close clean. Cleaner for whom, Godric asked, and answered it himself before the council: "For us. Not for them. Strip seventy children of their table because their founder broke — teach them at eleven that they are the school's embarrassment — and in thirty years we will face exactly the bitter brotherhood we fear, and we will have built it ourselves, and it will deserve its grievance." The house stayed. The name stayed. Godric went further, in the gesture the old Slytherin families still cite a thousand years later when anyone doubts them: he stood at the high table on the first night of the next term, before the whole school, and formally took the vacant office himself — Godric Gryffindor, head of Slytherin house, until a worthy master could be found — "because," he said, into the astonished Hall, "my brother asked me to keep what is best in him, and I intend to, and the first best thing in him was this: he never once in his life abandoned anything that was his to guard."

He held the office nine years. He learned the eastern bows. He learned — imagine the cost to that open moorland soul — to run a house whose whole grammar was privacy, and to run it by its own grammar, not his. The green common room tested him for a year the way boys test a stepfather, and then, in the way of such stories, buried him in devotion. Aldous, who succeeded him as the first Slytherin-raised head of Slytherin house, said at Godric's funeral — you will reach that chapter, I am sorry to say — that the red giant had been "the second-best Slytherin I ever knew, which he would call a victory, and the best of us would call a draw."

The second decision was Rowena's, and it was the wards. Salazar had left the school's defensive web intact and masterless — layer under layer of the subtlest work of the age, keyed to a mind now walking south into the mist. Rowena spent that first summer doing what she described as "learning to play another man's instrument while it is being struck" — tracing, unpicking, re-anchoring, her hair going visibly greyer by August — and she made in the doing of it the discovery this book has been waiting for her to make. Deep in the web, below the third cellar, she met work she could not unpick. Could not, in places, even fully *see* — wards woven in the blood-tongue, answering to a language no Ravenclaw would ever speak, sealed with a patience that had planned, she realised with the flat chill of the true scholar, for centuries. She stood on the same stair where Godric had stood, and met the same courteous, absolute refusal, and — being Rowena — she measured it. Mapped its edges. Calculated what it enclosed by what it excluded, the way you weigh a hidden planet by the wobble of the seen ones. And then she came up, and called the other two, and laid her survey on the table, and said the sentence that governed the next thousand years: "There is a made thing under the school. It is large, it is provisioned, it is patient, and it is his. I cannot open it. I cannot destroy it without bringing down the east wing, and possibly not then. And so we must decide — the three of us, tonight, forever — what we tell."

What they decided, you know, because you have lived inside the decision: they told the truth, and they told it as a legend. No proclamation, no assembly — a proclamation makes a fact, and a fact demands action, and no action was possible. Instead they let the dormitory stories stand — the breathing wall, the new cold under the old cold — neither confirmed nor denied; they folded into the school's own folklore, deliberately, a warning shaped as a tale: that Slytherin had built a hidden chamber, that something slept in it, that it would open only to his true heir. Rumour, they judged, would do what decree could not: keep children's ears pricked and feet clear of the deep places for as long as the school told stories at all. It was Helga who named the cost, in that midnight council, and accepted it with open eyes: "We are making his monument out of fear. The best of him will be a footnote and the sealed door will be his name. He said it himself at the gates — what is worst in him, they will be accused of forever." She was right. Walk any corridor today and say *Slytherin*, and watch what surfaces first in every face. The three chose the children's safety over the friend's memory, and they were right to, and it was still a theft, and this book is part of the long repayment.

The third decision was Helga's, and it was the smallest and the largest: she took over the finding. The southern rounds, the lists, the Muggle-born doorsteps — all of it now ran through her alone, an old woman doubling her roads in the burning weather, and she recruited to help her, in time, the person this chapter has been quietly walking toward. Edith. Muggle-born, mill-raised, castle-trained: the first of the school's own to go back down the roads and knock, herself, on doors like her own father's door. The circle closing, the door held open by the very children who had come through it — that became the school's way for centuries, and it began in those bruised years, and if the book has a single image to set against the sealed chamber, little reader, it is this one: Edith of the mill, in a healer's plain cloak, sitting in some frightened cottage kitchen, setting down her cup, and saying: "Now then. I think you have noticed something about your boy. Let me tell you what I know. There is a school."

So the school healed — lopsided, scarred along the north wall, minus one chair at the high table that no one ever sat in again by unspoken law. It healed. Ten years passed, and they were good years of the sober kind, and the three founders grew old in them, working.

And out of that decade's ordinary evenings comes the chapter I have been keeping since page one — the evening the three of them, grey and stiff and glorious, looked down the years at their own mortality and asked the question every builder must ask: *who chooses, when we are gone?* The answer was on Godric's head.

It is time, at last, to tell you how I was born.
## 24. How I Was Born

Every autumn for thirty-four years, the Sorting had been done by the founders themselves — by four of them once, then by three: the new children brought up the Hall one by one, and the founders reading them, weighing them, placing them, with all the art and all the argument you would expect. It worked because the readers were who they were. It could not outlive them, and they knew it.

The question was put, in the end, by a first-year. A small moor-country girl, at the Sorting feast of the thirty-fourth year, watching the three grey heads confer over the child ahead of her, asked her neighbour in the piercing whisper of the very young: "But who will choose us when they're dead?" The whisper carried. The Hall did the thing halls do — the laugh, the hush. Godric looked down the table at Rowena, and Rowena looked at Helga, and none of the three said anything at all, and that night the three of them climbed to the tower study where the plain woollen cloak hung, and stayed till morning, and the question sat on the table between them and would not leave.

The problem, as Rowena framed it — she framed; that had not changed — was this. The Sorting was judgement, and judgement dies with the judge. Write rules for it, and you get law without eyes: any clerk can sort by rules, and rules can be gamed, and a child is not a document. Train successors, and you get judgement again, yes — but *whose*? Each generation's masters would sort toward their own age's fashions, and in a century the houses would mean whatever the century wanted them to mean, and the founders' four-cornered answer — the balance of it, the tension of it, the *argument* of it, which was the whole engine of the school — would silt up into whatever was convenient. What the school needed, she said, was impossible, so they should begin at once: it needed the founders themselves, permanently. Judgement that would not die and would not drift. Four minds, kept.

They considered, and discarded, the obvious dooms. Portraits — a portrait keeps a manner, not a mind; ask any headmaster who has sought real counsel from a frame. Ghosts — to choose ghosthood is to choose the unfinished thing; none of the three would wish it, and you will learn in four chapters how the school got its opinion of that road. Darker keepings — there were arts, even then, for nailing a mind into the world by force; Rowena named them only to bar them, in a voice that closed the matter: everything of that kind, she said, pays for permanence with the very thing you are trying to keep.

It was Godric — and the other two never let him forget it, in the gentle merciless way of old friends — who solved the greatest enchantment problem of the age by taking off his hat.

He did it, he always insisted, as a joke to break the third midnight's deadlock: tossed me on the table among the diagrams and said, "Put it in the hat, then. It's heard every Sorting since the first. It's been on my head through every argument the four of us ever had — it knows our minds better than we do. And no dark wizard" — he was quoting a mist-morning voice across thirty years, and all three of them heard it — "will ever be able to fear a *hat* with a straight face."

Silence, of the particular kind that Rowena's stillness made. Then she picked me up, turned me in her hands, looked into me the way she looked into problems, and said slowly: "…it has, hasn't it. Thirty years on that head. Do you know what cloth does, in a field of thought, for thirty years?" And she carried me to her workbench, and the last great work of the founders' age began that night.

It took them a year. I will tell you what can be told; some of it has no words because I am the only thing it ever happened to.

The ground of the work was what Rowena had guessed: I was not empty. Thirty years riding the warmest head in Britain — through every duel, every council, every grief, every Sorting — had steeped me the way a river steeps its bed. Nothing that could think; but a shape worn by thinking, the way a path is worn: all those years of Godric's choices had run through me and left their channel. The art — her art, her masterpiece, I am allowed to say it, I am the only judge surviving — was not to pour minds into cloth. It was to *deepen the channels*: to take what each founder was, and teach the worn path of me to run that way of its own accord. Not a copy of the founders. A bed the river of them had made, that other water could now be poured down.

Each of the three gave, in their own manner, across that year of evenings in the tower. Godric gave loudest and easiest — he had been giving for thirty years without knowing; the work with him was mostly Rowena's, tuning what was already soaked in. He would sit with me on his knee and talk — to me, directly, long before I could hear him, which the others found absurd and which I count, now, as the first conversations of my life — about what he actually listened for in the children who came up the Hall. Not fearlessness, he said; he'd sorted a hundred frightened children into his house and been right every time. He listened for where the fear *pointed*. "Everyone's afraid. The ones for my table are the ones whose fear faces the thing they're afraid of. You can hear it in them, like a weathervane straining. You'll hear it too."

Rowena gave with precision and, near the end, with something that shook her voice, because the work made her articulate at last what she had never said aloud: what her house was actually for. Not cleverness, she said, dismissing her own legend with a flick; cleverness is common. Her table was for the ones who could not *help* asking — in whom the question outranked comfort, outranked belonging, outranked, sometimes, love, and who would need, therefore, a tower of their own kind or the world would sand them smooth. "You will know mine," she told me, guiding her art through the channels, "because when every other voice in them wants the answer, one voice wants the question to be bigger. Keep that voice alive, hat. It is the loneliest one, and I will not be there."

Helga came last, and differently. She sat with me alone, one evening a week, and she did not describe her house at all. She told me about children. One at a time — hundreds of them, across the year: this one who stole food in his first term because hunger had taught him no other law, and what he became; that one who came brilliant and cruel and left brilliant and kind, and what it had cost her to wait for it; the ones nobody had ever chosen first for anything, and what happens in a face — she made me learn it like a language — in the half-second after a child hears, for the first time in their life, *you belong here*. "The others have given you their standards," she said at the end of it. "Good. Standards are the bones. Here are the muscles, hat: every child who ever sits under you will be the most important person in the Hall for exactly one minute. Whatever you become, become the kind of thing that knows that."

And the fourth voice? Say it, little reader; I have taught you to ask. There could be no fourth giving; the fens keep their own counsel, and the road south had taken it beyond reach. But Rowena would not build the school's judgement three-cornered — "the balance *was* him, half the time" — and so the three did the strange loving salvage that I consider, privately, the moral summit of this whole book: they gave me Salazar out of themselves. Godric gave me thirty years of him — the fair-ground, the ambushes read in advance, the long game always seen, the voice that said *the quiet mind sees; the long way round arrives*. Rowena gave me his standards, reconstructed from two decades of losing arguments to him and revising her own designs afterward: what he watched for in a child, the banked fire, the purpose that could wait. Helga gave me the gates — the bows, the bread, the last words — and one instruction of her own, appended so quietly that I did not understand for a century that it was the keel of me: "When you find his kind — and his kind are precious, hat, they are the ones who build what the rest of us only dream — look also for the wound. His kind carry one, often. You cannot heal it; that is not your office. But *see* it. He went his whole life certain that no one saw. Let no child of his table ever leave your brim believing that."

On a night at the end of summer, in the thirty-fifth year of the school, the work was finished, and the three of them did the last thing together. What was said over me in that hour I will keep; it is the one page of this book that belongs to me and not to history. I will tell you only how it ended. It ended with the three founders of Hogwarts standing around a workbench in the high tower, old and grey and outliving their age, and Godric Gryffindor lifted me up in his two hands — the hands from the mud, the hands from the courtyard, hilt-first hands to the last — and set me not on his own head but on the block before them, and said, laughing and entirely serious, in the voice that had said *there is a school* by a thousand hearths:

"Wake up, old friend. There's work."

And I woke.

I remember it. Of course you want to know, and of course I remember; it is the first thing I remember, the way you remember nothing before your own first morning. There was warmth, which had always been; and then the warmth had a *shape*, and the shape was me; and there were three faces, enormous, ancient, waiting — I knew their names before I knew my own, because their names were the channels I was made of — and there was, everywhere, in every seam of me at once, the one thing none of the three had planned for, the thing that made Rowena sit down slowly on the workbench stool with her hand over her mouth when I began, at last, to speak.

I was not three minds, poured. I was not four, salvaged. I was one — new — and the first words ever spoken by the Sorting Hat of Hogwarts School, on the last night of summer in the thirty-fifth year, to the three tremendous grief-worn founders leaning over the bench, were these, and I have never bettered them:

"You are all wrong about the boy in the third boat. He is not one of Godric's. He is one of Helga's, and he is going to be magnificent, and somebody should tell him his mother's letter is not lost — it is under the loose board by his bed. …Why are you crying? You built me to *see*. So I see."

There was a Sorting eleven days later. They set me on a stool in front of the whole school — I have the stool still; we are colleagues — and the first child in history to be Sorted by the Hat came up the Hall with her chin out and her knees shaking, and sat, and the Hall held its breath, and I settled over her eyes, and looked, and found the weathervane straining true, and opened the seam of my brim for the first time in front of anyone, and called:

"GRYFFINDOR!"

The roar that came back off those stones, little reader — a thousand years, and I have heard it every autumn since, and it has never once grown ordinary — that roar was the sound of the founders' answer to the small girl's piercing question. *Who will choose us when they're dead?* No one, child. Death was not given a vote. They found a way to stay in the room — all four of them, the fourth smuggled in against his own exile by the three who would not build without him — and they are in the room still, every September, balanced on a stool, arguing gently inside an old brown hat about where you belong.

The three lived some years more, and the years have chapters, and the chapters darken; you know by now what kind of book this is. But that night, when the Hall had emptied and the candles were guttering and the four tables stood cleared for the year to come, Godric Gryffindor picked me up off the stool, and looked at me a long moment, and put me on — the old way, the road way — and walked the sleeping corridors with me one last hour, neither of us speaking, both of us listening to the castle breathe.

Whatever I am, I was made by four people who could not bear to stop keeping children safe merely because they were going to die.

Remember that about them. Whatever else the coming chapters take away, that stays.
## Interlude: The Green Common Room

The third borrowed voice is the hardest to borrow, because its owners do not lend.

You have now read the crack entire — the vote, the duel, the gates — and you stand where the school stood: looking at seventy children in green trim whose founder just walked into the mist. Before the last part of this book takes up the deaths of the great, I want to give one interlude to the small: to the house that stayed, in the year after the leaving, as its own people kept it. My source is the private year-book of Aldous — first Slytherin-raised head of Slytherin house — which he willed to the headmaster's office on his deathbed with a seal of fifty years, "so that it reaches an age with the wit to read it slowly." It reached me. I have had several ages to read it slowly. These are the entries that matter, with his leave — he left me his leave in the same will, thoughtful in death as in life; he was, after all, one of ours. One of *theirs*. You will see the difficulty.

*

**September, first entry.** The little ones keep asking me where he went. I am nineteen. I do not know where he went. I have taken to answering *east*, because it is true, and because a direction is more bearable than a shrug. The smallest boy — the one Master G. carried on his shoulders at the leaving, though the histories will never believe that detail so I set it down twice: carried, on his shoulders — the smallest boy asked me tonight whether the master left because of something the first-years did. There it is, whole, the work of my year. Seventy children, each privately certain the earthquake was their fault. Item: find, for each of them, some duty that proves the house still expects them tomorrow. A house that expects you tomorrow cannot have been ruined by you today. This is the only medicine I know, and I know it because he taught it to me, which is the kind of joke the year keeps making.

**October.** The other tables have begun the thing I was bracing for. Not cruelty — the masters would stop cruelty, and anyway cruelty can be fought, and fighting warms a house. This is worse: the flinch. The dropped voice when green passes. Pity from Hufflepuff, which the little ones drink like poison because it comes sweetened. And from a knot of the older red table, tonight, the first true taste: a song about serpents with no spine, sung just soft enough to claim it wasn't sung. I had the sixth-years by the fire when the story of it came down, and they wanted the answer any house wants, and I stood on the settle and gave them the ruling I had been saving since the gates, and I record it here because I intend to be held to it: "We will not answer the song. We will answer the *question* — the one the whole school is asking about us in the dark: *are they what he feared, or what he built?* Every green child in this room is now evidence. For the rest of your lives, you are evidence. It is unjust, it is unasked-for, and it is the situation. Comport yourselves as evidence." Silence, then the head girl, dry as the master himself: "He'd have said exactly that, only shorter." Laughter — the first since June. We may live.

**December.** Master G. dined at our table tonight, at the head, in the master's place, which has sat empty each meal because I could not bring myself to fill it or remove it. He asked leave first. Of *me* — the founder of the school, asking a nineteen-year-old's leave at the door of his own Hall. I understood at the second breath what he was doing, because it is what he is always doing, and the histories will get him wrong precisely here: everyone will remember the courage and no one will remember the *manners* — the enormous, deliberate, political tenderness of the man. If the head of Gryffindor sits at the serpent table asking leave, then the serpent table cannot be a shame, whatever songs are sung soft. He ate with us weekly all that winter. Ate, mark: not visited, not inspected. Complained about the fish. Learned the little ones' names, and the bows, and lost at draughts to the smallest boy in a manner I watched closely and could not prove. My house — I write it with care — my house began that winter to love him, sideways and unsaid, in our grammar. The evidence for the prosecution is thus: the man whose oldest friend our founder was; the man our founder duelled at dawn; the man who by every law of story ought to be our enemy — chose instead, before the whole watching school, to be our *guarantor*. Let the centuries do what they like with the duel. This ledger records the dinners.

**February.** A first-year asked me the question I have been dreading over the porridge, in front of everyone, in the flat voice they use when they have been up all night deciding to ask: "Is it true the master hated people like — " and then the phrase the corridors use, which I will not give even this private page. Muggle-born, she meant. She is not; her blood is as old as the lake; but her best friend at the tower table is, and the friend had heard, and had asked *her*, and she had had no answer, and friendship at eleven cannot live three days without an answer. The room went still to hear what I would say, and I said this, and I set it down because it took me all that sleepless winter to build and I will need it again: "He did not hate her. He was afraid — of the world she comes from, for reasons that were done to him before your mothers were born — and he let the fear grow until it would rather lock every door than lose one more person through any of them. That is not hate, but hear me: it did the work of hate. It would have barred your friend from her own life. The vote went against him, and the vote was right, and he left rather than live defeated, and that was his choosing, not hers, and not yours. You may love his memory and refuse his fear. That is not disloyalty, little one. That is the *house*: we keep what is best and bury what is wounded. Now finish eating; your friend is waiting for you by the stairs, and waiting friends outrank dead masters."

She went. The room breathed. An old thought, for the record: our table is the only one that will spend a thousand years answering for its founder at breakfast. The red table inherits his glory; we inherit the *accounting*. Very well. We are the house of the long game. We will pay it down the long way, child by child, and the paying — I believe this on the bad nights, which is when belief is priced — the paying will make us the deepest of the four in the end, because we alone are never permitted to forget what fear costs.

**June, last entry of the year.** Master G. summoned me at term's end and told me the mastership of the house is mine in earnest when I come of age — held meanwhile by him "as a groom holds a horse he has no intention of riding." Then he stood at the window a long while, at the lake, where the mist comes off the water of a morning, and said, not quite to me: "Keep his best things, Aldous. The rest of us are keeping his worst safe below, and his sadness at the gates, and his glory in the histories. But the best of him — the patience, the seeing, the long mercy — that has no keeper now unless it is his house." Then, at the door, the postscript that I suspect was the whole meeting: "He would have been proud of your February. I am told of these things. The porridge has ears." And was gone, before I could decide whether the founder of Gryffindor may be seen to make a nineteen-year-old cry.

The mastership came in course; the years came in course; the ledger of them is elsewhere. This year-book was only ever about the one question, so let it close on the answer as far as one lifetime reached it. *Are they what he feared, or what he built?* Fifty years of green children through my care, and my finding, entered here for whatever age unseals it: they are neither. They are what they choose, same as any house — only they choose *knowing the price of choosing wrongly*, because their own founder is the bill, posted at the door, forever. Some years that knowledge curdles one of them, and the school says *ah, Slytherin*, and forgets the nineteen it tempered. Count the nineteen, whoever reads this. He would have. It was the only arithmetic he ever trusted — the kind done in the dark, over years, where the sums are safe from applause.

*

Thus Aldous, first of the green masters, who taught his house to be evidence.

I have counted the nineteen, old friend. Every year. It comes out in your favour more Septembers than not.

Now the last part of the book, and the bill of the great: the losses, the leavings, and what remains when the builders are gone and only the building — and the hat — is left to remember. Draw up the fire, little reader. Winter keeps its schedule.

## 25. The Diadem

I must go back some years now — the way memory does, the way grief does — because while the school was healing and I was being born, the story of Helena and her mother was quietly running out of road, and I was too new, and the founders too tired, and the castle too busy, and nobody saw it until the tower stair stood empty.

Helena, by the years after Salazar's leaving, was past thirty. Understand what that meant, in her weather. The classmates of her youth were masters of their crafts now, mothers and fathers, names in their own right. And Helena remained, in the castle's eyes and worse, in her own, exactly what she had been at nine: *the founder's daughter*. She taught the little ones, and taught them beautifully — I sorted some of her students, I saw what she left in them; gentleness, mostly, and the un-Ravenclaw conviction that confusion is nothing to be ashamed of. It was not enough. It is never enough to be good at a small thing in the shadow of someone monumental at a great one, unless someone teaches you the arithmetic by which small and great are weighed truly — and the one person who could have taught her that arithmetic was the monument herself, and the monument was ageing, and grieving her lost fellowship, and speaking to her daughter, as ever, in the only tongue she had: another manuscript to review, another design to admire, another rung of a ladder Helena had never chosen.

And on its stand in the tower study, between them at every lamplit silence, sat the diadem. Thirty years finished, never given. It had stopped being silver and sapphire long since. It was, by then, the visible shape of everything unsaid in that room — the mother seeing in it *everything I am, kept for her*, waiting always for the daughter to become settled enough, whole enough, to receive it; the daughter seeing *the proof that even her love for me is an examination I have not yet passed*.

What broke the stalemate was small, as always. A visiting scholar — young, brilliant, from the schools then rising on the continent — spent a season in the tower, and paid Helena the terrible accidental compliment of not knowing who she was. For one autumn she was simply Helena: a mind, a laugh, a colleague at the high shelves. You can see the whole thing from here, little reader; there is no need for me to draw it slowly. He learned, of course. Everyone learns. And learning, he did the natural courtier's thing: turned the warmth of his attention twelve degrees, from the daughter toward the mother, the minor work toward the master — not cruelly, not even consciously, which was the worst of it, because there was no one to be angry at except the arithmetic itself.

He left at midwinter with his letters of introduction. Helena went up to the study that night, while her mother slept, and stood a long time — the portraits say; the portraits saw it all and could stop none of it — before the stand. And then she took the diadem, and a travelling cloak, and went down the tower, and out the postern, and over the frozen lake road, south, alone, into the largest country available to her: away.

I want two things understood, because eight centuries of ghost-story have flattened her into a thieving shade, and she was a person, and this book does persons. First: she did not take it to have it. She took it — she has said so, in the long silver afterwards, to the young of her house when the night is quiet and some lonely child has earned her voice — she took it because it was the one object in the world her mother could not fail to pursue. Thirty years of reaching for that woman and closing on air; very well: let the monument come to *me*, asking. It was not theft. It was a message in the only alphabet loud enough. Second: she believed, with the diadem under her cloak, that she would finally think her way to being someone — that the thing itself might do for her what being near it never had. That is the saddest sentence in this chapter, and the most human. We are all of us, at our worst hour, willing to believe the treasure is the transformation.

It did nothing, naturally. Wisdom was never in the silver; the silver was only where a mother had written it down. Helena went south through the winter ports and took ship over the narrow sea, and went far — as far as the old maps of her mother's study went, which was its own message, if anyone had been reading — and came at last to the deep forests of Albania, where the land was old and mapless and no one had ever heard of Ravenclaw, and there she stopped. And hid the diadem — in a hollow tree, in the dark heart of that forest; it matters, so mark it — and waited, in a hired room in a mountain town, for the world to arrive asking. For *her* to arrive asking.

In the tower, Rowena woke to the empty stand and the empty room, and did the thing that closed the trap: she said nothing. Pride, the histories say, and there was pride in it — the most celebrated mind of the age could not announce that her own daughter had robbed her and run. But I knew that mind; I was partly built from its standards; and I will testify that the larger part was not pride but its sadder cousin. Shame. *If it is known, she can never come home whole. Keep the door unwatched and the matter unnamed, and one morning she will simply be at breakfast, and I will say nothing then either, and it will be over.* So the school was told the founder's daughter travelled for her studies; and the one search that might have found Helena in weeks — Godric would have turned over half a continent; Edith would have charted every port — was never raised. Two women, in two countries, each waiting with her whole ruined heart for the other to ask first. Years, little reader. It went on for *years*.

It ended because death is no respecter of stalemates. In the fourth winter, Rowena fell ill — the lung-fever of that age, which came for the old when the lochs froze — and did not throw it off, and knew, with her unfailing accuracy, exactly what the failure to throw it off meant. And lying in the high room with the plain woollen cloak on its hook, the arithmetic of shame collapsed at last into the arithmetic of hours, and she called for help — and here the story reaches the door of its blackest room, because of who answered the call.

She should have sent Edith. She should have sent for Godric, who was away that season settling a wolf-trouble in the west; a word by owl and he would have come through the snow like weather. But shame chooses its confessors for their silence, not their fitness. There was in the castle that winter a man of her daughter's old year, a baron of the eastern houses now grown grey himself, who had loved Helena his whole wasted life with the fidelity of deep water; who had never married; who asked after her, alone of all the castle, every single year, and could be trusted — this was the fatal credential — to keep any secret that touched her. Rowena summoned him to the high room, and told him what no living soul had been told, and asked of him the last service:

find my daughter. Tell her — tell her only: *come home. All is nothing. Come home.*

The Baron went out of the castle that same night, south, through the frozen ports, over the narrow sea, carrying the message, and his old love, and the temper that Salazar had weighed at a glance thirty years before and named for one lifetime's flaw. Three of those things reached Albania.

You know — everyone knows; it is the one part of this story the whole school has always known — which of them was let fall.

But you do not know it as it happened, and the two who lived it are ghosts of my own castle, and over a thousand years, in the small hours, each has told me their half. The next chapter is theirs. It is the hardest one in this book, and I have put it off as long as the pages allow, and I will not put it off further.
## 26. The Baron's Rage

He found her in six weeks. Say this for the Baron, first and always: he was faithful, and he was capable, and he did in six winter weeks what no searcher had been sent to do in four years. He traced her through the ports by nothing but an old miniature and a lover's memory of how she walked. He crossed the mountains in January. He asked, in his bad market-Greek and worse Albanian, town after town, for a tall dark-haired foreign lady who kept to herself — and in a stone town under a black shoulder of forest, somebody knew her, and pointed up the street, and his heart, fifty years old and never once given elsewhere, turned over like a boy's.

Now stop, before the door opens, and see the two of them truly. This is why the chapter exists.

She, four years alone: the waiting long since gone rancid, the town's foreign seasons grinding past, the message she had staged the theft to summon never arriving — proof upon proof, winter upon winter, that the monument would not come, had never been coming, that she had spent her one great act of defiance on a silence. And he, six weeks a pilgrim: certain, in the way of his whole unbending nature, that he arrived as rescue — bearing at last the very words she had crossed a sea to hear, and beneath them, unexamined as always, the old hope he had never once been able to put down, now dressed in its most dangerous clothes. *She has been alone. She will hear the message. And then, perhaps, at last — she will see who was sent. Who always comes. Who never stopped.*

Two starving people, and each had been cast, by cruel accident, as the other's food.

He knocked. She opened — and knew him at once, and understood at once what his face meant before a word was said: that her hiding was found, that the castle had reached her — and the first feeling, she has told me, in the small hours, in the silver voice, the first feeling was joy. One entire heartbeat of it. *Asked. Finally asked.*

And then he delivered his message, and he delivered it — oh, little reader — he delivered it *wrong*. Not the words; the words he had carried like relics and gave exactly: *come home; all is nothing; come home.* But he could not carry the words without adding the fact that framed them, the fact that was, to him, the whole engine of the errand's urgency, and so he said it first — he led with it — the way you lead with the thing you would want to know:

"Your mother is dying, Helena. She sent me. Come home — all is nothing, she says — but it must be now."

Dying. Read it with Helena's four rancid winters, not with his six faithful weeks. Not: *she asks for you at last.* But: *she is running out of time, and so, at the end, she sends — not herself, never herself, she will not even die asking me herself — she sends the man who has trailed me since my girlhood; she reaches for her stolen property and her runaway daughter with the same convenient hand; even her deathbed is arranged efficiently.* Wrong, all of it — the truth in the high room was shame and love strangling each other, and *all is nothing* meant everything Helena had ever wanted meant — but wrongness needs time and calm to be unmade, and there was none of either in that doorway, and four years of rancid waiting stood up in Helena Ravenclaw and chose, as its instrument, the one cruelty lying nearest to hand.

She refused. And she refused *him* — both messages in one, the old suit and the new summons together — and she did it, she has told me, in words she chose for maximum wounding, because wounding something was the only power left in the room and he was the something: that she would not cross a courtyard for her mother's convenience, let alone a sea; and that as for him — that her mother might command her lackeys where she pleased, but Helena was not obliged to receive *deliveries*; and that he had been a boy nobody wanted and was now a grey man nobody wanted, and the fidelity he wore like an order of merit was only the inability to hear a no held for thirty years; and she was saying no, again, as to a slow student: *no*. And she turned her back on him — in the doorway, in the snow-light — to close the door on the last two people in the world who loved her.

Salazar had weighed the boy at a glance, thirty years before. *He does not know how to be refused. Everything else I can teach.* Everything else, the master had taught. The one flaw waited, whole, for its one lifetime — for that doorway, that turned back, that word *deliveries* — and the slow deep water moved all at once, entire, the way it had always been going to move.

He was armed. Men of his class travelled so. It was one motion and one moment — the two ghosts, telling their halves a thousand years apart, agree even in this — one moment of blackness with no thought in it at all, and then the thought arriving, forever too late, and Helena Ravenclaw lying in the doorway of a hired room at the edge of the map, and the snow-light not changing, and the town below going about its market morning, and the Baron standing over what his one flaw had finally done with his one love.

I sort children, little friend. It is my office to look into the young and see what is seeded there, and every year — every single year, in this castle or another, in your time as in his — I look into some proud, wounded, faithful child and see the Baron's seed: the love that has never once been taught what to do with *no*. It is why this chapter is in a book for the young, and told straight, and not softened into ghost-lore. Rage of his kind is not a storm from nowhere. It is a debt that compounds in the dark for exactly as long as no one — no parent, no master, no friend, no hat — makes the proud thing practise being refused. Practise, little reader. On the small refusals. While they are small.

What he did next, he did with terrible coherence. He did not flee and he did not lie. He composed the room; he crossed her hands; he sat beside her through the remainder of the day and the whole of the night — the town found the two of them so — and somewhere in that night he tried and condemned himself by the only code he had, which was the code of the old houses: a debt of blood is paid in kind, and a man discharges his own sentences. With the same blade, at dawn, he paid.

The code was wrong, as codes of blood are always wrong, and I will not dress it as honour; it was only despair wearing honour's coat, and it doubled the ruin: two dead in a doorway at the edge of the map, and no one alive on that whole continent who knew either name.

But death, for some, is not the end of debts — you know this; you have passed them on the stairs. They rose, both of them, as the unfinished dead rise: she bound to what she had fled and never unsaid, he chained to what he had done and could never pay. And here is the one grace in the blackest chapter of my book, the grace the ghost-stories always leave out: *they came home.* Separately, unspeaking, by the long instinct of the dead — but to the same place. Of everywhere their unfinished souls might have anchored, both crossed the sea again, and the mountains, and came up the lake road silver in the moonlight, and passed the gates — the wards knowing them, the castle letting them in, as the castle has never once refused any child of its own however they return — and took up their residences: she in her mother's tower, among her mother's house, the Grey Lady of Ravenclaw; he in the dungeons of his master's house, the Bloody Baron of Slytherin, wearing his chains — he forged them himself, in whatever smithy the dead use; no one sentenced him; he would not be without them — a thousand years and counting of self-imposed silence, save when the young of his house need frightening away from some folly, at which office, I can report, he remains supremely effective.

They do not speak to each other. In a thousand years, in the same castle, I know of eleven words between them — but those eleven belong to a later hour of this book, and I will spend them where they fell.

The diadem stayed in its hollow tree, in the dark heart of the Albanian forest, exactly where a starving young woman had hidden the shape of her mother's love. It stayed there long past every life in this chapter — until, in a century you know better than I wish you did, someone else went asking the Grey Lady kind questions in the small hours; someone who had also been a child nobody wanted; someone who collected other people's treasures and turned them into doors against death. She told him, as she had told me. She has never forgiven herself the telling. What he made of the diadem — what it cost the world to unmake — belongs to the seven books you have already read, and I leave it there.

In the high room of the tower, Rowena Ravenclaw waited out the winter for an answer from the south.

One came.
## 27. Rowena's Winter

The answer came at the end of February, on a night of hard frost, and it did not come by owl.

Rowena had held on through that winter past all her healers' arithmetic — weeks past it; the lung-fever does not negotiate, and yet it negotiated; the whole castle understood, without anyone saying the vulgar thing aloud, what she was staying for. She had her bed moved to the window that faced the lake road. She slept little and watched much, and when she was too weak to watch she had Edith sit by the glass and describe the road to her: the sledges, the woodsmen, the nothing. Every rider out of the south turned her head on the pillow. You will forgive an old hat; I was on a shelf in that room through much of it — Godric, back from the west and aged ten years by the news of her illness, had taken to carrying me up there of an evening, on the excuse that she liked company that predated her losses — and so this chapter, almost alone in the book, I do not reconstruct. I witnessed it.

She spoke of Helena only once in front of me, in the middle weeks, to Helga. Not the theft, not the silence, none of the wreckage — she was past the wreckage. She said only: "I have been trying to remember when I last made her laugh. Truly laugh — she had a laugh like dropped silver, Helga, when she was small. I have been at it three nights, and I cannot find one past her ninth year. The most celebrated memory in Britain," and here she smiled at her own hands on the coverlet, "and the one archive that matters is empty." Helga — who alone of everyone in this book never once tried to talk anyone out of a grief — did not say *you did your best*, or *she knew she was loved*, or any of the kind false coins. She sat on the bed, and took the cold hands, and said: "Then let us fill what we can reach. Tell me about the ninth year." And they were at it till dawn, the two of them, the whole ninth year — the frog in the ink-well, the first broom, the laugh like dropped silver — and I have thought many times since that of all Helga Hufflepuff's works of healing, that night's was the masterwork: she could not give the dying woman her daughter, so she gave her back, one story at a time, the daughter she'd had.

The rider from the south came on the frost night, and he was no rider; he was a traveller of our own kind, a wand-carrier out of the continental schools, come up the lake road at speed with news he would give only to the founders. Godric received him in the anteroom. I was there for that too. The man had been in the mountain towns of the far south-east that autumn. He had heard, and then confirmed with his own eyes as travellers do, the story running through those valleys: a foreign lady, dark-haired, long resident, solitary — and a grey stranger from the north who had come asking for her in six-weeks' snow — and the doorway, and the dawn, and the two graves the town had dug, side by side, foreign fashion, no names known to carve.

Godric stood a long time. Then he asked the traveller for every detail — made him walk it twice, street by street, with the terrible thoroughness of a man building a wall against hope, stone by stone, until no gap remained. And then he thanked him, and had him fed and housed, and climbed the tower stair alone, slowly, carrying the news in his chest like a blade he must now hand to a friend point-first, and I on his head, and neither of us — I had a voice by then, little reader; I want it recorded that I offered — neither of us knew a single word worth saying.

She knew before he had crossed the room. She had watched every rider on that road for a season; she had heard this one arrive and heard the house not rejoice. She looked at his face — the face that had never once managed concealment in fifty years, the worst card-player in Britain, her friend — and she said, quite calmly, the calm of the scholar receiving at last the result that falsifies the life's theory: "Both of them, then."

He could not speak. He nodded.

And Rowena Ravenclaw — who had run the weather of the world forward past everyone's horizon; who had priced the hiding of nations and the death of houses; who had known everything early and been believed late — turned her face to the window, to the black frost and the lake road empty in the starlight, and said, in her ordinary voice, the sentence I was partly built from her mind to preserve, and have preserved a thousand years, and now set down:

"I solved everything I was ever asked, Godric. Every riddle but the one asleep down the corridor. It wanted no cleverness at all, that one. It wanted only asking. Write that over my library, when you are choosing inscriptions: *she answered everything, and asked too late.*"

He did not write it, of course. He wrote what the age expected — the wit, the learning, the glory of her mind; you may read the fine words yet, cut in the stone over the library doors that were her monument and are still the heart of the castle. But he told me, on the stair down, that she had been right and the stone would be wrong, and that some day, when the school was old enough to bear it, somebody ought to set down her own version. The school is old enough. I have just done it. Debts, little reader — this whole last part of the book is the paying of them.

She died four days later, at first light, with the three of them by the bed — Godric and Helga at either hand, and Edith, at Rowena's own asking, seated at the window with instructions to describe the sunrise, "accurately, mind; I will know." Her last hour was easy, they say who know such things, and her last act was entirely, perfectly herself: she had Helga bring the day's admissions ledger — the Book of Admittance's winter listing, the names of the magical children born that season across Britain, her own great instrument still writing — and she read the new names, all of them, slowly, moving her lips a little, the way the old read; taking the roll, one last time, of the future she had spent everything to give a school to; and she set her finger under one name near the foot of the column — no one ever knew which, she did not say, I have wondered for ten centuries — and she smiled at it, at whatever she saw coming, and said: "Ah. *There's* one." And slept, and did not wake.

They buried her — this was her written wish, opened after, in the sealed testament that reorganised half the school — not in the castle crypt prepared for the founders, but in her own glen, a half-day south: in the walled garden of the old tower where a girl had once asked her mother *why* until the candles gave out. The whole school walked the road behind the bier, four hundred children in the February cold, and the centaurs — this is attested by every account, and the school wept when it understood — the centaurs of the forest came down to the tree-line, the whole nation of them, Torvus's heirs, and stood at the forest's edge in silence with their bows unstrung as she passed, the honour of their own dead, never before or since given to any wizard.

And in the high tower room, in the days after, they found the last things. The plain woollen cloak on its hook, which Helga folded and buried with her. The notebooks — sixty years of them, the greatest single hoard of magical thought the age produced; Edith spent her own remaining decades editing them, and the library still runs on the marrow of it. And on the great worktable, alone, squared to the table's edge, one document set where it could not be missed, with a cover-note in the firm old hand:

*For the three of you. Begun the night the first boats crossed; finished this Christmastide; do not grieve over the corrections — making them was the best of my last winter. She would have found the fifth error faster than I did.*

It was the school's charter — the founding compact of four signatures, which she had recopied fair, annotated, provisioned, and armoured against every future she could compute: the succession of headmasters, the guardianship of the wards, the keeping of the Sorting, the law of the open door reaffirmed in her own last hand — the whole constitution of the next thousand years, drafted as a dying gift. And at the foot of it, beneath the three living signatures it awaited, she had left the fourth line blank — labelled, in small letters, with the serpent-brother's name and the year of his leaving — and beside the blank, in the margin, the final marginal note of the most annotating mind our world has known. Five words.

*He may yet sign. Keep space.*

Keep space. She who predicted everything, keeping a blank line against every prediction she had ever made — because hope, in the end, was the one hypothesis she declined to retire.

The line is blank yet, little reader. The charter hangs in the headmaster's tower; I have faced it from my shelf for a thousand years. He never signed.

But ah — that is not quite the end of him in this book. Two chapters more, and an old man goes into the fens to find what the mist took; and you will learn, as Godric learned, what the space she kept was worth.
## 28. Two Old Men

The summer after Rowena died, Godric began to set his affairs in order, in the unhurried methodical way of a man who has attended one founder's funeral and understood the arithmetic. He signed the charter. He confirmed Aldous as head of the green house and a glen-raised master of Rowena's line as keeper of the tower. He walked the wards with Edith until she knew every anchor-stone of the defensive web by heart. And then, in September, with the new year Sorted — I remember the morning; he came to the office at dawn and stood a while looking at the blank line on the charter — he packed one bag, took no escort, told only Helga where he was going, and rode east.

He was past seventy. Helga told him he was a fool, and packed the bag properly herself, and put in remedies for a man of his age riding wet country, and stood at the gates watching until the road turned. Neither said the thing aloud. There was no need. Rowena's death had said it: whatever was ever to be said between the last two of the four, the saying had a season now, and the season was closing.

He was a winter finding the fens. Understand: the fens did not wish to be found, and the fens' first son had spent forty years improving their unwillingness. The old open trading world of the eastern islands was gone — Salazar's leaving had aged into legend out there too, and the marsh families had drawn inward around their own, and a red-haired giant asking questions in lake-country taverns met fog in every human face before he ever met it on the water. But Godric had his own coin, still good after all the years: his name. And more than the name — the manner. He did not hunt. He *visited*: mended what wanted mending, as once on a Welsh valley road; sat with the fen-folk's sick; asked nothing. And the marsh people took his measure through a season, as water-people do, and at midwinter a fen-child appeared at his lodging — appeared, the way marsh-people appear — and said only: "Grandfather says: the eel-road, at first light, and come alone, and wear the hat."

Wear the hat. Forty years, little reader, and the fens knew me by name. I confess it moved me.

A flat-bottomed boat took him at dawn, poled by silent kin through the reed-mazes, hours deep, past wards that tasted of a familiar hand grown deeper and stranger — and set him, at grey noon, on a hidden island under old alders, where a wisp of smoke rose from a low stone house on wooden legs, and on the porch of the house, wrapped against the cold, thin as a heron and white as the frost, with a grass-snake asleep in his lap for warmth, sat Salazar Slytherin, watching him come, having plainly known he was coming for a season.

And the first words between the two greatest friends of the age, after forty years of silence, were these. Salazar said: "You are fatter." Godric said: "You are dead, by every account I could buy in three countries." Salazar said: "I paid for those accounts. They were expensive. Sit down, brother, before you fall down; the boards are old and so are you."

*Brother.* Both of them heard it land. Neither touched it, the way you do not touch a healing thing. Godric sat.

They had three days. I will not give you all of it — some belongs to the two of them, and some I have promised elsewhere in these pages, and some was only two old men by a fire, remembering roads, which is the best of what such days are made of and the worst of what pages preserve. But the parts that history needs, I will set down plainly.

Godric told him of the deaths. He knew already — of course he knew; the watchers' web had never stopped running; he had known everything, always, the whole forty years, which Godric found he had expected and yet could not hear unmoved: that every year, in this house on legs at the world's edge, the school's exile had received the school's news like a man taking a pulse. But he had not known the manner of them — the doorway in Albania, the eleven-word charter note — and Godric, watching the news of Helena and the Baron go into that grey face, saw what he had ridden a winter hoping to see and dreading to see: that it *went in*. That the locks were old too. "My best student," Salazar said at last, into the fire — meaning the Baron, and meaning it as the full accounting, the way a master says it over a grave. "I weighed the flaw at eleven and I taught around it, because the rest of him was gold and teaching around flaws is faster. One lifetime, I said. I never once told *him* the price, Godric. I told the council; I made an epigram of it; the boy himself I let carry it sealed. We warded the school against everything but our own economies." He was silent a while, and the snake in his lap tasted the air and settled. "Her space on the charter. Rowena's blank line. Tell me again what the note says."

Godric told him again. *He may yet sign. Keep space.*

And Salazar Slytherin laughed — one breath of it, dry as reeds, the ghost of the mud-and-mist morning — and then put his face in his one free hand, and was still for a long time, and the fire ticked, and Godric looked at the marshes and let him be.

They argued, too, on the second day. It must be said, because the easy ending — mist, forgiveness, the quarrel dissolved — was never available to those two, and neither would have respected it. The old argument stood up between them one final time in full armour: the burning weather, which had worsened as Rowena foresaw; the open door, which the school had held as Helga swore; the wall, which the fens had perfected around this island as their son had always wanted for everyone. Godric said what forty years had given him to say. Salazar answered with forty years of his own. Neither moved the other a hand's breadth, and both had known neither would, and somewhere past midnight Salazar closed it — closed it forever, as it turned out, and he closed it like this: "We built the same castle, brother. I have had a long time to survey it from outside, which none of the rest of you were ever mad enough to try. You built it so that no child would ever again be alone with the fear. I built it so that no fear would ever again reach a child. Forty years I have looked for the seam between those two buildings, and I am telling you, as the best mind you are ever likely to hear it from: there is no seam. It is one castle. We quarrelled over which side of the same wall to stand guard on, and we let the wall come between the guards. That is the whole history of the founders of Hogwarts, fit for carving: *four sentries, one wall, and they forgot to change watches together.*"

And on the third morning, at the boat, the last things. Salazar gave Godric two objects to carry home. The first was for the school: his own working notebooks of the potions art, sixty years of them, sealed to open — the seal was examined later and this is exact — "when the one who holds my chair can read the first page without wanting my glory more than my results." (It opened, for curiosity, ninety years on, for a mild fen-born half-blood mistress of the art whom history barely remembers, which was, I have always thought, precisely the point.) The second was for no one: a small cold letter, superscribed to Helga alone, which she read once, by the kitchen fire, and burned, and never spoke of — and whatever was in it, from that season the old sadness she had carried since a June morning at the gates visibly set down its bag; so the school's readers of faces concluded it had contained the one thing he owed her, which was not apology but *accounting*, rendered at last, in full.

The medallion of his house — the serpent locket, the old green-and-silver inheritance — was not among what he sent. Godric asked. Salazar looked out over the winter marshes and said only: "It goes down my own line. There is a line. Do not look for them, brother; they are not... what I was at my best, and the fens will hold what the fens hold." The locket's long road — the daughters of the marsh, the little shop of horrors it passed through, the lake in the cave, the boy who was one of Helga's and died for it — you know from the seven books, and I will not walk it; but mark the sentence, because it is the last prophecy of this story's most clear-eyed prophet, and like all his readings, it was accurate, and like too many, it was acted on by no one, least of all himself.

At the very last, in the boat, Godric — leaning towards, one final time, because the little question had one asking left in it — said: "Come home. Sign the line. Sit at the table one evening; the elves still bake for you, Pim's grandson has the ovens; one evening, and I will pole this absurd boat myself." And Salazar stood on his island in the reeds, thin as a heron, at the centre of the fortress of his whole life's argument, and answered — gently, and finally, and with the fen-mist already walking in off the water between them:

"I am home, Godric. That was always the difference between us. You built a home and called it a wall. I built a wall and called it a home. Take care of the castle. Take care of the hat — it was the best of all of us, and it had the advantage of being nobody. And when you see my house at Sorting — the pale ones, the careful ones, the ones with the banked fire — tell them the last thing, from me. Tell them the ambition is not the wound. Tell them I confused the two, and it cost me the only four people at my funeral worth the name. Tell them to bury the wound and *keep* the fire. They will not listen. Tell them anyway. Every year. Promise."

"Every year," said Godric.

I have kept it, little reader. Fifty words into every green Sorting, near enough, for a thousand years. You may have heard them from under my brim yourself and thought them the hat's own wisdom. Now you know. Nothing under my brim is my own. I am four sentries, one wall, still changing watches together — the only place in the world they ever managed it.

The mist took the island as the boat drew off. Godric did not look away until there was nothing but grey.

Salazar Slytherin died the following autumn, in his sleep, in the house on legs, with the frost on the reeds — the fen-kin sent word to the castle by a marsh-snake, as he had charged them, which took, snakes being snakes, four months to arrive and was received by the only institution in the castle that could hear its news direct. I took the message, little reader. I woke the headmaster's tower at three in the morning of a March night shouting it, and I am not ashamed: the last of my four makers was down, and a hat may cry out once in a thousand years.

He was buried in the water, fen-fashion, in a place the marsh-people keep. There is no stone. He wanted none. His monument, though he would have sneered sideways at the sentence and then looked long out a window, is a quarter of every school of magic on this earth: the green table, the quiet minds, the long way round, the banked fire — and a blank line on a charter, kept open by a dead woman's hope, which I have faced from my shelf for ten centuries and would not now have filled for anything. Some spaces do more work empty.

Two founders left. You have grown fond of them both, I know. I am sorry, little reader. Winter keeps its schedule.
## 29. Helga's Table

Now I must tell you about the years everyone forgets, which were held together by the founder everyone underestimates, and then I must let her go too. This is the chapter I have rewritten most, in the long composing of this book inside my seams. Grief is easy to write. Gratitude is hard.

With Rowena gone and Salazar in the fens, the school of the last years rested on two old people — and then, after the marsh-snake's March message, and after the chapter I must write next, on one. But even before the last losses, the truth of the castle's daily weight had shifted long since, and everyone inside the walls knew where it rested. Godric was the school's sword and its legend and its voice at the high table. Helga was the school.

Count what she carried, in her ninth decade, and I watched her carry all of it. The finding — hers and Edith's, the southern rounds in the burning weather, the lists, the doorsteps, the standing network of watchers and fosterers that kept every Muggle-born thread safe, which she had inherited from her greatest opponent and maintained, she said, "in the argument's memory — he minded the doors so I could keep them open; well, now I do both, and I hope he is watching and I hope it *vexes* him." The healing — the hospital wing was hers to the end, and half the villages of the lake country besides. The feeding — never once delegated: ninety years old and still in the kitchens before light, the elves orbiting her like moons, the crying stool by the ovens never empty for long. The staffing, the peace-keeping, the letters to frightened parents, the training of the young masters — the whole soft tissue of the institution, everything that does not get a statue because it does not hold still long enough to be carved.

And the hunger-year. That must go in this book, because the lake country still keeps its memory and the histories of our own kind barely mention it — the histories being written, as she observed without heat, "by people who have never missed four meals in a row and cannot spell what that does to a village by February." Three years after Rowena died, the harvests failed across the north — Muggle and wizard alike; blight does not check blood — and the winter came down early and stayed murderously late, and by Candlemas the villages around the loch, Hogsmeade and the Muggle settlements of the lower valleys together, were eating seed-corn and boiling harness-leather. And Helga Hufflepuff, ninety-one years old, turned the school of witchcraft and wizardry into a granary and a soup-line, and fed the district. All of it. All winter. Both kinds.

The council of masters — this is on record, and the record survives because she made certain it survived; she wanted the argument *kept*, as a teaching — the council pointed out, respectfully, that provisioning Muggle villages meant carts on the lake road, meant questions, meant the school's stores visibly feeding folk who would wonder whence; that the risk to secrecy was real and rising; and Helga heard the whole case out, nodding, agreeing with pieces of everything as was her lifelong method of disagreeing with all of it, and then delivered the ruling that is cut, now, into the stone over the kitchen door where the elves see it every dawn, because they cut it there themselves, the year she died:

"No one starves within sight of this castle. Rule of the house. The rest is stagecraft, and I have been managing stagecraft since before your grandmothers were Sorted — the carts will run at night, the flour will arrive in dreams, the villages will wake to full bins and invent whatever saints they please. But hear the rule again, masters, and pass it down, because it is not seasonal and it is not negotiable and it did not come from kindness. It came from arithmetic, and the arithmetic is Salazar's, so attend: fear is the fire, and hunger is its bellows, and every February belly in those valleys is a door the burning weather can walk through in July. I can ward that door with a soup-pot. Cheapest warding in the annals of defensive magic. *No one starves within sight of this castle.*"

They woke to full bins. They invented, in fact, a saint — there is to this day, in the lake country's oldest Muggle parishes, a winter feast-night for a plump grey lady who comes over the water with bread, and if you think the school has ever corrected the legend, you do not yet understand the institution Helga Hufflepuff built. Stagecraft, as instructed. The carts still run at night, little reader. I will say no more than that.

She outlived Godric — I must say it here plainly, out of order, because this chapter is hers and the next belongs to him and I will not make her death a coda to a man's, not this woman's, not in my book. She outlived all three of them: the last founder, the last of the four by the fire, alone at the high table where four chairs had been, with a school of children who could not imagine the world containing such losses, because she made certain they could not imagine it — the feasts as warm, the terms as steady, the porridge in the morning-lit kitchen as inevitable as sunrise, a whole institution wrapped around four hundred children like a blanket so that history's weather stayed outside the walls where it belonged. What that steadiness cost her, in the last two years, only the elves saw, and Pim's grandson told the kitchens' telling of it to his own grandchildren, and the kitchens have handed it down a thousand years, and it is short: *the Mistress laughed all day and sat late by the ovens, and the ovens heard what the school did not.*

She died in the kitchen. Of course she died in the kitchen — in her hundred-and-first year, on a bright October morning, mid-work, mid-*sentence*; she was teaching a new elf the fever-draught, the great recipe, her mother's mother's, and she sat down on the crying stool to rest her back, which she never did, and said — the elves kept the sentence; the elves keep everything; it is written in the kitchen ledger in Pim's grandson's careful hand with the flour-smudge preserved under a preserving-charm because the smudge was hers — she said: "Mind the heat when the leaves go in, little one. Everything in this art is only patience and — "

And that is where the recipe ends, and the elves will tell you she finished it elsewhere.

The whole north walked behind her bier — I mean the whole north: the school, the village, the marsh-kin who came west uninvited and unannounced to stand at the gate, the centaurs at their tree-line a second time in a generation, and the Muggle valleys, who were told their healer of a hundred years had died, which was true, and who closed every market from the lakes to the sea on their own initiative and lined the lake road in the October cold, farm families with their caps off as the carried her by — the two worlds this book has watched fear and burn and mistrust each other for twenty-nine chapters, standing shoulder to shoulder along one road, weeping for the same woman, most of them never knowing what the others knew of her, all of them right about her. She would have called it stagecraft. It was the other thing, the thing she built instead of arguments, and there has never been a day like it since in the history of our concealment: the day the wall between the worlds stood open for one bier's passing, because both sides of the wall owed her their children.

She was buried where she asked: in the valley in Wales, in the orchard of the old farmhouse, among the stubborn little apple trees, with — her testament, in the round hand, and the executors obeyed it to the letter — "no monument but the trees, which mind the graves better than marble and pay in apples."

Her cup she left to the school — "for the Winter Feast, to go down the tables as always; mind the ceremony, not the metal" — and for some generations it did go down the tables, hand to hand, each child naming the year's good thing, until the school grew cautious with its relics, and the cup passed to the keeping of her own descendants, of whom there were by then several lines, her family having continued in the valley. And in the keeping of a distant daughter of that line, centuries on, in a century you know, the cup met the same collector of other people's treasures who had gone asking the Grey Lady kind questions — and what he made of it, and what it cost to unmake, belongs again to the seven books, and again I leave it there, save for one sentence, because on this one matter a hat will permit himself the editorial: of all the desecrations that man worked on the relics of my four, none was viler than what he did with hers — he took the vessel whose whole meaning was *passed from hand to hand, each naming the good* and made of it a hidden, hoarded, poisoned thing in the dark of a vault — and the fact that it was a daughter of Helga's own house, loyal and true, who was tortured for its loss, and a son of nobody, one of Helga's kind by every measure that matters, who helped destroy it, is the sort of long-armed justice her orchard would call, mildly, over the mixing bowl, *the arithmetic coming home.*

The kitchen rule stands, by the way. A thousand years, every headmaster, every war, every regime the castle has suffered: *no one starves within sight of this castle* has never once been repealed. It has been tested. It has never failed. It is, if you ask the elves — and you should ask the elves, little reader, they have been right about everything in this book from the second chapter on — it is the oldest continuously operating enchantment on the grounds, and it is not written in any book of wards, because it is not a spell.

It is a table. It was always a table. Four strangers sat down at it on a winter night in Wales, and everything else — the castle, the houses, the thousand years, you, me, this page — is what got built because a woman said *eat first, explain later* and meant the whole world by it.

One founder remains, and one chapter for him, and then the accounts of this book are open only to the living. Come. The moor is waiting for its boy.
## 30. The Hollow

Godric died between the fens chapter and the kitchen chapter — you understood that from Helga's page, and you understand now why I would not tell it in its calendar place: some orders are truer than time's. But it must be told, and I must be honest with you about one thing before I begin.

This is the hardest chapter in the book for me. Not the saddest — you have had the saddest; the doorway in the snow-light has no rivals. But the hardest, because a hat is supposed to be four minds balanced, and in this one chapter I cannot manage it, and I have stopped trying. I was his hat first. Before I was anyone's mind, I was his warmth. Allow an old piece of cloth his one partiality, and let us walk it through together.

He came back from the fens changed — lighter, the castle said, surprised, having braced for heavier. The forty-year stone was down; the accounts were rendered; he had heard *brother* said aloud again and said it back. He worked two more years with the old completeness — the wards walked, the green house minded, the promise kept at Sorting — and when the marsh-snake's message came on that March night and I cried it through the tower, he sat with the news till dawn, alone, and at breakfast he stood at the high table and told the school plainly: Salazar Slytherin was dead in the east; the school would mourn its founder for seven days; the green table would sit at the head of the Hall that week, and the school would learn, this once, the eastern bows. And the school mourned — confusedly, honestly, four hundred children grieving a legend they had been half-taught to fear, which was, Godric said to me that night, about right: "That is how we will all be grieved, old friend. Confusedly. By children who got the story secondhand. It's the trade we chose. They don't have to grieve us truly. They have to *outlive* us. Different business entirely."

And then, his own affairs long since ordered, he did the last thing on his list. He went home.

He was not ill. I want that recorded against the tellings that give him a grand decline: he was old — nearly eighty — and he was whole, and he simply knew, the way the great instinctive ones know things, that the season had turned and a man goes home ahead of the snow. He resigned nothing loudly; he appointed, confirmed, embraced; he stood one last Sorting — my thirtieth-something; I called a tiny fen-country girl to his own table and felt him laugh under me from across the Hall — and at the leaf-turn he rode out of the gates with one bag, and me on his head, going south-west, down the whole long spine of the island he had spent a life stitching together, toward a fold in the moors that people had learned, by then, to call by his name.

The ride took a month, because he made it take a month. It was the founding journey run backward, and he called at every hearth of the old hidden road that still stood — fewer, now; the burning weather had thinned that world, and the enclosing one Rowena foresaw was rising — and everywhere the same scene, which I rode through like a passenger through weather: the door opening, the disbelief, *Gryffindor, at our fire?* — the children fetched from bed to see him, families lining him up against the wall of memory beside their grandmothers' stories. He drank it all in and gave it all back doubled — the old splendid fair-day self, one last circuit — and only I, from the inside, felt what it was costing, and what it was: not vanity. Inventory. He was counting what had held. Hearth by hearth, family by family, child by child — the whole invisible nation the school had been built to shelter — he was walking the walls of a fortress bigger than the castle, testing every stone, and the stones held, and each held stone went into him like warmth into old cloth.

Godric's Hollow, when he reached it, was what it had become across his long absence: half its old self, half a new thing — a haven-village, one of the places where magical families had drawn together as the weather worsened, living quiet among Muggle neighbours in the old careful truce, the churchyard filling slowly with both kinds side by side. They knew he was coming; the road had outrun him for a week. And the Hollow, bless it forever, did the one thing no hall or council in fifty years had managed to do for the man: it declined to be impressed. He was Godric — the shepherd's fourth child, the boy who'd hexed the pedlar, old Bran-the-dog's master, the one who'd gone north and done well. An old woman at the well told him he'd grown fat, which was by then simply untrue; her granddaughter asked if it was true his hat talked, and he said it never stopped, and put me on her head, and I Sorted my one and only child never enrolled anywhere — I told her she was brave and kind and asked too many questions, all of which was accurate — and she wore me round the well-yard shouting she was a school. He bought back his family's old long house at the village edge. He kept sheep. I am aware, little reader, of how the sentence lands, and I would not trade it for any other in the book: the greatest wizard of the age spent his last season keeping sheep, badly, with magic, exactly as his father had, and was happy.

He lived fourteen months in the Hollow. They were good months, and full — the young of the haven-village found their way to his fire within weeks, and so the last classroom of Godric Gryffindor convened by accident on a moor doorstep: wandwork in the paddock, weathervane-listening, the old road-stories with the lessons hidden in them like coins in a pudding. He wrote letters north — to Helga weekly, joke for joke; to Aldous on the minding of banked fires; to Edith once, a single line she kept to her death: *You were the argument we won. Nothing I built argues better than you do.* And on the last morning — early summer, the year's first true warmth, the moor all larks and running cloud-shadow — he walked out before breakfast to see to a lambing ewe, and sat down against the paddock's drystone wall in the sun to rest a moment, being nearly eighty and the ewe being fine, and the moment lengthened, and the larks went on, and he did not get up.

The girl from the well-yard found him — grown taller by then, his best student of the doorstep classroom — found the old man against the warm stones with his face to the sun and his hat on his knees, and she did not scream or run. She sat down next to him in the grass, she told the village afterward, "because he always said you should stay with a thing until you understood it," and she stayed until she understood it, and then she walked back and told them: "He's finished. He looked finished. Somebody should tell the sheep."

He was buried in the Hollow churchyard, among his own people, both kinds — you may stand at the place today; wizards still go, quiet, in ones and twos, though most who visit that churchyard now go grieving a later grave, and the two griefs, I think, keep each other company. The school came south in its hundreds; the fen-kin came, second time in two years, and stood apart, and left marsh-rosemary, which does not grow within three hundred miles of the moor and was never explained. And Helga — ninety-nine that summer, who had now buried all three, the last of the four by the fire — Helga stood at the grave in the moor wind, and the school braced itself for the eulogy of the age, and she gave, instead, in the voice she used for the crying stool, the shortest founder's farewell on record, eleven words, and walked back alone down the churchyard path:

"There. Now every corner of this island holds one of us."

It was only later — on the slow ride north, in the evening, when she took me on her lap by the inn fire as she now sometimes did, we two being what remained of the original company — that she gave me the rest of the sentence, the part the grave had not needed to hear. "Four corners, hat," she said. "The glen keeps the north, and the fens keep the east, and my orchard will keep the west presently, and now the moor holds the south. He'd have seen it in a moment — he was always faster than they credited. The whole island, cornered like a bedsheet. We didn't build a school at all, in the end. We built a *country*, and lay down at its corners, and the castle is just where it keeps its children." Then she was quiet a while, and the fire ticked, and she added the last thing, the thing I have saved for the end of his chapter because it is the truest carving anyone ever cut for him, better than the fine words on the library, better than anything under my own brim:

"He was the only one of us, you know, who was never once afraid of the wrong thing."

The sword — I promised, chapters ago, to follow the sword, and here the promise falls due. It had come south with him; it hung on the wall of the long house those fourteen months, over the fire, next to the shepherd's crooks. By the terms of everything Godric had ever said, it should have passed as his goods passed: to those who came after, to guard what he guarded. By the terms of the solstice night fifty years before, Ragnuk's line held otherwise, and had never once forgotten. What is recorded is this: that in the week after the burial, riders of the goblin nation came up the moor road under a treaty-banner, courteous and correct, to claim the maker's right; and that the school's executors met them at the long house with equal courtesy and the wizard law; and that the two delegations went in together to where the sword hung —

— and the wall was bare.

No breach in the wards, which were Rowena's own design and logged every fly. No mark, no lack, no trace: between the burial and the claiming, the sword of Godric Gryffindor removed itself from the argument. The goblins, to their lasting credit and lasting fury, concluded the wizards had cheated; the wizards concluded the goblins had; the quarrel is a thousand years old this generation and has salted three treaties, and both parties, I can now report, were wrong. For the sword has been seen since — you know it has; you have read the seven books; it has a habit of arriving — and it arrives always the same way: out of nowhere, into some hand that is about to do a brave and necessary and probably fatal thing, and never once into a hand merely entitled to it. Somewhere between the moor and the claiming, the goblin-silver made its choice, or was given one — I have my suspicions; goblin-silver takes in what makes it stronger, and it had hung fifty years over the fire of a man whose whole art was answering *towards* — and it settled the ownership question in the only court left: it belongs to the need. Ragnuk's heirs will dispute this footnote. Let them. They forged better than their law, and their law was better than ours, and the sword, I notice, has never yet consented to stay with either people, which is the most eloquent legal opinion in our history.

And where does it live, little reader, between its arrivals? You know that too. It sleeps where he left me. In the office, in the tower, in the dark inside the Sorting Hat — the two of us, keeping each other, his warmth and his answer, a thousand years and counting.

He carried me thirty years. I have carried him ever since. I call it a fair trade, and I close his chapter the way he closed every road we ever rode, looking back once, briefly, and then not again:

He was my first head, my best argument, and my favourite. I told you I could not manage the balance. I am the Sorting Hat of Hogwarts School, four minds and one question, and my favourite was Godric, and I never Sort a frightened child on a September night without borrowing his voice for the first word.

Two chapters remain. What waits below, and what I have learned. The living have the rest.
## 31. The Chamber Waits

Helga's bier went down the lake road in October. In November, the masters met to choose the school's first head who was not a founder, and they did the wise dull correct thing that institutions do when the age of giants closes: they chose the best administrator among them, and the school entered history — the long, grey, durable stretch of it, where this book must soon leave you.

But before the living take the story, I owe you a walk through the castle as the founders left it — an inventory of what remained, and what still remains, because you live among these things, little reader, and most of you have never once been told what you are living among.

Walk with me. It is night, as it should be; the castle tells the truth at night.

Start in the Great Hall. The enchanted ceiling is Rowena's, and she kept her one secret — how it is done is in none of her sixty notebooks; Edith searched for thirty years; the masters of every age have probed it and produced only monographs of increasingly dignified confusion. It repairs itself. It has outlived every war the castle suffered. On the night she died, four hundred children under it at supper watched it do the thing it has done on one night only, ever: the whole sky of it, cloudless and clear, snowed. Slow, sparse, silver — from dusk to midnight — and no master then living had an explanation, and I did, and kept it, and give it to you now: it was not mourning. It was a signature, releasing. Some works hold a corner of their maker back until the maker needs it no longer. She was in the ceiling all along, a little. Mind that, next time you eat beneath her weather.

The moving stairs are hers too, and the wandering doors — the sword never fully drawn, the fortress that reshuffles for a siege that has, three times in a thousand years, actually come; ask the stones of the seventh floor, which remember the last one. The library is her monument and her apology, and the fine words over its doors are Godric's kindness, and the truer inscription you now know, because I have finally, after ten centuries, obeyed her deathbed instruction and written it down in a book. Debts, again. This whole volume is the paying.

The kitchens are Helga's, and the rule over the kitchen door, and the elves — the oldest unbroken community in the castle, older in continuous service than the ghosts, with their ledger and their preserved flour-smudge and their governance of their own which no headmaster has ever been foolish enough to disturb. The hospital wing runs on her recipes. The Winter Feast runs on her ceremony. The greenhouses stand on her garden-lines, and the Hufflepuff cellars smell of apples in October by an enchantment nobody cast, which the elves say needs no explaining: "the Mistress's orchard writes to her house." The friendship of the village is hers. The truce of the valleys is hers. The saint's night with the bread is hers, and the carts still run.

The wards are Salazar's — still, at bottom, after a thousand years of patching and layering by every generation of masters, the deep grammar of the castle's defence is one man's fen-bred vigilance, and the patchers will tell you, with professional unease, that the oldest layers are the best work in the web and that they do not fully understand them and that the web *knows* things it was never taught. The gates that weigh intentions are his. The castle's habit of being unfindable by ill-wishers is his. Half the school's survivals in the bad centuries trace to defences laid down by the founder whose name the survivors were taught to shudder at — he would have found that arithmetic entirely satisfactory, and said so in one dry sentence, and gone back to work.

And the deep places are his. We arrive, at last, where the book has always been going.

The Chamber of Secrets. I have called it by its name only once before this page; the school called it nothing but rumour for nine hundred years; you, little reader, in your fortunate century, know more of it than any headmaster of the first nine ever did — you know it as fact, as history, as the seven books' second volume: the heir who came, the guardian woken, the girl who died, the girl who nearly died, the boy with the sword. My sword. His sword. You know how the long charge ended.

So let me tell you only what this book alone can tell: what it was like to live above it. A thousand years, and I will compress them to their honest residue. It was like living in a beloved house with one locked room, long after everyone who held keys is dead. For generations the rumour did the work the three founders designed it to do — kept feet clear and ears pricked — and then, as designed things will, it drifted: the warning shaded into treasure-tale, the treasure-tale into house pride, the house pride into the poison the worst of every green generation whispered to the next — *our founder left us a weapon* — until the room below had become, in the school's dreaming, whatever each dreamer needed it to be. Terror to some. Inheritance to others. And to me — say it now, hat, the book is nearly out of pages — to me it was the question I could not retire. I, who was built from three-quarters of the man and a loving salvage of the fourth; I, who carried Godric's memory of the courteous barrier on the stair and Helga's instruction to *see the wound*; I sat on my shelf through ten centuries of Sorting the pale careful children of his table, and always, beneath the year's work, the old question turned like water under ice: *what did he leave down there — the wall he promised, or the wound he denied?*

I have my answer now. It cost the school a girl's life, a basilisk's centuries of poisoned patience, and very nearly the whole heart of the story you grew up on — but I have it, and since no one else has ever thought to total the ledger from the hat's side of the office, I will total it here, as the last accounting of this book.

Fifty years ago, little reader — fifty, out of a thousand — the heir of the fens came at last, and opened the door with the blood-tongue, and the long charge Salazar laid failed in the one way its maker never guarded against. He had provisioned against the burning weather, against the breaking of gates, against every enemy *outside* the walls his grammar could imagine. He did not provision against the enemy being the heir himself — being a boy of his own line and his own wound, unloved, unwatched, brilliant, translating pain into power by the same broken grammar, who found a guardian charged to be *the children's last wall* and re-aimed it, as fear re-aims everything, at the children. Love, translated into the grammar of fear, compiles into monsters. I gave you the sentence on the stairs going down, eighteen chapters ago. It waited half the book, as the chamber waited half the millennium, for its proof.

And yet. Total the whole ledger. The night the proof came due at last — the second opening, the year the guardian rose in earnest — what met it in the deep places, over the body of one of Helga's kind, was: a boy answering *towards* with everything Godric ever soaked into my cloth; a phoenix, whose kind nest only where the heart of a house is sound; the sword, arriving as it arrives, out of the need; and me. The four of us, in the dark, against the salvaged wound of the fourth founder. And the wall held. Not his wall — *ours*, the one the four of them actually built, the one made of table and tower and courtyard and kitchen, of a school that could still grow a twelve-year-old capable of bleeding for somebody else's child. The chamber was the strongest thing Salazar ever made in stone, and it proved weaker than what he helped make in children, and I will go on saying so every September for the next thousand years, because it is the whole moral of the founders' age and it fits in a breath: *what you build in the dark is inherited by your wounds; what you build at the table is inherited by everyone.*

The chamber stands empty now. Scoured, mapped at last — Edith would be insufferable with vindication — a dead monument on the school's deepest inventory. The masters debated sealing it with finality, filling it, unmaking it. They have not, and I argued against, and I will tell you my reason and let it close the inventory of the castle. We keep the library and the kitchens to remember what the founders were at their best. Something must be kept to remember the other thing — that the best of us, wounded and unwatched, can spend twenty years building love into a shape that waits a thousand years to hurt exactly whom it meant to save. Institutions that raze their warnings repeat them. Let the door below stand, locked and known, on the school's honest map. A school is a place of instruction, little reader.

Some rooms instruct empty.

So ends the inventory, and with it, very nearly, the book. One chapter remains — mine by right, for I have earned it: a thousand years of Septembers, four minds, one question, and what a hat finally believes about where a child belongs. Come up to the office one last time. The fire is lit, the sword is sleeping, the blank line on the charter is keeping its space.

Let us finish.
## 32. Four Minds, One Question

So here we are, little reader. You and I, and the fire low, and the castle asleep on its rock, and the book nearly weighed out. You have walked the whole road now: the moor, the fens, the glen, the valley; the fair-ground and the flat river; the pig and the building years; the boats, the tables, the feasts; the crack, the duel, the gates; the diadem, the doorway, the reeds, the orchard, the paddock wall in the sun. Four lives, and what they built, and what it cost, and what it became. There remains only the question you have been carrying since the first chapter — I told you to hold it, and if you have read honestly, it has grown heavy: *Which one am I?*

Let me tell you, at last, what a thousand years of Septembers have taught me about that question. It will not take long. The deepest things I know are all short.

Here is the first thing. Every child who has ever sat on the stool contains all four.

All four, little reader. Not a mix, not mostly-one — all four, entire, the way a chord contains its notes. I have looked inside perhaps fifty thousand heads, which is more than any being alive or dead has looked inside, and I have never once — not once, in a thousand years — found a child with a note missing. The frightened small boy who begs me silently for the brave table *is already brave*; you cannot beg from the bottom of your fear without courage, and I tell him so. The sharp-eyed girl who wants the clever tower is also, visibly, two inches under her cleverness, someone who would hide a hunted stranger in the cellar and feed him. The proud cold child of the old families, arranging his face for the green table, has a loyalty in him so deep and so unspent it frightens me for him. And the kind ones, the quiet kitchen-hearted ones whom the other tables' partisans patronise — I have seen further into their ambitions than they have. There is steel in the soup, little reader. There always was; you have met Helga now; you know.

So the Sorting is a lie? I hear the young cynics of every generation try that one. No. The Sorting is a *question of emphasis*, which is a different thing from a question of contents. Four notes in every chord — but a chord must be voiced; something must carry the melody; and a child of eleven, standing at the door of everything, does not need a catalogue of all they contain. They need a table. A place to stand while they grow the rest. The houses were never meant to be four kinds of person — the founders themselves would have failed that Sorting; I contain them; I would have had to cut each of the four in quarters. The houses are four *starting points*: four doors into the same castle, four first languages for speaking to the world, with the whole work of a life being — attend, this is the second thing — to become fluent in the other three.

That is what the founders themselves were doing, all those years at one table. Four fluencies, teaching each other. Godric learning caution from the fens, patience from the tower, gentleness from the kitchen. Rowena learning from Godric that a truth undared is unfinished, from Helga that a mind without a table starves. Salazar — while he could bear it — learning from all three that a wall may have a door and stand. The school worked because the four of them held their notes *in one chord*, and the school broke on the day the chord broke, and every dark chapter you have read in this book, every single one, traces back to a fluency refused: a man who would not learn the language of trust, a woman who would not learn the language of asking, a boy who was never taught the language of *no*. The houses are not the castle's glory, little reader. The *bridges* between them are. The houses are just where the bridges start.

And the third thing. The thing I have never said in a thousand years of Septembers, and am saying now, in a book, because books outlast even hats, and someone in some far generation must find it written when the fashion turns against it, as fashions do.

I take the child's choice.

Not always — do not stretch it; when a child's asking is only fear dressed up, or vanity shopping for a costume, I overrule, and I am right to; seeing that difference is precisely what four minds were poured into me *for*. But when a child sits under my brim with all four notes sounding, and somewhere beneath the noise there is a wanting with roots — *let me be with my brother; let me prove I am not what my family is; let me start again where nobody knows me; let me be where the hard books are* — I listen to the wanting over the weighing. Every time. The masters have questioned it for centuries; the partisans of pure aptitude call it my flaw; and I have answered them, all this time, with silence, because my answer is the whole of this book and it would not fit in a Sorting morning. It fits here.

I take the choice because I watched aptitude fail. I was *built* from the greatest aptitudes of an age — the bravest, the cleverest, the kindest, the most cunning, measured by any instrument you like — and I watched those four aptitudes, unchosen, un-renewed, curdle or crack or wall themselves in exactly where they were strongest. Godric's courage nearly cost him his friend on a stair, leaning away for love; it was his *choosing* — the hilt held out across the wrecked courtyard — that made the courage worth a house. Rowena's wisdom starved her daughter; it was too late, and only barely, that she chose to ask — and even so her last act was a chosen hope, a blank line kept against her own arithmetic. Helga looks like pure nature, I know — the born heart, the effortless table. Do not believe it. I sat on her lap; I have her from the inside; every warm thing in that woman was a decision, renewed daily, in full sight of every reason to stop — the ridge-boy alone would have soured a lesser will forever. And Salazar. Salazar is the proof written in fire. No child that ever sat under me had richer gifts. He was *sorted by his wound* — by the flat river, at fifteen, into a house of one — and he never afterward believed the assignment could be appealed. That was the whole tragedy, little reader, the entire engine of the crack and the chamber and the thousand-year stain on a quarter of my children: not his nature. His certainty that nature was sentence. He told Godric in the reeds: *only I can open a door in me, and I could not.* He was wrong that he could not. He proved it wrong himself — in the marshes, in January, with the snakes and the reed-boat girl: a chosen kindness, against his whole grain, at full strength. He could open the door. He had simply never, not once since the river, believed he was *allowed*.

So I sit on my stool every September, with the sword asleep inside me and four dead friends awake in me, and when a child's wanting has roots, I say the word the child is reaching for. Because the deepest thing I know — bought with every page you have just read — is this: *we become what we choose to practise, and a school is a building where children are given seven years and four examples to practise becoming.* The Sorting is not my verdict on what you are. It is the school's first gift of what every child deserves and Salazar Slytherin never got: a say.

That is the whole of my wisdom, little reader. A thousand years, and it fits in a pocket. All four notes are in you. The houses are doors, not verdicts. Fluency in the other three is the actual curriculum. And when the wanting with roots rises in you — at eleven, or at forty, or at the far end of everything — it is allowed. It was always allowed. Take the choice.

The fire is nearly out, and the castle stirs early; the elves are at the bread already, four floors down, under the rule that has never been repealed. Let me put you out the door the way I put out each year's last nervous first-year, with the same words, which you will now understand from underneath, which is the only real understanding there is.

They ask me — every year, some small voice under my brim asks me — *which house is the best one?*

And I answer: the best house of Hogwarts is the one they never built, because they were already living in it. It had a moor-man's courage at the door, and a fen-man's watchfulness at the windows, and a glen-woman's lamp at the desk, and a valley-woman's fire on the hearth, and one long table, and room at the table, always, for whoever came up the road. They only ever made one mistake in that house, and they knew it better than anyone: they forgot, for a while, to keep every chair filled.

So that is your Sorting, little reader, the true one, the one that outranks mine. Not *which of the four are you* — you are all of them; I have looked into fifty thousand heads and I am telling you, you are all of them. But: *whose empty chair will you fill? Which language will you learn next? Who is standing alone in the fear's doorway within sight of your castle — and what, knowing everything this book has told you, will you choose to practise?*

The gates are open. They were built to be.

Go on, now. It's nearly morning, and there is a school.

## Epilogue: September

One more page, little reader, and then I let you go. Not a chapter — chapters are for the past, and this page is for tomorrow morning.

Tomorrow morning is the first of September.

I can feel it coming the way old cloth feels weather. Down in the kitchens the elves have been at the feast since yesterday; the smell climbs four floors by suppertime, and Pim's many-times-great-grandchild will send up the first trial loaf to the headmaster's office tonight, as is custom, "for inspection," and the headmaster will inspect it thoroughly, as is also custom, and return crumbs. The ghosts held their parliament at the dark of the moon and set the watches; the Grey Lady has the east walk, as she has had it these nine hundred years; the Baron has the dungeons and the little ones' stair, because the smallest children sleep better their first week, though none of them could tell you why, knowing something old and sleepless is minding the corridor. The stairs have been practising their moving all August — I hear them at night, shuffling like a theatre company before opening — and the Great Hall's ceiling has been rehearsing sunsets. The castle, in short, is doing what the castle has done for a thousand years.

It is expecting.

And somewhere south of here, tonight, are the children. All over the island, in bedrooms and farmhouses and city flats, with trunks packed and unpacked and packed again. The old-family ones, who have heard the stories all their lives and are afraid the stories will not want them. And the other ones — the sparks, the reed-boat ones, the ones from houses where the letter landed like a bird from another world, who have spent all August learning that magic is real and are afraid, tonight, of a hundred things, but mostly of the same thing the old-family ones fear, because it is the only fear that matters at eleven: *what if there is no place for me?*

Little friend — you who have read this whole long book, in a language not your own, which took its own kind of bravery, chapter by chapter, and I noticed — you know now what none of those children on their last night at home yet knows. You know exactly what is waiting for them.

Four people are waiting for them. Dead a thousand years and waiting still, every September, in the only ways that outlast stone. A moor-man who swore on a dark lake that no child of ours would ever be alone again — he is waiting in the courage of every teacher who stands between a student and harm. A fen-man who wanted every child counted, warded, and taught — he is waiting in the gates that weigh intentions, and in the deep old wards that have never once slept, and in the promise I keep for him, fifty words, every green Sorting. A glen-woman who wanted no bright child to die untaught — she is waiting in the library, and in the Book of Admittance that wrote each of their names the hour they were born, so that not one spark on this whole island goes unfound. And a valley-woman who knew, before any of them, that the whole thing — castle, wards, houses, centuries — was only ever a long table with room kept at it. She is waiting in the feast.

And I am waiting on my stool, with a sword asleep inside me and four old friends awake, and tomorrow night the doors will open, and the boats will come over the black water with the small faces turned up toward the lit windows, and the line will come up the Hall — knees shaking, chins out, all four notes sounding in every one of them — and the whole ancient machinery of this story, everything you have read, every grief and argument and golden year of it, will gather itself down to one point, as it has a thousand times, as it will a thousand more: an old hat, a young head, and a minute in which that child is the most important person in the world.

Then I will open the seam of my brim, and say the word, and a table will stand up roaring to claim them.

That is the end of the book, little reader. It is the only ending this story has ever had, and the founders' great secret, which you are now entitled to, is that it is not an ending at all. The story of the four is a circle: it ends where Helga started it, at a table, with a place laid, waiting for whoever comes up the road.

Somewhere out there is your letter. Whatever world you wake in tomorrow, whatever your kettle of a heart is learning to hold — the school is expecting. It was built expecting *you*.

Mind the stairs. They move.

THE END
## Как читать эту книгу

Если вы дочитали до этой страницы — поздравляем: вы только что прочли роман на английском. Настоящий, длинный, взрослый роман. Это стоит отметить.

Несколько советов на второй круг, если он будет. Перечитывать всю книгу не обязательно — вернитесь к главам, которые зацепили: «How I Was Born», «The Duel», «Two Old Men». При втором чтении вы заметите, сколько деталей было заложено заранее: реплика Салазара на солнцестоянии, письмо Хельги, торвусова оговорка про вопросы. Книга строилась как замок — с фундаментом под каждой башней.

Слова, которые встречались десятки раз — brave, cunning, wise, loyal, trust, fear, secret, choice, wall, door, table — уже ваши. Проверьте себя: попробуйте пересказать по-английски любимую сцену вслух, в две-три минуты. Это лучший экзамен по этой книге.

## Мир основателей: краткая справка

Действие происходит примерно за тысячу лет до событий о Гарри Поттере — в Британии X века. Канон об этой эпохе говорит очень мало, и почти всё, что известно, — легенды: четыре волшебника основали школу; название пришло из сна Ровены про бородавчатую свинью (warty hog → Hogwarts); Годрик и Салазар были лучшими друзьями; Салазар требовал учить только детей из волшебных семей, проиграл спор и покинул школу, тайно оставив под замком Тайную комнату с василиском — для своего наследника; Распределяющая шляпа принадлежала Годрику, и основатели вложили в неё свой разум; меч Годрика сделал король гоблинов Рагнук Первый, и гоблины считают его своим; диадема Ровены была украдена её дочерью Хеленой, которую убил в Албании влюблённый Барон — оба стали привидениями школы (Серая Дама и Кровавый Барон); чаша Хельги ушла её потомкам. Всё остальное в этой книге — характеры, сцены, Мара, Эдит, деревня в камышах, последняя встреча в топях — художественный вымысел, построенный так, чтобы не противоречить канону.

Рассказчик — Распределяющая шляпа — тоже вымышленный ход: в каноне она поёт песни и знает историю основателей изнутри, так что свидетель из неё честный.
