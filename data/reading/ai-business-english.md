## 1. What Is Artificial Intelligence, Really?

Everyone talks about artificial intelligence, but very few people can explain it in simple words. Let’s fix that.

Artificial intelligence, or AI, is software that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence: understanding language, recognizing images, making predictions, and even writing code. The key difference between AI and traditional software is simple. Traditional software follows rules that a programmer wrote by hand. AI learns patterns from data.

Imagine you want a program that can tell cats from dogs in photos. With traditional software, you would have to describe a cat with rules: “it has pointy ears, whiskers, a small nose…” This approach breaks very quickly, because the real world is messy. With AI, you take a different path. You collect thousands of labeled photos — this one is a cat, this one is a dog — and you train a model on this data. The model finds the patterns by itself. Nobody writes the rules; the rules emerge from the examples.

This is called **machine learning**, and it is the engine behind almost everything we call AI today.

### Models, Training, and Data

Three words appear again and again in every conversation about AI: model, training, and data.

A **model** is the “brain” of the system — a mathematical structure with millions or billions of adjustable parameters. **Training** is the process of showing the model huge amounts of data and adjusting those parameters until the model’s predictions become accurate. **Data** is the raw material: text, images, numbers, clicks, transactions. There is a popular saying in the industry: “garbage in, garbage out.” If you train a model on bad data, you will get a bad model, no matter how clever your algorithm is.

The most famous type of AI today is the **large language model**, or LLM. Models like this are trained on enormous amounts of text from the internet, books, and code. As a result, they can write emails, summarize documents, answer questions, translate languages, and hold a conversation. When you type a request into a chatbot, you are writing a **prompt** — an instruction for the model. The model then **generates** a response, word by word, predicting what should come next.

### Why Now?

AI research is more than seventy years old. So why did the boom happen only recently? Three things came together.

First, **computing power** became cheap and available. Training a modern model requires thousands of powerful chips working in parallel, and cloud providers now rent this power to anyone with a credit card. Second, **data** exploded. The internet produced more text, images, and video than humanity had created in all previous history. Third, researchers found better **architectures** — better ways to structure the models themselves.

The result is a technology that improves at a speed nobody expected. Tasks that seemed impossible five years ago — writing working software from a plain-English description, for example — are now routine. This is why investors, founders, and large companies are all racing to figure out what AI means for their business.

### Hype vs. Reality

Every powerful technology attracts hype, and AI attracts more than most. It is important to keep a cool head. AI models are impressive, but they have real limitations. They sometimes **hallucinate** — produce confident answers that are simply wrong. They can be biased, because they learn from human data, and human data contains human prejudice. And they do not “understand” the world the way people do; they are extremely good at predicting patterns.

For a business person, the practical conclusion is this: treat AI as a powerful tool, not as magic. It can dramatically speed up work, cut costs, and open new opportunities — but it needs supervision, clear instructions, and good judgment. The people who win with AI are not the ones who believe the hype. They are the ones who test, measure, and adopt what actually works.

## 2. How Businesses Use AI Today

Forget science fiction. The most valuable uses of AI in business are surprisingly boring — and that is exactly why they make money.

When people imagine AI in business, they often picture robots or self-driving cars. In reality, the biggest wins happen in everyday operations: answering customer emails, sorting documents, writing product descriptions, analyzing sales numbers. AI does not replace the business; it removes friction from a thousand small tasks. And when you remove friction at scale, the savings become serious money.

### Customer Support

Customer support is usually the first place where a company adopts AI, because the economics are so clear. A support team answers the same twenty questions all day long: “Where is my order?”, “How do I reset my password?”, “Can I get a refund?” An AI assistant can handle these routine requests instantly, twenty-four hours a day, in any language. The human agents then focus on the difficult cases — angry customers, unusual problems, situations that need empathy and judgment.

The results are easy to measure. Companies typically report faster **response times**, higher **customer satisfaction**, and lower **cost per ticket**. Nobody loses their job to a chatbot that answers password questions; instead, the team stops drowning in repetitive work.

### Content and Marketing

Marketing teams produce enormous amounts of text: ads, landing pages, email campaigns, social media posts, product descriptions. AI has changed the economics of this work completely. A task that took a copywriter two hours — writing ten versions of an ad headline — now takes ten minutes: the AI drafts, the human selects and polishes.

Notice the pattern: **the human stays in the loop**. Good marketers do not publish raw AI output. They use AI for the first draft, for brainstorming, for translation and adaptation — and they keep the final decision for themselves. The skill of the future is not writing from a blank page; it is editing, curating, and knowing what “good” looks like.

### Sales and Back Office

In sales, AI reads through thousands of leads and predicts which ones are most likely to buy, so the sales team spends its time on the right people. It writes personalized outreach emails, prepares meeting summaries, and updates the CRM automatically — the task every salesperson on Earth hates doing by hand.

In the back office, AI processes invoices, checks contracts for risky clauses, matches payments to orders, and flags unusual transactions. These tasks are invisible to customers, but they eat thousands of working hours. Automating them does not sound exciting at a conference, but it shows up directly in the company’s **bottom line**.

### Small Business, Big Leverage

Here is the most interesting shift: AI gives small companies capabilities that only large corporations could afford before. A two-person startup can now have customer support in five languages, a content pipeline that produces daily videos, and data analysis that used to require a full-time analyst. One founder with good AI tools can do the work of a small team.

This is what people mean by **leverage** — getting more output from the same input. Money is leverage. Employees are leverage. Software is leverage. And AI is the cheapest leverage in the history of business, available to anyone who takes the time to learn the tools.

The companies that struggle with AI usually make the same mistake: they look for one giant “AI transformation” project. The companies that succeed do the opposite. They find one painful, repetitive process, automate it, measure the result, and then move to the next one. Small wins, stacked week after week, turn into a serious competitive advantage.

## 3. Business Processes: Find the Bottleneck

Before you automate anything, you need to see your business as a system. This chapter gives you the language for that.

A **business process** is a repeatable sequence of steps that turns an input into an output. A customer order comes in — that is the input. Somebody checks payment, packs the product, prints the label, ships the box, sends a tracking number — those are the steps. A happy customer with a package — that is the output. Every company, from a coffee shop to a bank, is a collection of such processes, whether the owner sees them or not.

The difference between a chaotic business and a scalable one is simple: in a scalable business, the processes are **documented**. They exist on paper (or in Notion), not only in the founder’s head. If a process lives only in your head, you cannot delegate it, you cannot improve it, and you certainly cannot automate it.

### The Bottleneck

Here is the most useful idea in all of operations: every process has a **bottleneck** — one step that is slower than all the others and limits the speed of the whole system. Think of traffic on a highway that narrows from three lanes to one. It does not matter how wide the road is before or after; the narrow section defines how many cars get through.

Business works the same way. Imagine your video production: scripts are written quickly, filming is fast, but editing takes three days per video. Editing is your bottleneck. If you hire a second scriptwriter, nothing improves — you just create a bigger queue in front of the editor. The only change that matters is the one that widens the bottleneck: a faster editor, better tools, or simpler videos.

This leads to a rule that saves enormous amounts of money: **optimize the bottleneck first, and ignore everything else.** Improvements anywhere else in the process are an illusion of progress.

### Map, Measure, Improve

Serious operators improve processes in three moves.

First, **map the process**. Write down every step, who does it, and how long it takes. Most founders are shocked the first time they do this honestly — the process always has more steps than they thought, and half of the steps exist “because we’ve always done it this way.”

Second, **measure it**. How long does the whole cycle take from start to finish? This is the **turnaround time**. Where does the work sit and wait? What percentage of orders go through without a problem, and how many need rework? You cannot improve what you do not measure — this phrase is a cliché precisely because it is true.

Third, **improve it** — in a strict order. Eliminate steps that add no value. Simplify what remains. Standardize the steps so they are done the same way every time. And only then **automate**. This order matters enormously. If you automate a bad process, you do not get a good process; you get bad results delivered faster. Automation multiplies whatever you already have — including the chaos.

### Where AI Fits In

AI is the newest and most flexible automation tool, and it changes what can be automated. Classic automation handled only structured, predictable work: if an invoice arrives, copy the amount into the spreadsheet. AI handles messy, human work: read this email and decide whether it is a complaint, a question, or spam; summarize this call; draft a reply in a polite tone.

A useful way to think about it: give AI the tasks that follow a pattern but are annoying to describe with strict rules. Sorting, summarizing, drafting, translating, extracting information from documents — this is AI territory. Final decisions, relationships, taste, and responsibility remain with people.

Start small. Pick one process, map it, find the bottleneck, and ask a simple question: “Which of these steps is repetitive enough that a smart assistant could do it?” Automate that one step, measure the result for two weeks, and then repeat. This boring loop — map, measure, improve, automate — is how ordinary companies quietly become extraordinary ones.

## 4. Startup Language: Founders, MVPs, and Product-Market Fit

Startups have their own dialect of English. Learn it once, and every pitch deck, podcast, and tech blog becomes easy to read.

A **startup** is not just a new company. The word means something more specific: a young company searching for a business model that can grow very fast. A new barbershop is a small business. A company building software that could serve a million barbershops is a startup. The person who starts it is a **founder**; if there are two or three of them, they are **co-founders**.

The startup world runs on a simple, brutal logic: most startups die. They do not die because the founders are lazy or the technology is weak. They die for one dominant reason — they build something that **nobody wants**. This single fact explains almost all startup vocabulary, because the entire method is designed to avoid this one death.

### The MVP

The central tool of the method is the **MVP — minimum viable product**. It is the smallest, simplest version of your product that real users can actually use. Not a presentation, not a design mockup — a working thing, however ugly.

Why build small? Because your ideas about what customers want are guesses, and the MVP is the cheapest way to test a guess. Founders say: “launch fast and iterate.” **To iterate** means to improve in small, quick cycles: release a version, watch how people use it, learn something, release the next version. Each cycle is an **iteration**. The opposite approach — spending a year building the “perfect” product in secret — has a name too: founders call it a death march, because you learn nothing about real customers until it is too late.

Together with the MVP comes the habit of talking to users. **User feedback** — what customers say and, more importantly, what they do — is treated as the most valuable substance in the building. A complaint is a gift. A user who leaves silently is a disaster, because you never learn why.

### Product-Market Fit

The holy grail of the early startup is **product-market fit**, often shortened to PMF. It is the moment when the market starts pulling the product out of your hands: users come back without reminders, they tell their friends, growth stops feeling like pushing a rock uphill. Before PMF, nothing else matters — not the logo, not the office, not the press. After PMF, the game changes completely: now the challenge is to **scale**, to grow the team, the infrastructure, and the marketing without breaking what works.

What if the product is not working? Then the founders face the hardest decision in the startup world: to **pivot**. A pivot is a sharp change of direction — new audience, new product, sometimes a completely new idea — while keeping the company and the lessons learned. Some of the biggest companies in tech started as something else entirely: a podcast platform became Twitter, an online game became Slack. A pivot is not failure; staying too long with a dead idea is.

### The AI Startup Wave

Today we are living through the largest startup wave in history, and it is powered by AI. The reason is economic: AI collapses the cost of building. A product that needed ten engineers and a year of work can now be built by two people in a month. Founders even have a term for building software mostly by describing it to AI in plain language — **vibe coding**.

This creates both an opportunity and a threat. The opportunity: the barrier to entry has never been lower, and a solo founder in Bandung competes on equal terms with a team in San Francisco. The threat: if it is easy for you, it is easy for everyone, so the product itself is rarely the **moat** — the defensible advantage that protects a business from competitors. Distribution, brand, speed, community, and unique data — these are the new moats. The startups that win the AI wave will not be the ones with the cleverest technology. They will be the ones who reach customers first and learn from them fastest.

## 5. Building a Product: From Idea to Roadmap

“Product” is the most overloaded word in tech. This chapter unpacks it — and the everyday language of the people who build products.

In tech English, a **product** is not just “the thing we sell.” It is the whole experience a user gets: the software, the design, the onboarding, the support, even the emails. The person responsible for deciding *what* to build is the **product manager** (PM). The people who decide *how* to build it are engineers and designers. In an early startup, the founder plays all these roles at once — which is why founders must speak product language fluently.

### Features, Releases, and the Roadmap

A product is made of **features** — individual capabilities. Search is a feature. Dark mode is a feature. Export to PDF is a feature. Teams **ship** features — this is the universal verb for releasing something to users, and it carries a certain pride: real teams ship. A collection of changes released together is a **release** or a **version**.

The plan of what will be built and roughly when is the **roadmap**. A good roadmap is not a list of promises with dates; it is a list of priorities that answers one painful question: what do we build next, and — much harder — what do we say **no** to? Every serious product person will tell you that product management is mostly the art of saying no. Users ask for hundreds of things. Competitors ship shiny features. The team has ideas. If you say yes to everything, you get a bloated product that does nothing well.

To choose, teams look at **use cases** — real situations in which a user needs the product. “As a busy founder, I want to record a voice note and get a clean text transcript, so that I don’t have to type.” Writing use cases in this form forces you to think about the user’s goal, not your technology.

### Talking About Quality

Some vocabulary you will meet in every product discussion. A **bug** is an error in the software; teams fix bugs. A **crash** is when the app stops working completely. **UX (user experience)** is how the product feels to use; **UI (user interface)** is what it looks like. When a product is easy to understand without a manual, it is **intuitive**. When users must struggle, they experience friction — and modern users have zero patience: they will simply leave and try a competitor.

Two more terms carry a lot of weight. **Onboarding** is the user’s first experience — the first minutes in which they must reach the “aha moment,” the point where they feel the product’s value. Products live or die in onboarding. And **retention** is whether users come back — day after day, week after week. Retention is the single most honest signal of product quality. Downloads can be bought with ads; retention cannot be bought at all.

### Building with AI Inside

Today, teams face a new question: where does AI belong inside the product? The temptation is to add a chatbot to everything and call it innovation. The better path is quieter: use AI where it removes a real step for the user. Instead of a “smart assistant” button, the product simply drafts the reply, sorts the inbox, fills the form, suggests the next action. The best AI features do not look like AI features at all — they look like the product got easier.

There is also a new speed of building. With AI coding tools, a prototype that once took a month now takes a weekend. This changes the strategy: when building is cheap, the scarce resource is knowing *what* to build. Prototype three versions, put them in front of five users, watch what happens, keep the winner. The teams that internalize this loop — build, test, learn, repeat — move at a pace that older companies cannot match. Speed of learning has become the real competitive edge.

## 6. Marketing in the Age of AI: Funnels, Hooks, and Content

Marketing English is the most useful dialect of business English — because it is the language of attention, and attention is the scarcest resource on Earth.

Modern marketing starts from one honest admission: nobody is waiting for your content. Every person you want to reach is drowning in messages. Therefore, the first job of marketing is not to inform — it is to earn attention. Everything else comes after.

### The Funnel

The master metaphor of marketing is the **funnel**. Imagine a wide cone: many people enter at the top, few come out at the bottom as paying customers. The classic stages: **awareness** (they learn you exist), **interest** (they want to know more), **consideration** (they compare you with alternatives), **conversion** (they buy), and **retention** (they stay and buy again).

At every stage, some people leave — they **drop off**. The percentage that moves from one stage to the next is the **conversion rate**, and it is the central number of all marketing. A thousand people watch your video; fifty click the link; ten leave an email; one buys. Each of those transitions is a conversion rate, and each can be measured and improved. When a marketer says “the funnel is leaking,” they mean too many people drop off at some stage — and the job is to find where and why.

The moment you want a viewer to act, you give them a **call to action**, or **CTA**: “comment the word AI,” “download the free guide,” “book a call.” A weak CTA is the most common leak in creator funnels: the content is great, but the viewer is never told what to do next.

### Content Is the New Advertising

The old world bought attention with ads. The new world earns it with **content**: short videos, posts, newsletters, podcasts. The logic is simple — content builds **trust** before you sell anything, and people buy from those they trust.

Creator marketing has its own micro-vocabulary. The **hook** is the first one to three seconds of a video — the sentence or image that stops the scroll. If the hook fails, nothing else exists, because the viewer is already gone. **Engagement** is everything the audience does with your content: likes, comments, shares, saves. Platforms reward engagement with **reach** — the number of people who see the content. **Organic** reach is free, earned by the algorithm; **paid** reach is bought with an advertising budget. A piece of content that spreads explosively **goes viral**.

Behind the scenes, marketers live in a world of testing. An **A/B test** shows two versions — two hooks, two headlines, two thumbnails — to different audiences and measures which performs better. The discipline here matters more than creativity: strong marketing teams test constantly, keep what wins, and kill what loses without emotion. “In God we trust; all others must bring data,” as the saying goes.

### What AI Changes

AI changes marketing in three ways.

First, **volume**. One person with AI tools can now produce what a small agency produced five years ago: daily scripts, translated captions in three languages, dozens of hook variations, personalized emails for every segment. The cost of content production has collapsed.

Second, **personalization**. Instead of one message for everyone, AI can adapt the message to each audience segment — different pains, different examples, different language — at a scale no human team could manage.

Third — and this is the trap — **sameness**. When everyone uses the same tools with lazy prompts, everything starts to sound the same, and the audience feels it instantly. The winners use AI for speed and volume, but keep the taste, the stories, and the personality human. Your experience, your face, your specific numbers and failures — this is content no model can generate, and it is exactly what earns trust. AI multiplies your voice; it cannot replace having one.

## 7. Sales: Pipelines, Deals, and Churn

Marketing earns attention; sales turns attention into revenue. The vocabulary here is small, concrete, and used in every B2B conversation on the planet.

Sales starts with a **lead** — a person or company that might buy. Not every lead is equal. A **cold lead** has never heard of you; a **warm lead** has shown interest — watched your content, downloaded your guide, asked a question. The process of finding leads is **lead generation** (“lead gen” in speech), and the process of checking whether a lead is serious — do they have the need, the money, the authority to decide? — is **qualification**. Salespeople qualify leads so they do not waste weeks on someone who was never going to buy.

### The Pipeline

Where marketing has a funnel, sales has a **pipeline** — the set of all potential deals, organized by stage. A typical pipeline: new lead → contacted → demo booked → proposal sent → negotiation → **closed-won** (they bought) or **closed-lost** (they did not). Each potential sale in the pipeline is a **deal** or an **opportunity**.

The pipeline lives in a **CRM** — customer relationship management software — where every call, email, and promise is recorded. Sales managers ask the same questions every Monday: How many deals are in the pipeline? What is the total pipeline **value**? Which deals are stuck, and why? What is our **close rate** — the percentage of deals we win? A healthy pipeline is the difference between predictable revenue and a monthly panic.

Two verbs dominate sales speech. You **follow up** — write or call again after a first contact, politely and persistently, because most deals are won on the fourth or fifth touch, not the first. And you **close** — bring the deal to a signed contract. “Always be closing” is the old sales cliché; the modern version is gentler: always be helping, and the closing follows.

### Keeping Customers: Churn and Retention

Winning a customer is expensive; losing one is quiet. **Churn** is the rate at which customers leave you. If you start the month with 100 subscribers and lose 5, your monthly churn is 5%. Churn is the silent killer of subscription businesses: with 5% monthly churn, you lose almost half your customers in a year, and your marketing must run at full speed just to stay in place.

The opposite force is **retention**, and the economics are dramatic: keeping an existing customer is usually five to ten times cheaper than acquiring a new one. This is why mature companies invest in **customer success** — a function whose entire job is making sure customers achieve their goals with the product, renew their contracts, and ideally expand them. When an existing customer buys more, that is an **upsell** (a bigger plan) or a **cross-sell** (an additional product). The cheapest growth in any business is hiding inside the customers you already have.

### AI in the Sales Team

AI has quietly become the hardest-working member of many sales teams. It researches a prospect before the call — the company, the news, the person’s background — in seconds instead of an hour. It drafts personalized outreach that does not smell like a template. It joins the call, takes notes, writes the summary, and updates the CRM — the administrative work that salespeople famously avoid. It listens to hundreds of recorded calls and finds patterns: which phrases correlate with closed deals, which objections kill them.

An **objection**, by the way, is the customer’s reason not to buy — “too expensive,” “not now,” “we already use a competitor.” Good salespeople do not fear objections; they prepare answers for each one in advance. AI is excellent at this preparation: feed it your ten most common objections, and it will help you draft, test, and sharpen responses.

What AI cannot do is the core of the craft: build trust, read the room, and take responsibility for a promise. The best sales teams use AI to eliminate everything around the conversation — so the human can be fully present inside it.

## 8. Data and Metrics: Speaking in Numbers

In modern business, opinions are cheap and numbers are respected. This chapter teaches you to speak in numbers — the shortest path to sounding professional in English.

Every serious business conversation eventually arrives at the same question: “What do the numbers say?” The general word for business numbers is **metrics** — anything you measure regularly: visitors, sales, churn, watch time. Among all your metrics, a few are so important that the team watches them weekly; these are **KPIs — key performance indicators**. A content business might track views as a metric, but its KPIs are subscribers and revenue, because views without subscribers are just noise.

Metrics live on a **dashboard** — one screen where the key numbers are always visible. A good dashboard answers in ten seconds: are we growing or not, and where is the problem?

### The Language of Change

Numbers move, and English has precise vocabulary for describing the movement. Metrics **increase** or **grow**; more dramatically, they **jump**, **surge**, or **skyrocket**. In the other direction, they **decrease**, **decline**, or **drop**; dramatically, they **plunge** or **crash**. When nothing changes, numbers **stay flat** or **plateau**.

The most important habit: always speak in percentages and comparisons, not bare feelings. Not “views got better” but “views grew 40% month-over-month.” **Month-over-month (MoM)** compares this month to last month; **year-over-year (YoY)** compares to the same month last year — a fairer comparison for seasonal businesses. A **trend** is the direction over time; a **spike** is a sharp one-time jump; an **outlier** is a strange data point that does not fit the pattern and usually has a story behind it.

Two more essentials. An **average** is the sum divided by the count — useful, but easily distorted by outliers. And the most abused word in analytics: **correlation**. When two metrics move together, they correlate — but correlation does not mean one causes the other. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths correlate, because both rise in summer. Confusing correlation with causation is the classic analytical mistake, and pointing it out politely — “that might be correlation, not causation” — is a power move in any meeting.

### From Data to Decisions

Data matters only if it changes decisions. The professional phrase is **data-driven** — making choices based on evidence rather than intuition alone. In practice, being data-driven is a loop: form a **hypothesis** (“I believe shorter hooks will raise watch time”), design a test, collect the data, and read the result honestly — including when it proves you wrong. Especially when it proves you wrong; that is the moment you actually learn something.

Beware of two traps. The first is **vanity metrics** — numbers that look impressive but change nothing: total registered users (how many are active?), total views (how many became customers?), followers (how many buy?). The second trap is drowning: measuring everything and understanding nothing. Strong operators pick three to five numbers that truly reflect the health of the business and ignore the rest until those move.

### AI as Your Analyst

Until recently, working with data required an analyst who spoke SQL. AI has demolished this barrier. You can now upload a spreadsheet and ask in plain English: “Which content topics drove the most subscribers last quarter?” “Compare weekend and weekday sales.” “Find the outliers and explain them.” The model writes the code, runs the analysis, draws the chart, and explains the result in human language.

This does not make analysts useless — it makes *you* dangerous. The bottleneck was never the calculation; it was asking the right question. A founder who knows their business deeply, armed with an AI analyst, will out-think a data team that does not know what to look for. The skill to develop is not statistics; it is curiosity with discipline: ask, check, ask again, and never trust a number you cannot explain.

## 9. Money: Revenue, Margins, and Runway

Financial English frightens people unnecessarily. In reality, day-to-day startup finance runs on about twenty words — and here they are.

Start with the top of the story. **Revenue** is all the money that comes in from customers — also called **sales** or, informally, the **top line**, because it sits at the top of the financial statement. Revenue is not profit. From revenue you subtract **costs** (also **expenses**): salaries, software, advertising, rent. What remains is **profit** — the **bottom line**. A company can have huge revenue and zero profit; a small business with fat profit can be healthier than a famous startup burning millions.

Between revenue and profit lives the **margin** — the percentage of revenue you keep. If a course sells for $100 and delivering it costs $20, the margin is 80%. This is why software and information products are such beloved business models: their margins are enormous compared with physical goods, where materials, shipping, and warehouses eat the difference.

### Subscription Math

Modern tech runs on subscriptions, and subscriptions have their own two-letter dialect. **MRR — monthly recurring revenue** — is the predictable money that arrives every month from subscribers; **ARR** is the same annually. Investors love recurring revenue because it is predictable: one-time sales must be re-earned every month from zero, while MRR compounds.

Around MRR orbit three linked numbers. **CAC — customer acquisition cost** — is what you spend to win one customer: total marketing and sales spend divided by new customers. **LTV — lifetime value** — is the total profit one customer brings over their whole life with you. The ratio between them is the health check of the entire business model: LTV should be at least three times CAC. If you spend $50 to acquire a customer who brings $300 over two years, the machine works — feed it more money. If CAC exceeds LTV, every new customer makes you poorer, and growth is just accelerated dying.

### Burn and Runway

Two startup words come straight from aviation. **Burn rate** is how much cash the company loses per month — a startup that spends $30k and earns $10k burns $20k monthly. **Runway** is how long the cash lasts at that speed: $100k in the bank with a $20k burn means five months of runway. Every founder knows their runway the way a pilot knows their fuel, because when the runway ends and the plane has not taken off, the story is over.

To extend the runway, startups **raise** money from **investors** — a **funding round**. Rounds have names by stage: **pre-seed** and **seed** for the earliest bets, then **Series A, B, C** as the company grows. In exchange, investors receive **equity** — a share of ownership — at an agreed **valuation** (what the whole company is deemed worth). Raising at a $10 million valuation and selling 10% means $1 million in the bank. The alternative path has its own proud name: **bootstrapping** — growing purely on your own revenue, keeping all equity, and answering to no one. Neither path is “correct”; venture capital buys speed with dilution and pressure, bootstrapping buys freedom with slowness.

### Reading the Money with AI

Financial analysis used to require an accountant’s time and a spreadsheet wizard’s skills. Now you can hand AI a year of transactions and ask the questions that matter: Where does the money actually go? Which expense grew fastest? Which product line has the best margin? What happens to runway if revenue drops 30%? The model builds the forecast, models the scenarios, and — most valuably — explains anomalies you would never have noticed.

The discipline remains yours: look at the numbers every single week, not once a quarter when it hurts. Companies rarely die from one big mistake. They die from small negative numbers, ignored for months, quietly compounding. Money, like English, rewards those who practice a little every day.

## 10. People and Teams: Hiring, Delegating, Managing

A business scales exactly as fast as its founder learns to work through other people. Here is the English for that transition.

The moment a company grows beyond one person, a new set of verbs enters the founder’s life. You **hire** people (and sometimes, painfully, **fire** them). Before hiring, you write a **job description** — what the role does and what skills it requires. Interested people become **candidates** or **applicants**; you **interview** them, check their **references**, and make an **offer**. The whole pipeline — from posting the role to a signed contract — is called **recruiting** or, in bigger companies, talent acquisition.

Two English distinctions confuse Russian speakers. First: an **employee** works for you long-term on a salary, while a **contractor** or **freelancer** works project by project — legally and financially very different animals. Second: **salary** is the fixed annual or monthly pay; **compensation** is the total package — salary plus bonuses, plus sometimes equity. In startups, equity matters: early employees often accept a lower salary in exchange for **stock options** — the right to buy shares cheaply later, a lottery ticket on the company’s success.

### The First Ninety Days

Hiring is only half the job; the other half is **onboarding** — the process of turning a stranger into a productive team member. Weak onboarding is brutally expensive: the new person spends months guessing, makes avoidable mistakes, and often quits — and then you pay for recruiting all over again. Strong onboarding is boring and effective: documented processes, a clear first project, an assigned person to ask questions, and honest **feedback** early and often.

Feedback deserves its own paragraph, because English business culture treats it differently. Feedback is expected to be regular, specific, and two-directional — not a yearly verdict, but a constant, low-drama exchange. Two phrases carry the culture: “Here’s what’s working” and “Here’s what I’d like you to change.” Praise in public, correct in private. And the golden rule of delegation: delegate the **outcome**, not the steps. “I need the video published by Friday, quality like our last three” beats a list of forty micro-instructions — because the first version creates an owner, and the second creates an executor who waits for instructions forever.

### Remote, Async, and the Modern Team

Modern teams are often **remote** — working from different cities and time zones — which forces a skill that offices never taught: **asynchronous communication** (“async”). Instead of endless meetings, work moves through written updates, recorded videos, and clear documents. The core async habit is to over-communicate in writing: decisions, context, and next steps live in text that anyone can read at any hour. Teams that master async enjoy a hidden superpower — deep, uninterrupted work — while meeting-driven companies burn their days in synchronization.

The vocabulary of team health matters too. **Productivity** is output per time. **Accountability** is owning your results — the opposite of excuses. **Alignment** means everyone understands and agrees on the direction; misaligned teams row hard in different directions and call it effort. And **burnout** — exhaustion from prolonged overwork — is not a badge of honor but a management failure with a long recovery time.

### The AI-Augmented Team

AI is quietly reshaping what “a team” means. The practical rule emerging everywhere: hire people for judgment, taste, and relationships — and give every one of them AI tools for everything else. A marketer with AI drafts campaigns at triple speed; a developer with AI ships what used to need three developers; an assistant with AI handles the research load of a small department.

This changes hiring itself. The most valuable skill in a candidate is no longer “knows the tool” — tools change monthly — but learns fast and delegates well to machines. Founders now openly ask in interviews: “Show me how you use AI in your work.” A blank stare is a red flag; a confident demo is worth more than a diploma.

One caution as teams shrink in size and grow in power: the smaller the team, the more each person’s judgment matters, and the more expensive each hiring mistake becomes. Hire slowly, fire fast, as the hard old saying goes — and treat your small team’s culture as the asset it is, because with five people, one toxic hire is not 20% of the team; it is 100% of the atmosphere.

## 11. Risks, Ethics, and the Fine Print

Every technology powerful enough to help is powerful enough to harm. Talking about AI risks intelligently — in English — is now a basic professional skill.

The AI conversation is polarized between two loud camps: “AI will solve everything” and “AI will destroy everything.” Serious business people occupy the quiet middle: enormous opportunity, real risks, and a duty to understand both. Let’s build the vocabulary of the middle.

### What Can Go Wrong

Start with the model itself. AI systems **hallucinate** — state falsehoods with total confidence. In a poem this is charming; in a legal document or a medical answer it is dangerous. The professional response is not to abandon the tools but to build **verification** into the process: AI drafts, a human checks, and nothing important ships unverified. The rule is simple — the more expensive the mistake, the more human review it needs.

Second, **bias**. Models learn from human data, and human data carries human prejudice. A hiring model trained on a company’s past decisions can quietly learn to prefer the same kind of people the company always hired — automating discrimination while looking objective. The word for the antidote is **fairness**, and the practice is testing: checking how the system performs across different groups before trusting it with decisions about people.

Third, **privacy**. Every prompt you type may contain something sensitive: customer data, financials, unreleased plans. The questions every business must answer: What data are we sending to which provider? Is it used for training? Where is it stored? Terms like **personal data**, **consent** (permission to use someone’s data), and **data protection** stop being lawyer-speak and become operational questions. Rough rule: never paste into an AI tool anything you would not paste into a public forum — unless you have read and trusted the provider’s data policy.

Fourth, **security**. AI creates new attack surfaces. **Deepfakes** — convincing fake video or audio — have already been used to impersonate CEOs and authorize fraudulent transfers. **Phishing** emails, once easy to spot by their broken English, are now written fluently by the same models you use. The defense is process, not paranoia: verify unusual requests through a second channel, especially anything involving money.

### Rules of the Game

Governments are responding, and the vocabulary of **regulation** now appears in business plans. **Compliance** means following the applicable rules; a company that ignores them faces fines and bans. The regulatory map is a patchwork — different regions take different approaches, from strict regimes with heavy documentation requirements to lighter-touch frameworks — and it shifts every year. For a founder, the practical takeaway is not to memorize laws but to know the questions: Where are my users? Whose data do I process? Which rules apply to my industry? **Transparency** — being open about when and how you use AI — is increasingly both a legal requirement and, more interestingly, a trust advantage: companies that disclose their AI use honestly are simply easier to believe.

### Responsibility as Strategy

Here is the reframe that separates mature operators from tourists: **responsible AI** is not a tax on innovation — it is a moat. Trust takes years to build and one incident to destroy. A leaked dataset, a discriminatory algorithm, a deepfake scandal traced to your tools — any of these can erase a brand overnight. Meanwhile, the boring virtues — verification, privacy discipline, honest disclosure — compound into a reputation that competitors cannot copy with any amount of compute.

The practical checklist fits on a sticky note. Keep humans reviewing high-stakes output. Know where your data goes. Test for bias when decisions touch people. Verify money-related requests twice. Disclose AI use where it matters. None of this is difficult; all of it is discipline — and discipline, as everywhere in business, is the real differentiator.

## 12. The Future of Work: Adapt, Upskill, Compound

Nobody knows exactly what AI will change. But history gives us a pattern, and the pattern gives us a strategy.

Every technological wave triggers the same conversation. The printing press would destroy memory. Machines would end employment. Computers would eliminate office work. Each time, some jobs disappeared, more jobs appeared, and — the part everyone forgets — nearly every surviving job transformed. The farmers did not all become programmers; farming itself became a different profession. The realistic expectation for AI is the same, but faster: less “robots take all jobs,” more “every job quietly becomes a different job.”

The word for this pattern is **disruption** — when a new technology breaks the existing order of an industry. Disruption sounds destructive, and for the unprepared it is. But look closer at any disruption and you find a transfer: value moves from those who resist the change to those who ride it. Travel agents who fought the internet vanished; those who became specialized travel designers thrive. The question is never “will the wave come?” — it is “which side of the transfer will you be on?”

### Automation vs. Augmentation

Two words define the two futures of any given task. **Automation** means the machine does the task instead of the human. **Augmentation** means the machine amplifies the human — one person, more powerful. The near future of most knowledge work looks like augmentation: the marketer commanding a content engine, the developer directing a fleet of coding agents, the analyst asking questions no dashboard anticipated.

This reshapes the skill market with brutal clarity. Skills that AI performs cheaply — routine writing, standard code, basic analysis, translation — fall in market value even as they remain useful. Skills that direct AI rise: **judgment** (knowing what good looks like), **taste** (knowing what people want before they do), **domain expertise** (knowing an industry so deeply that you spot what the model gets wrong), and distribution (owning an audience’s attention and trust). Notice something about that list: these are exactly the skills that compound with experience. The future punishes the generic and pays the specific.

### The Learning Strategy

The professional response has a name: **upskilling** — continuously adding new skills — and its cousin **reskilling**, learning a new profession entirely. The old model of education — study once, work forever — is dead; the new model is **lifelong learning**, and it is not a slogan but a weekly calendar entry.

What should a business person actually learn? Three layers. First, **AI literacy**: using the tools daily until they feel like a hand, not a gadget — prompting well, knowing failure modes, building small automations. Second, fundamentals that do not expire: clear writing, clear thinking, negotiation, finance, and — yes — English, the language in which the frontier publishes first. Third, your compounding edge: the intersection of skills that makes you rare. AI plus your industry. Content plus your market. Each skill alone is a commodity; the intersection is a moat.

And here the theme of this whole book closes its loop. You are reading business English about AI — stacking two compounding skills in one motion. That is not studying; that is strategy.

### A Closing Word

The honest summary of everything in these twelve chapters fits in four sentences. AI is the cheapest leverage in business history, and it rewards those who adopt early, test constantly, and keep humans responsible for judgment. Processes beat heroics; metrics beat opinions; retention beats acquisition; trust beats everything. The language of this game is English, and its vocabulary is smaller than it looks — you now own the core of it. Read daily, speak aloud, use three new words in real work every week — and in a year, the person who “could communicate, but…” will be gone, replaced by someone who negotiates, pitches, and closes in fluent business English.

Good luck — although, as founders like to say, luck is just preparation meeting opportunity.
